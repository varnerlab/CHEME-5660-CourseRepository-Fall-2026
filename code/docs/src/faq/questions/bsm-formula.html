What does each term in the BSM call formula mean?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

The BSM call premium is the discounted expected payoff under the risk-neutral pricing rule. One term values the share received at exercise, the other values the strike paid, and N of d-minus is the risk-neutral probability that the call finishes in the money.

2 Worked explanation

What does the formula compute? The Black–Scholes–Merton (BSM) formula gives the same premium that the Monte Carlo simulation estimates in L9a: the discounted expected payoff under ℚ\mathbb{Q} of a European call on a share with no dividends. When the call is exercised, the holder receives a share worth S(T)S(T) and pays KK. Let 𝟙{S(T)>K}\mathbb{1}\{S(T)>K\} equal 1 when the call finishes in the money and 0 otherwise. Splitting the payoff into the two cash flows gives:

𝒫c=𝒟T,0−1(gy)𝔼ℚ[S(T)𝟙{S(T)>K}]⏟S(0)N(d+): value today of the share received−K𝒟T,0−1(gy)ℚ(S(T)>K)⏟K𝒟T,0−1(gy)N(d−): value today of the strike paid. \mathcal{P}_{c}=\underbrace{\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{T,0}(g_{y})\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[S(T)\,\mathbb{1}\{S(T)>K\}\right]}_{S(0)\,N(d_{+})\text{: value today of the share received}}-\underbrace{K\,\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{T,0}(g_{y})\,\mathbb{Q}\bigl(S(T)>K\bigr)}_{K\,\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{T,0}(g_{y})\,N(d_{-})\text{: value today of the strike paid}}.

Where does N(d−)N(d_{-}) come from? Under ℚ\mathbb{Q}, geometric Brownian motion with drift gyg_{y} gives lnS(T)=lnS(0)+(gy−σ22)T+σTZ\ln S(T)=\ln S(0)+\bigl(g_{y}-\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\bigr)T+\sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z, where ZZ is a standard normal random variable. The call finishes in the money when:

S(T)>K⇔Z>−ln(S(0)/K)+(gy−σ22)TσT=−d−. S(T)>K \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Z>-\frac{\ln\bigl(S(0)/K\bigr)+\bigl(g_{y}-\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\bigr)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}=-d_{-}.

The normal distribution is symmetric, so ℚ(Z>−d−)=N(d−)\mathbb{Q}(Z>-d_{-})=N(d_{-}), the risk-neutral probability that the call finishes in the money. The first term uses the same event but weights each exercised outcome by its share price, so N(d+)N(d_{+}) is not a probability of exercise. Higher share prices get more weight, so N(d+)>N(d−)N(d_{+})>N(d_{-}). The contingent claims notebook builds the call premium from these two pieces.

A worked call. Take S(0)=100S(0)=100 USD/share, K=105K=105 USD/share, T=0.25T=0.25 year, σ=0.30yr−1/2\sigma=0.30\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}, and gy=0.04yr−1g_{y}=0.04\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}. Then d+=−0.1836d_{+}=-0.1836, d−=d+−σT=−0.3336d_{-}=d_{+}-\sigma\sqrt{T}=-0.3336, N(d+)=0.42716N(d_{+})=0.42716, and N(d−)=0.36934N(d_{-})=0.36934. The discounted strike is 105e−0.01=103.955105\,e^{-0.01}=103.955 USD/share, so:

𝒫c=100(0.42716)−103.955(0.36934)=42.716−38.395=4.32USD/share. \mathcal{P}_{c}=100(0.42716)-103.955(0.36934)=42.716-38.395=4.32\ \mathrm{USD/share}.

Under ℚ\mathbb{Q}, this call finishes in the money with probability 0.37. The BSM put formula gives 𝒫p=8.28\mathcal{P}_{p}=8.28 USD/share, and put–call parity checks both: 4.32−8.28=−3.96=100−103.964.32-8.28=-3.96=100-103.96.

How do the BSM equation and the lattice fit in? The BSM equation is a partial differential equation for the premium. We do not solve it in this course; L9a states that its solution, worked backward from the payoff, equals the risk-neutral premium, and the contingent claims notebook derives it. A European premium computed on a CRR lattice, where the holder always holds, approaches the BSM premium as the time step shrinks: with 91 steps, about one per day, the lattice gives 4.31 USD/share for this call. The BSM formula has no early exercise, so an American contract whose early exercise can pay, such as a put, needs a method that models it: the CRR lattice (L9b) or Longstaff–Schwartz simulation (L10a).
