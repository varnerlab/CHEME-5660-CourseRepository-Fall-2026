How do a collar's strikes set its cost, floor, cap, and chance of loss?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

A higher put strike raises the floor but costs more. A higher call strike raises the cap but brings in less. If the collar costs money to open, its breakeven sits above that of the shares, so it loses slightly more often than the shares, but never by more than its maximum loss. A collar still contains a sold call, so the shares can be called away just as in a covered call.

2 Worked explanation

What does each strike set? With shares bought at S(0)S(0), a put bought at K1K_{1} for 𝒫1\mathcal{P}_{1}, and a call sold at K2K_{2} for 𝒫2\mathcal{P}_{2}, every number in L11b follows from one quantity, the total cost grown to expiration, C=𝒟T,0(gy)⋅(S(0)+𝒫1−𝒫2)C=\mathcal{D}_{T,0}(g_{y})\cdot\left(S(0)+\mathcal{P}_{1}-\mathcal{P}_{2}\right):

maximum loss=C−K1,maximum profit=K2−C,S‡=C. \text{maximum loss}=C-K_{1},\qquad\text{maximum profit}=K_{2}-C,\qquad S^{\ddagger}=C.

Raising K1K_{1} lifts the floor, but the put costs more, so CC rises. Raising K2K_{2} lifts the cap, but the call collects less, so CC also rises. The net premium 𝒫1−𝒫2\mathcal{P}_{1}-\mathcal{P}_{2} decides whether the collar opens for a debit, at zero cost, or for a credit.

Three collars on one share. Take S(0)=100S(0)=100 USD/share, three months to expiration, and gy=0.04yr−1g_{y}=0.04\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}, so 𝒟T,0(gy)=1.0101\mathcal{D}_{T,0}(g_{y})=1.0101. The quotes (USD/share) are BSM premiums at σ=0.25yr−1/2\sigma=0.25\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}: puts at 95 for 2.43 and at 90 for 1.13, calls at 105 for 3.35 and at 110 for 1.92. Each collar is labeled by its strikes K1/K2K_{1}/K_{2}, amounts are in USD/share, and the last column is the probability of a loss under ℚ\mathbb{Q}, ℚ(S(T)<S‡)\mathbb{Q}(S(T)<S^{\ddagger}):

Position Net premium Max loss Max profit Breakeven ℚ\mathbb{Q}(loss)
95/105 credit 0.92 5.08 4.92 100.08 0.495
95/110 debit 0.51 6.52 8.48 101.52 0.541
90/110 credit 0.79 10.21 9.79 100.21 0.500
Shares 101.01 unlimited 101.01 0.525

For K1=95K_{1}=95, a call near 108 USD/share would pay for the whole put, a zero-cost collar. Listed strikes come in steps, so in practice we pick the nearest one.

Why can a collar lose more often than the shares? The 95/110 collar opens for a debit, so its breakeven, 101.52, sits above the shares’ 101.01. Between the strikes, it moves one for one with the shares but trails them by the debit grown to expiration, so it loses on slightly more outcomes, 0.541 against 0.525. In exchange, it can never lose more than 6.52 USD/share, while the shares can lose 101.01. The shares alone earn more for any S(T)>K1−𝒟T,0(gy)⋅(𝒫1−𝒫2)=94.48S(T)>K_{1}-\mathcal{D}_{T,0}(g_{y})\cdot(\mathcal{P}_{1}-\mathcal{P}_{2})=94.48 USD/share; below that, the floor pays. A credit collar such as 95/105 lowers the breakeven instead, but caps the profit sooner.

Which strikes should we choose? No formula picks them. The put strike sets the largest loss we accept, the call strike the largest gain we give up, and the net premium follows from both. The SPY collar example compares these numbers using market quotes.

Does a collar protect us from losing the shares? No. A collar holds the same short call as a covered call. If S(T)>K2S(T)>K_{2}, the call is exercised and the shares are sold at K2K_{2}. The put protects the value of the shares, not our ownership of them. Listed calls are American, so the shares can also be called away before expiration, most likely just before an ex-dividend date, when the call is deep in the money and the dividend is worth more than the extrinsic value left in the call (L10a). To keep the shares, we must buy the call back before then, at its current premium.
