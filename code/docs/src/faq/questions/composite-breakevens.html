How do we track the signs, breakevens, and POP of a multi-leg contract?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

Add the leg premiums with a plus sign for each leg we buy and a minus sign for each leg we sell. Then write the profit on each interval between the strikes, where it is a straight line, and set the sloped lines to zero to find the breakevens. The POP is the probability that the share price at expiration lands where the profit is positive.

2 Worked explanation

Step 1: the composite premium and its sign. Each premium 𝒫i\mathcal{P}_{i} is a positive number, and the orientation θi\theta_{i} (11 bought, −1-1 sold) sets its sign. So the composite premium is what we pay minus what we collect:

𝒫𝒞=∑i∈𝒞θini𝒫i=∑long legsni𝒫i−∑short legsni𝒫i. \mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}\theta_{i}\,n_{i}\,\mathcal{P}_{i}=\sum_{\text{long legs}}n_{i}\,\mathcal{P}_{i}-\sum_{\text{short legs}}n_{i}\,\mathcal{P}_{i}.

A positive 𝒫𝒞\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}} is a price we pay, a debit. A negative one is a price we are paid, a credit of −𝒫𝒞-\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}. Take an iron condor on a share at S(0)=100S(0)=100 USD/share, 30 days from expiration, with these quotes (European BSM premiums at σ=0.30yr−1/2\sigma=0.30\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}, in USD/share): a long put at 85 for 0.08, a short put at 90 for 0.40, a short call at 110 for 0.66, and a long call at 115 for 0.22. Then:

𝒫𝒞=(1)(0.08)+(−1)(0.40)+(−1)(0.66)+(1)(0.22)=−0.76USD/share, \begin{aligned} \mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}&=(1)(0.08)+(-1)(0.40)+(-1)(0.66)+(1)(0.22)\\ &=-0.76\ \mathrm{USD/share}, \end{aligned}

a credit of 0.76 USD/share. With gy=0.04yr−1g_{y}=0.04\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}, 𝒟T,0(gy)=e0.04⋅30/365=1.0033\mathcal{D}_{T,0}(g_{y})=e^{0.04\cdot30/365}=1.0033, so the credit grown to expiration is 0.76250.7625 USD/share.

Step 2: the profit on each interval. Between neighboring strikes, each leg stays in or out of the money, so the profit is a straight line. On each interval, keep only the in-the-money legs. Below 85 USD/share, for example, both puts pay:

P̂𝒞(S(T))=0.7625−(90−S(T))+(85−S(T))=0.7625−5=−4.2375USD/share. \begin{aligned} \hat{P}_{\mathcal{C}}(S(T))&=0.7625-(90-S(T))+(85-S(T))\\ &=0.7625-5=-4.2375\ \mathrm{USD/share}. \end{aligned}

The other intervals follow the same way (USD/share):

Step 3: breakevens, maximum profit, and maximum loss. Setting each sloped line to zero gives S−‡=90−0.7625=89.2375S^{\ddagger}_{-}=90-0.7625=89.2375 and S+‡=110+0.7625=110.7625S^{\ddagger}_{+}=110+0.7625=110.7625 USD/share. The maximum profit is the credit grown to expiration, 0.7625 USD/share, and the maximum loss is 5−0.7625=4.23755-0.7625=4.2375 USD/share on either side.

Why don’t the two sides’ losses add up? Each side does act as its own credit spread. As separate trades, the put spread collects 0.40−0.08=0.320.40-0.08=0.32 and the call spread 0.66−0.22=0.440.66-0.22=0.44 USD/share. At S(T)=80S(T)=80 USD/share, the put spread loses 5 USD/share less its credit grown to expiration, and the call spread keeps its credit grown to expiration. The two credits add up to the condor’s credit, so together they make the condor’s maximum loss:

(0.32+0.44)(1.0033)−5=0.7625−5=−4.2375USD/share. (0.32+0.44)(1.0033)-5=0.7625-5=-4.2375\ \mathrm{USD/share}. The share price can finish below 90 or above 110, but not both, so at most one side loses, and the other side’s credit always offsets part of it.

Step 4: the probability of profit. The condor makes money between its breakevens, so POP=ℙ(S−‡<S(T)<S+‡)\text{POP}=\mathbb{P}\left(S^{\ddagger}_{-}<S(T)<S^{\ddagger}_{+}\right). Under ℚ\mathbb{Q} with the same σ=0.30yr−1/2\sigma=0.30\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}, the POP is 0.790. The short legs’ POEs are 0.111 (put) and 0.133 (call), and 1−0.111−0.133=0.7561-0.111-0.133=0.756 is smaller than the POP: each breakeven lies beyond its short strike, so the condor still profits on some outcomes where a short leg is exercised (exercised but profitable). With simulated share prices, we skip the breakevens: compute the profit of each sample and count the positive ones. This works for any number of legs.

Try it. Drop the long put, so the condor becomes a protected strangle. What happens to the credit, the lower breakeven, and the maximum loss below it?

Show the answer
The credit rises to 0.84 USD/share, or 0.8428 grown to expiration. The lower breakeven moves to 90−0.8428=89.157290-0.8428=89.1572 USD/share, and the maximum loss below it is 89.1572 USD/share, at S(T)=0S(T)=0: the put side is no longer capped.
