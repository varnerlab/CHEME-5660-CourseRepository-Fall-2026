Why does daily leverage not multiply the long-run return?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

The daily multiple applies before each day’s result compounds. A benchmark can return to its starting price while a product targeting twice its daily return finishes below its starting value.

2 Worked explanation

A daily multiple is applied to each day’s return, and those results compound. Many leveraged ETFs target a multiple of daily performance; their longer-horizon return can differ substantially from that multiple of the benchmark’s return. See the SEC’s investor bulletin on leveraged and inverse ETFs.

Consider an idealized product that delivers exactly twice each day’s benchmark return, ignoring fees, financing costs, and tracking differences. Start both values at 100. The benchmark gains 10% on day one, reaching 110, then loses 1/11≈9.09%1/11\approx9.09\% on day two, returning to 100.

The daily two-times product follows a different calculation:

100(1+2(0.10))(1+2(−111))=120(911)≈98.18. 100(1+2(0.10))\left(1+2\left(-\frac{1}{11}\right)\right) =120\left(\frac{9}{11}\right) \approx98.18.

The benchmark finishes unchanged while the product loses approximately 1.82%. This does not mean every leveraged path must decline. It shows why the sequence of returns and the reset interval matter, and why returning to the benchmark’s starting price does not guarantee that the leveraged product also returns to its starting value.
