Why does a higher yield lower a bond’s price?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

With promised payments fixed, a higher yield discounts every payment more heavily. Duration and convexity summarize the local sensitivity of the resulting price.

2 Worked explanation

Why does a higher yield lower a bond’s price? Keep the promised payments fixed. Raising the yield increases the denominator used to discount each payment, so each present value falls. For example, the two payments from L2a, 2 USD in six months and 102 USD in one year, are worth 100 USD at a 4% nominal yield and 98.09 USD at 6%. The investor requires more growth from the same cash payments, which requires a lower purchase price.

What does duration measure? At the 4% yield, those payment present values are 2/1.02=1.96082/1.02=1.9608 and 102/1.022=98.0392102/1.02^2=98.0392 USD. Divide by the 100 USD price to get their weights. Macaulay duration averages the payment times using these weights:

Dmac=0.5(0.019608)+1(0.980392)=0.9902yr. D_{\mathrm{mac}}=0.5(0.019608)+1(0.980392)=0.9902\ \mathrm{yr}.

Most value arrives at the last date, so duration is close to one year. Moving more present value to the earlier coupon would lower this weighted time.

How much will a yield change move the price? Modified duration gives the first-order price sensitivity; convexity corrects for curvature. With the lecture’s nominal-yield convention:

Dmod=Dmac1+y/n,ΔVBVB≈−DmodΔy+12K(Δy)2. D_{\mathrm{mod}}=\frac{D_{\mathrm{mac}}}{1+y/n},\qquad \frac{\Delta V_B}{V_B}\approx-D_{\mathrm{mod}}\Delta y+\frac12K(\Delta y)^2.

For a separate bond with Dmod=5D_{\mathrm{mod}}=5 yr, K=30yr2K=30\,\mathrm{yr}^2, and a yield increase Δy=0.01yr−1\Delta y=0.01\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}, the estimated price change is −0.05+0.0015=−0.0485-0.05+0.0015=-0.0485, or a 4.85% decrease. This local approximation is most useful for small yield changes.

How do we value payments with a yield curve? The curve supplies a discount factor D(0,tj)D(0,t_j) for each payment date. Adding the discounted payments gives the price; setting that price equal to par and solving for the coupon rate gives:

VB=∑j=1NXjD(0,tj),cpar=n1−D(0,tN)∑j=1ND(0,tj). V_B=\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_jD(0,t_j),\qquad c_{\mathrm{par}}=n\frac{1-D(0,t_N)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}D(0,t_j)}.

The par coupon makes the present value of the coupons equal the difference between par and the present value of the final par repayment.

For example, if the six-month and one-year discount factors are 0.98 and 0.95, the same payments have value 2(0.98)+102(0.95)=98.862(0.98)+102(0.95)=98.86 USD. A par coupon must fill the 100−100(0.95)=5100-100(0.95)=5 USD gap. Thus each coupon is 5/(0.98+0.95)=2.59075/(0.98+0.95)=2.5907 USD, corresponding to cpar=2(2.5907)/100=0.05181yr−1c_{\mathrm{par}}=2(2.5907)/100=0.05181\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}.
