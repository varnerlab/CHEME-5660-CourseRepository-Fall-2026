What changes when another price observation arrives?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

An exponential moving average updates the estimated mean and variance of the growth rate. Its half-life controls how quickly older observations lose influence, while the forecast horizon sets how far ahead we use the resulting model.

2 Worked explanation

What should we do with a new price observation? We convert it to a growth rate, update the estimates using only the information available now, and use the updated estimates for the next forecast. An exponential moving average (EMA) controls how much the new observation changes the estimates.

What changes when another price arrives? The new growth observation gkg_k differs from the previous mean by the innovation δk=gk−mk−1\delta_k=g_k-m_{k-1}. We retain a fraction λ\lambda of the old information and give the new observation weight 1−λ1-\lambda:

mk=λmk−1+(1−λ)gk=mk−1+(1−λ)δk. m_k=\lambda m_{k-1}+(1-\lambda)g_k =m_{k-1}+(1-\lambda)\delta_k.

For a 21-observation half-life, λ=2−1/21≈0.9675\lambda=2^{-1/21}\approx0.9675, so the new observation receives a weight of about 3.25% in the updated mean. The half-life describes how long the estimates remember old data; TT describes how far ahead we forecast.

Why is the variance update different? We measure the variance about the updated mean. Re-centering the old observations on the new mean adds the squared shift of the mean to the old variance. Combining that contribution with the new observation’s gives:

vk=λ[vk−1+(mk−1−mk)2]+(1−λ)(gk−mk)2. v_k=\lambda\left[v_{k-1}+(m_{k-1}-m_k)^2\right] +(1-\lambda)(g_k-m_k)^2.

The mean update gives mk−mk−1=(1−λ)δkm_k-m_{k-1}=(1-\lambda)\delta_k, and the new observation’s deviation is gk−mk=λδkg_k-m_k=\lambda\delta_k. Substituting these two deviations and collecting terms yields:

vk=λvk−1+λ(1−λ)2δk2+(1−λ)λ2δk2=λ[vk−1+(1−λ)δk2]. \begin{aligned} v_k&=\lambda v_{k-1}+\lambda(1-\lambda)^2\delta_k^2 +(1-\lambda)\lambda^2\delta_k^2\\ &=\lambda\left[v_{k-1}+(1-\lambda)\delta_k^2\right]. \end{aligned}

These are exponentially weighted moments, and vkv_k carries no finite-sample correction for bias. We initialize ms=μ̂g,0m_s=\hat\mu_{g,0} and vs=σ̂02/Δtv_s=\hat\sigma_0^2/\Delta t from the training data. The updated growth variance has units yr−2\mathrm{yr}^{-2}; multiplying by Δt\Delta t and taking the square root gives the GBM volatility σ̂k=vkΔt\hat\sigma_k=\sqrt{v_k\Delta t}. Each new forecast uses the updated estimates. In the course example, the estimates then stay fixed over that forecast’s horizon, so updating them does not by itself model volatility clusters that occur within the horizon.

Work one update. To keep the arithmetic simple, take λ=0.9\lambda=0.9, previous mean mk−1=0.10yr−1m_{k-1}=0.10\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}, previous variance vk−1=4yr−2v_{k-1}=4\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}, and new growth gk=1.10yr−1g_k=1.10\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}. Then δk=1.00yr−1\delta_k=1.00\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1} and:

mk=0.10+0.1(1.00)=0.20yr−1,vk=0.9[4+0.1(1.00)2]=3.69yr−2. m_k=0.10+0.1(1.00)=0.20\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1},\qquad v_k=0.9[4+0.1(1.00)^2]=3.69\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}.

The mean rises. The variance falls because the new observation lies close to the old mean compared with the previous spread. At daily spacing, the updated volatility is 3.69/252=0.1210yr−1/2\sqrt{3.69/252}=0.1210\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}.

Try it. Use gk=3.10yr−1g_k=3.10\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1} instead. What happens to the variance?

Show the answer
δk=3\delta_k=3, mk=0.40m_k=0.40, and vk=0.9[4+0.1(9)]=4.41v_k=0.9[4+0.1(9)]=4.41. A sufficiently large deviation raises the variance. When we evaluate forecasts later, we should use each one as it was saved when issued, so the evaluation reflects only the information available at that time.
