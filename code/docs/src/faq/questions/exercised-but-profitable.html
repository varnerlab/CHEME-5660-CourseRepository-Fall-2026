How can a short option be exercised and still make money?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

Exercise depends on the strike price, but profit depends on the breakeven, and the premium moves the breakeven past the strike. Between the two, the seller is assigned, but the premium still covers what the seller owes. So the seller’s POP is larger than one minus the POE, and the buyer’s POP is smaller than the POE.

2 Worked explanation

Two reference points. In the L10a example, we sell an NVDA call with strike K=235K=235 USD/share and premium 𝒫c=9.88\mathcal{P}_{c}=9.88 USD/share, expiring in T=0.1178T=0.1178 year, with gy=0.0413yr−1g_{y}=0.0413\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}. The call is exercised when S(T)>K+0.01S(T)>K+0.01. The seller’s profit at expiration is the premium grown to expiration minus the payoff owed:

P̂𝒞(S(T))=𝒟T,0(gy)⋅𝒫c−(S(T)−K)+=9.93−(S(T)−235)+, \hat{P}_{\mathcal{C}}(S(T))=\mathcal{D}_{T,0}(g_{y})\cdot\mathcal{P}_{c}-(S(T)-K)^{+}=9.93-(S(T)-235)^{+},

which is positive below the breakeven S‡=K+𝒟T,0(gy)⋅𝒫c=244.93S^{\ddagger}=K+\mathcal{D}_{T,0}(g_{y})\cdot\mathcal{P}_{c}=244.93 USD/share. So the share price at expiration falls into one of three ranges:

Share price at expiration Exercised? Seller’s profit (USD/share)
S(T)≤235.01S(T)\leq 235.01 no 9.93, the whole premium
235.01<S(T)<244.93235.01<S(T)<244.93 yes between 0 and 9.93
S(T)>244.93S(T)>244.93 yes negative, without limit

At S(T)=240S(T)=240 USD/share, the call is exercised and the seller owes 240−235=5240-235=5 USD/share, but keeps a profit of 9.93−5=4.939.93-5=4.93 USD/share. Being assigned means the seller must deliver the shares at the strike price; it does not mean the trade lost money.

How do the probabilities fit together? The seller profits when the call is not exercised, or when it is exercised but finishes below the breakeven:

seller’s POP=(1−POE)⏟S(T)≤K+0.01+ℙ(K+0.01<S(T)<S‡)⏟exercised, still profitable. \text{seller's POP}=\underbrace{(1-\text{POE})}_{S(T)\,\leq\,K+0.01}+\underbrace{\mathbb{P}\left(K+0.01<S(T)<S^{\ddagger}\right)}_{\text{exercised, still profitable}}.

Under ℚ\mathbb{Q}, the POE is 0.4295, so 0.6976=0.5705+0.12710.6976=0.5705+0.1271: about one time in eight, the seller is assigned and still makes money. For the buyer, the same middle range is exercised but unprofitable, so the buyer’s POP is smaller than the POE.

The same thing in a composite contract. In the L11a put credit spread on AMD, the short put at K2=520K_{2}=520 USD/share is exercised below 519.99 USD/share, but the breakeven is S‡=511.20S^{\ddagger}=511.20 USD/share, below the strike by the credit grown to expiration. Under ℚ\mathbb{Q}, the short put’s POE is 0.492 and the spread’s POP is 0.540, which is larger than 1−0.492=0.5081-0.492=0.508. Between 511.20 and 520 USD/share, the short put is assigned and the spread still makes money. Multi-leg breakevens shows how to find them for any composite contract.

Try it. What is the seller’s profit on the NVDA call if S(T)=250S(T)=250 USD/share?

Show the answer
9.93−(250−235)=−5.079.93-(250-235)=-5.07 USD/share. The share price is above the breakeven, so the payoff owed is larger than the premium kept.
