What does a fitted log-price line predict?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

The fitted line predicts log price at an observation time. Exponentiating gives a fitted price trend; interpreting it as an expected price under GBM requires the volatility correction.

2 Worked explanation

What does the fitted line predict at one observation? Suppose the fitted intercept is ln(100)\ln(100) and the slope is 0.06yr−10.06\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}. At t=0.5t=0.5 yr, the fitted log price is ln(100)+0.06(0.5)\ln(100)+0.06(0.5). Exponentiating gives 100e0.03=103.05100e^{0.03}=103.05 USD/share. If the observed price is 104 USD/share, the residual is the observed log price minus the fitted log price:

rj=ln(104)−[ln(100)+0.03]=0.00922. r_j=\ln(104)-[\ln(100)+0.03]=0.00922.

The residual is dimensionless because we fitted logarithms. In matrix notation, the row [1,0.5][1,0.5] multiplies the intercept and slope to make this same prediction. L6b will replace elapsed time with market growth as the predictor.

What are we fitting, and what does the interval describe? For observation jj, the lecture fits log price as an intercept plus mean growth times elapsed time. Stacking all observations gives:

lnSj=lnS0+μgtj+ϵj⇔𝐲=𝐗̂𝛉+𝛜. \ln S_j=\ln S_0+\mu_gt_j+\epsilon_j \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathbf y=\hat{\mathbf X}\boldsymbol\theta+\boldsymbol\epsilon.

Each row of the design matrix is [1,tj][1,t_j]. Least squares chooses the intercept and slope to minimize the squared residuals. For a fitted coefficient θ̂j\hat\theta_j, the standard error estimates its uncertainty; a smaller value indicates greater precision. The lecture’s two-sided confidence interval is:

θ̂j±t1−α/2,νSE(θ̂j). \hat\theta_j\ \pm\ t_{1-\alpha/2,\nu}\operatorname{SE}(\hat\theta_j).

This is an interval for a model coefficient. A prediction interval for a future price must also account for future random price changes. The classical coefficient interval assumes independent, constant-variance, normally distributed errors. GBM log-price errors accumulate through time and share earlier shocks, so this simplifying regression-error assumption limits the interval’s interpretation. Fitting a line alone does not validate its uncertainty calculation.

Try it. Does the fitted price 103.05 USD identify GBM’s expected price at six months?

Show the answer
it describes the fitted log-price trend and, under GBM, the median price. If σ=0.20yr−1/2\sigma=0.20\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}, expected price is 100e(0.06+0.202/2)(0.5)=104.08100e^{(0.06+0.20^2/2)(0.5)}=104.08 USD. We need the volatility correction when averaging prices.
