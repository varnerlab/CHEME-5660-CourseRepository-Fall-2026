In the Farmer–Joshi market, what decides whether a price drop fades or turns into a crash?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

After a drop, the two groups trade in opposite directions. Trend followers who see a large enough fall sell, which pushes the price down further. Value investors who now see the asset as underpriced buy, which pushes it back up. If the selling outweighs the buying, the fall reaches more trend followers and keeps going. In the example, the mix of the two groups decided which side won.

2 Worked explanation

How does one step work? Each trader holds a position xt(i)x_{t}^{(i)} of −ci-c_{i}, 00, or +ci+c_{i}, and places an order only when that position changes, ωt+1(i)=xt+1(i)−xt(i)\omega_{t+1}^{(i)}=x_{t+1}^{(i)}-x_{t}^{(i)}. The market maker moves the log price by the net order divided by the liquidity λ\lambda:

lnSt+1=lnSt+1λ∑iωt+1(i)+σSΔtZt+1 \ln S_{t+1}=\ln S_{t}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{i}\omega_{t+1}^{(i)}+\sigma_{S}\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_{t+1}

The new price changes every trader’s signal, and the next step starts. When the price falls, traders with no position enter:

Trader Signal after a fall Below −χiin-\chi^{\text{in}}_{i} Effect on the price
Trend follower lnSt−lnSt−θi\ln S_{t}-\ln S_{t-\theta_{i}} turns negative sells pushes it down
Value investor lnSt−(lnVt+v(i))\ln S_{t}-(\ln V_{t}+v^{(i)}) falls buys pushes it back up

Exits add orders on both sides. A trend follower with a long position sells to exit once its signal falls below χiout\chi^{\text{out}}_{i}, which is zero or negative. A value investor with a short position buys to exit once its signal falls below χiout\chi^{\text{out}}_{i}.

When does a drop keep going? Each order moves the price only once, in the step it is placed. A trend follower that has sold has nothing more to sell until it exits. A value investor that has bought has nothing more to buy. Each step of the fall pushes some new trend followers past their entry thresholds, and some new value investors past theirs. If the new sellers outweigh the new buyers, their selling causes the next fall, which reaches the next trend followers. The drop stops when the buying outweighs the selling, or when no trend followers are left to sell.

How does the mix of traders matter? The Farmer–Joshi example keeps 1,200 value investors and changes the number of trend followers. The table shows averages over three markets:

Trend followers Excess kurtosis Lag-1 autocorrelation of |g|\lvert g\rvert

With 600 or fewer trend followers, the value investors absorb the drops, and the growth rates look Normal. With 1,200 or more, large moves are common (fat tails), and large moves tend to follow large moves (volatility clustering). At 900, some markets have large moves and some do not.

What should the other settings do? The example changes only the mix. The price rule tells us what to expect from two other settings:

  • Liquidity λ\lambda. An order of size cc moves the log price by c/λc/\lambda. A larger λ\lambda means each order moves the price less, so each step of a fall should reach fewer new thresholds.
  • Thresholds. Trend followers with small entry thresholds react to small falls. In the example, the entry thresholds are drawn between 0.2 and 4, so traders join a fall at different points, not all at once.

Can value investors make a fall worse? Not because of the price. A falling price lowers every value investor’s signal, so it can only make them buy or close a short. But not all of them buy. Each value investor compares the price with its own estimate of the value, lnVt+v(i)\ln V_{t}+v^{(i)}, and the offsets v(i)v^{(i)} differ. An investor with a low estimate may still think the asset is overpriced after a drop, so it does not buy, and if it is short, it stays short. Value investors sell during a fall only if the value itself falls.

Does a trend follower close a short if the price stays flat? It can. Once the fall is more than θi\theta_{i} steps old, the trader’s signal compares two prices from after the fall, so it returns to about zero. A short trend follower exits when its signal rises above −χiout-\chi^{\text{out}}_{i}. With χiout\chi^{\text{out}}_{i} near zero, noise alone can trigger the exit. With χiout=−0.2\chi^{\text{out}}_{i}=-0.2, the trader waits until the log price has risen by 0.2 over its lag.

What does the model leave out? The market maker fills every net order at the price the rule sets, so a large imbalance only costs a large price move. Traders have no cash or share balances, and any trader can go short. In the L12b Wolfram market, by contrast, traders can run out of cash or shares (see how the dealer sets the price). We also do not fit λ\lambda in the course. Farmer and Joshi chose their parameters to keep the price near the value and to match the volatility of real prices. To fit λ\lambda to a real market, we would measure how far prices move for a given net order in trade data.

Try it. With λ=1\lambda=1, 20 trend followers each sell c=0.005c=0.005 in one step, and 8 value investors each buy c=0.005c=0.005. Ignoring the noise, by what percentage does the price change? What if λ=2\lambda=2?

Show the answer
The net order is (8−20)×0.005=−0.06(8-20)\times 0.005=-0.06, so lnS\ln S falls by 0.06/λ0.06/\lambda. With λ=1\lambda=1, the price falls by 1−e−0.06=5.8%1-e^{-0.06}=5.8\%. With λ=2\lambda=2, it falls by 1−e−0.03=3.0%1-e^{-0.03}=3.0\%.
