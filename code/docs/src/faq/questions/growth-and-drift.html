How do mean growth and drift differ?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

Drift controls the growth of the expected price. Mean growth describes expected log growth per unit time. The half-variance correction connects these two averages under GBM.

2 Worked explanation

Which average do drift and mean growth describe? Geometric Brownian motion uses drift μ\mu for expected price growth and volatility σ\sigma for the size of random changes. With constant parameters, the price equation is:

dS(t)S(t)=μdt+σdW(t). \frac{dS(t)}{S(t)}=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW(t).

Here W(t)W(t) is a Wiener process; its increment over Δt\Delta t has variance Δt\Delta t. The random price increment has zero conditional mean, so averaging the price equation gives d𝔼[S(t)]/dt=μ𝔼[S(t)]d\mathbb E[S(t)]/dt=\mu\mathbb E[S(t)]. Solving this mean equation gives 𝔼[ST]=S0eμT\mathbb E[S_T]=S_0e^{\mu T}. We will compare that expression with the average obtained from the log-price solution to find how drift relates to mean growth.

For the price at a fixed time TT, the solution in terms of mean growth μg\mu_g is:

ln(ST/S0)=μgT+σTZ,Z∼𝒩(0,1). \ln(S_T/S_0)=\mu_gT+\sigma\sqrt T\,Z, \qquad Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1).

Averaging this equation makes the random term vanish because 𝔼[Z]=0\mathbb E[Z]=0. Thus μg\mu_g is expected log growth per unit time. To average the price itself, we exponentiate first. The normal identity 𝔼[eaZ]=ea2/2\mathbb E[e^{aZ}]=e^{a^2/2}, valid for any real number aa, gives:

𝔼[ST]=S0eμgT𝔼[eσTZ]=S0e(μg+σ2/2)T. \mathbb E[S_T] =S_0e^{\mu_gT}\mathbb E[e^{\sigma\sqrt T Z}] =S_0e^{(\mu_g+\sigma^2/2)T}.

Comparing this result with 𝔼[ST]=S0eμT\mathbb E[S_T]=S_0e^{\mu T} gives μ=μg+σ2/2\mu=\mu_g+\sigma^2/2, or μg=μ−σ2/2\mu_g=\mu-\sigma^2/2. This is the half-variance correction. Averaging prices and averaging their logarithms are different operations; the normal exponential average supplies the precise difference for GBM. The L4b derivation obtains the same correction from Itô’s lemma.

A numerical comparison. Let S0=100S_0=100 USD/share, μ=0.08yr−1\mu=0.08\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}, σ=0.20yr−1/2\sigma=0.20\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}, and T=1T=1 year. Then μg=0.06yr−1\mu_g=0.06\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}, giving:

𝔼[ST]=100e0.08≈108.33USD/share,median(ST)=100e0.06≈106.18USD/share. \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[S_T]&=100e^{0.08}\approx108.33\ \mathrm{USD/share},\\ \operatorname{median}(S_T)&=100e^{0.06}\approx106.18\ \mathrm{USD/share}. \end{aligned}

The median splits the possible prices into equal lower and upper halves. It follows from the median Z=0Z=0 and the increasing exponential function. The right tail pulls the expected price above the median; neither summary identifies a particular future path.

Which number belongs in a simulation? The equivalent updates are:

St+Δt=SteμgΔt+σΔtZ=Ste(μ−σ2/2)Δt+σΔtZ. S_{t+\Delta t}=S_t e^{\mu_g\Delta t+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z} =S_t e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)\Delta t+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z}.

The same draw ZZ gives the same price in both forms. If we estimate μg\mu_g from observed growth rates, the correction is already included; subtracting it again would lower the simulated mean growth incorrectly.

Try it. Hold μ=0.08yr−1\mu=0.08\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1} fixed and increase σ\sigma to 0.40yr−1/20.40\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}.

Show the answer
μg=0\mu_g=0, the expected one-year price remains about 108.33 USD/share, and the median becomes 100 USD/share. Higher volatility separates the two averages further.
