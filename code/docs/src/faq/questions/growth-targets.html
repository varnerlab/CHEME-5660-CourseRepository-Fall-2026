How do exact growth targets differ from growth floors?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

An exact target requires the portfolio’s mean growth to equal a chosen value. A floor accepts any allocation that meets or exceeds it, so a floor can keep the minimum-variance portfolio when that portfolio’s mean already clears the floor.

2 Worked explanation

How much additional risk does a growth requirement impose? The lecture first requires the weighted sample means to equal a target, (𝐠′)⊤𝐰=g⋆(\mathbf g^{\prime})^{\top}\mathbf w=g_\star, while allowing short positions. Plotting the minimum variance at each target traces the minimum-variance frontier.

Return to the two uncorrelated assets with variances (1,4)yr−2(1,4)\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2} and suppose their means are (0.04,0.14)yr−1(0.04,0.14)\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}. The GMV weights (0.8,0.2)(0.8,0.2) give mean growth 0.06yr−10.06\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}. A floor of 0.050.05 leaves this allocation unchanged. An exact target of 0.050.05 instead requires weights (0.9,0.1)(0.9,0.1) and variance 0.85yr−20.85\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}. The exact target selects a lower-growth, higher-risk allocation than the GMV portfolio; the floor accepts the GMV allocation.

To see where those weights come from, write the first-asset weight as ww and the second as 1−w1-w. The mean growth is 0.04w+0.14(1−w)=0.14−0.10w0.04w+0.14(1-w)=0.14-0.10w. Setting it to 0.05 gives w=0.9w=0.9. A floor asks only for a mean of at least 0.05; the GMV mean of 0.06 already qualifies.

the floor now binds, at weights (0.5,0.5)(0.5,0.5), giving variance 1.25yr−21.25\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}. A long-only floor above 0.14yr−10.14\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1} is infeasible. Setting the floor to the smallest asset mean, as the examples do in their GMV solve, admits every fully invested long-only allocation.

The formula for an exact target

The two-asset calculation above determines the weights from the budget and growth equations. With more assets, many allocations can satisfy both equations; the covariance determines which has the least risk. The lecture writes that solution using four shorthand coefficients. For a positive-definite 𝚺g\mathbf\Sigma_g and unequal asset sample means, calculate:

a=𝟏⊤𝚺g−1𝟏,b=𝟏⊤𝚺g−1𝐠′,c=(𝐠′)⊤𝚺g−1𝐠′,d=ac−b2>0. \begin{aligned} a&=\mathbf1^{\top}\mathbf\Sigma_g^{-1}\mathbf1,& b&=\mathbf1^{\top}\mathbf\Sigma_g^{-1}\mathbf g^{\prime},\\ c&=(\mathbf g^{\prime})^{\top}\mathbf\Sigma_g^{-1}\mathbf g^{\prime},&d&=ac-b^2>0. \end{aligned}

The budget and growth constraints determine how much of each of two vectors enters the solution. The resulting weights and variance are:

𝐰⋆(g⋆)=c−bg⋆d𝚺g−1𝟏+ag⋆−bd𝚺g−1𝐠′,σg2(g⋆)=ag⋆2−2bg⋆+cd=1a+ad(g⋆−ba)2. \begin{aligned} \mathbf w_\star(g_\star) &=\frac{c-bg_\star}{d}\mathbf\Sigma_g^{-1}\mathbf1 +\frac{ag_\star-b}{d}\mathbf\Sigma_g^{-1}\mathbf g^{\prime},\\ \sigma_g^2(g_\star) &=\frac{ag_\star^2-2bg_\star+c}{d} =\frac1a+\frac ad\left(g_\star-\frac ba\right)^2. \end{aligned}

The last expression separates the GMV variance, 1/a1/a, from the extra variance that the target imposes. The GMV mean is b/ab/a. The upper branch, g⋆≥b/ag_\star\geq b/a, is the efficient one: every point on the lower branch has a counterpart on the upper branch with the same risk and higher growth.
