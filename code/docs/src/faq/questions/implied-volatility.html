Which volatility goes into the premium, and how do we find implied volatility?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

Historical volatility is estimated from past share prices. Implied volatility is the value that makes the BSM premium of a European contract equal its market premium, and bisection finds it because the premium rises with volatility.

2 Worked explanation

Why does volatility matter to the premium? A call pays (S(T)−K)+(S(T)-K)^{+}, and a put pays (K−S(T))+(K-S(T))^{+}. Neither payoff falls below zero. In the BSM model, with the other inputs fixed, a larger volatility spreads out the share price at expiration, which adds outcomes with large payoffs and costs the holder nothing in the outcomes where the payoff is already zero. So the premium rises with σ\sigma.

Which volatility? L9a gives two ways to put a number on σ\sigma:

Historical volatility Implied volatility
Source Past share prices: σann=Δt⋅σg\sigma_{\mathrm{ann}}=\sqrt{\Delta t}\cdot\sigma_{g} The market premium of an option
Direction Looks backward Looks forward
Use Our forecast under ℙ\mathbb{P} Comparing a model premium with market quotes

Here σg\sigma_{g} is the standard deviation of the growth rates (units: yr−1\mathrm{yr}^{-1}) and Δt\Delta t is the time between observations (units: years). Trading platforms compute the Greeks with the implied volatility (L10a).

How does bisection find the implied volatility? We look for the σ\sigma whose BSM premium equals the market premium. Take a European call with S(0)=100S(0)=100 USD/share, K=105K=105 USD/share, T=0.25T=0.25 year, and gy=0.04yr−1g_{y}=0.04\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}, quoted at 6.00 USD/share. The bracket σlo=0.05\sigma_{\text{lo}}=0.05 and σhi=1.00\sigma_{\text{hi}}=1.00 gives BSM premiums of 0.07 and 18.21 USD/share, which bracket 6.00. Each step prices the midpoint. A premium above 6.00 USD/share moves the upper end down, and one below moves the lower end up:

Step σlo\sigma_{\text{lo}} σhi\sigma_{\text{hi}} σmid\sigma_{\text{mid}} 𝒫(σmid)\mathcal{P}(\sigma_{\text{mid}}) Update
1 0.0500 1.0000 0.5250 8.79 σhi←σmid\sigma_{\text{hi}}\gets\sigma_{\text{mid}}
2 0.0500 0.5250 0.2875 4.08 σlo←σmid\sigma_{\text{lo}}\gets\sigma_{\text{mid}}
3 0.2875 0.5250 0.4063 6.42 σhi←σmid\sigma_{\text{hi}}\gets\sigma_{\text{mid}}
4 0.2875 0.4063 0.3469 5.24 σlo←σmid\sigma_{\text{lo}}\gets\sigma_{\text{mid}}
5 0.3469 0.4063 0.3766 5.83 σlo←σmid\sigma_{\text{lo}}\gets\sigma_{\text{mid}}

Each step halves the bracket. After 14 steps, the bracket is narrower than 10−410^{-4}, and the implied volatility is σ̂=0.385yr−1/2\hat{\sigma}=0.385\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}.

What does the implied volatility tell us? L9a uses it for a back-of-the-envelope one-standard-deviation range for the share price, S(0)(1±IVT)=100(1±0.385⋅0.5)S(0)\bigl(1\pm\text{IV}\sqrt{T}\bigr)=100\,(1\pm0.385\cdot0.5), or about 80.75 to 119.25 USD/share over the next three months. Under the model, the share price ends inside this range about 68% of the time.

Why does each strike have its own implied volatility? The BSM model assumes one constant σ\sigma for every strike and expiration. Backing out σ\sigma strike by strike from market quotes gives a different value at each strike, the volatility smile or skew. The market’s premiums are not consistent with a single constant volatility. The SPXW volatility skew notebook measures this skew for S&P 500 index options.

Try it. If the same call were quoted at 4.32 USD/share, what implied volatility would bisection return?

Show the answer
About 0.30, because 4.32 USD/share is the BSM premium of this call at σ=0.30yr−1/2\sigma=0.30\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}.
