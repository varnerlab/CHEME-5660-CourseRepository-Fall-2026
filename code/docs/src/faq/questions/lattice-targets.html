Which lattice prices beat a trading target?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

First translate the required scaled NPV into a sale-price threshold. Then add the real-world probabilities of terminal nodes whose prices strictly exceed it.

2 Worked explanation

How do we turn a trading requirement into a probability? First find the sale prices that satisfy the requirement, then add the probabilities of those outcomes. We buy shares now and sell at a scheduled terminal date. Scaled NPV compares discounted sale proceeds with the purchase cost.

Which lattice outcomes beat our target? Buying n0n_0 shares costs n0S0n_0S_0 today; their sale brings n0STn_0S_T at time TT. Ignoring dividends and trading costs, discount and divide by the purchase cost:

ρT=−n0S0+n0STe−gyTn0S0=STS0e−gyT−1. \rho_T=\frac{-n_0S_0+n_0S_Te^{-g_yT}}{n_0S_0} =\frac{S_T}{S_0}e^{-g_yT}-1.

Try the two-step tree from L3b. Start at 100 USD/share with u=1.20u=1.20, d=0.90d=0.90, and real-world up probability p=0.65p=0.65. Set the benchmark to zero for this illustration and require ρT>0.05\rho_T>0.05. The terminal prices are 81, 108, and 144 USD/share for zero, one, and two up moves. The required price is 100(1.05)=105100(1.05)=105, so the middle and upper nodes succeed. Their combined probability is:

ℙ(ρT>0.05)=2(0.65)(0.35)+0.652=0.8775. \mathbb P(\rho_T>0.05)=2(0.65)(0.35)+0.65^2=0.8775.

There are two paths to the middle price but only one to the top price. This is why their probabilities have different counting factors.

How does the threshold formula reproduce this calculation? At a terminal lattice node with kk up moves, substitute ST/S0=ukdN−kS_T/S_0=u^kd^{N-k}. For a target ρ⋆>−1\rho_\star>-1 and factors u>d>0u>d>0, taking logs of ρ(k)>ρ⋆\rho(k)>\rho_\star and collecting the terms in kk gives:

klnu+(N−k)lnd>ln(1+ρ⋆)+gyT,k>τ(ρ⋆)=ln(1+ρ⋆)+gyT−Nlndln(u/d). \begin{aligned} k\ln u+(N-k)\ln d&>\ln(1+\rho_\star)+g_yT,\\ k>\tau(\rho_\star)&=\frac{\ln(1+\rho_\star)+g_yT-N\ln d}{\ln(u/d)}. \end{aligned}

The up count is an integer, so the first strictly successful count is ⌊τ⌋+1\lfloor\tau\rfloor+1. Add the binomial probabilities for that count and all higher counts. If τ=2\tau=2 exactly, success starts at three up moves; two only meets the target. Clipping the first count to [0,N+1][0,N+1] represents the cases where every node or no node succeeds. This turns a trading requirement into a probability over the model’s terminal outcomes.

Here τ=ln(1.05/0.902)/ln(1.20/0.90)≈0.902\tau=\ln(1.05/0.90^2)/\ln(1.20/0.90)\approx0.902, so the first successful count is one, as we found by inspecting the prices.

Try it. Raise the target to ρ⋆=0.08\rho_\star=0.08 with a strict inequality.

the 108 USD middle node only meets the target. Only 144 USD succeeds, giving probability 0.652=0.42250.65^2=0.4225.

Where do bid and ask enter? Buying at an ask of 100.10 and immediately selling at a bid of 99.90 loses 0.20 USD/share. For executable trade cash flows, use the entry ask and sale bid; a midpoint-based price model omits that spread cost.
