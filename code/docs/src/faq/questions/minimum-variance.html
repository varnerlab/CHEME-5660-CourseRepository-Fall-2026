Why mix assets when one has a smaller variance?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

A mix can have less variance than either asset alone, because each asset’s variance is multiplied by its squared weight and the covariance determines the cross terms. The global minimum-variance (GMV) calculation finds the allocation with the least variance under its constraints.

2 Worked explanation

Why mix assets when one has a smaller variance? Suppose two uncorrelated assets have growth variances 1 and 4yr−24\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}. Put weight ww in the first and 1−w1-w in the second. The portfolio variance is:

σg,p2=w2+4(1−w)2=5(w−0.8)2+0.8[yr−2]. \sigma_{g,p}^2=w^2+4(1-w)^2 =5(w-0.8)^2+0.8\quad[\mathrm{yr}^{-2}].

The squared term vanishes at w=0.8w=0.8, leaving a variance of 0.8yr−20.8\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}, which is below that of either asset alone. Each asset’s variance is multiplied by its squared weight, and zero covariance removes the cross term. This calculation finds the best allocation among all possible weights, whereas random sampling compares only the allocations we happen to draw.

How do we generalize to many assets? The global minimum-variance (GMV) problem takes the covariance matrix as given and chooses the weights. A positive-definite covariance gives a unique minimum. We first allow signed weights, so the budget is the only constraint:

minimize𝐰12𝐰⊤𝚺g𝐰subject to𝟏⊤𝐰=1. \underset{\mathbf w}{\operatorname{minimize}}\quad \frac12\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf\Sigma_g\mathbf w \qquad\text{subject to}\quad\mathbf1^{\top}\mathbf w=1.

Why does the inverse covariance appear? We attach a multiplier to the budget constraint and set the gradient to zero:

ℒ(𝐰,λ)=12𝐰⊤𝚺g𝐰−λ(𝟏⊤𝐰−1),𝚺g𝐰=λ𝟏⇒𝐰=λ𝚺g−1𝟏. \begin{aligned} \mathcal L(\mathbf w,\lambda) &=\tfrac12\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf\Sigma_g\mathbf w -\lambda(\mathbf1^{\top}\mathbf w-1),\\ \mathbf\Sigma_g\mathbf w&=\lambda\mathbf1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbf w=\lambda\mathbf\Sigma_g^{-1}\mathbf1. \end{aligned}

The factor 1/21/2 simplifies differentiation. Requiring the weights to sum to one determines λ\lambda and gives:

𝐰GMV=𝚺g−1𝟏𝟏⊤𝚺g−1𝟏,σg,GMV2=1𝟏⊤𝚺g−1𝟏. \mathbf w_{\mathrm{GMV}} =\frac{\mathbf\Sigma_g^{-1}\mathbf1}{\mathbf1^{\top}\mathbf\Sigma_g^{-1}\mathbf1}, \qquad \sigma_{g,\mathrm{GMV}}^2 =\frac{1}{\mathbf1^{\top}\mathbf\Sigma_g^{-1}\mathbf1}.

The numerator accounts for the variances and covariances, and the denominator rescales the weights so that they sum to one. The data example solves 𝚺̂g𝐱=𝟏\hat{\mathbf\Sigma}_g\mathbf x=\mathbf1 and divides 𝐱\mathbf x by the sum of its entries. Mean-growth estimates enter only when we add a growth requirement.

A two-asset calculation. For the uncorrelated assets above, with growth variances 11 and 4yr−24\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}, the unnormalized vector is (1,1/4)yr2(1,1/4)\,\mathrm{yr}^{2}. Normalizing gives weights (0.8,0.2)(0.8,0.2) and variance 1/(1+1/4)=0.8yr−21/(1+1/4)=0.8\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}. The lower-variance asset receives more of the budget.

What does a negative weight mean? A negative weight is a short position. With 1,000 USD and weights (1.2,−0.2)(1.2,-0.2), we borrow 200 USD worth of the second asset, sell it, and buy 1,200 USD of the first. The short position is a liability: a price increase raises the cost of buying back the borrowed shares. The lecture’s calculation omits borrowing fees, collateral, and dividends owed.
