Why study models that miss important market behavior?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

A simple model connects assumptions with consequences we can calculate and test. Its forecasts become useful when we state the event clearly and compare the forecast probabilities with later outcomes.

2 Worked explanation

We use a simple model to connect assumptions with consequences we can calculate and test. A forecast can be a probability distribution over outcomes; it does not have to name the exact future price.

The fixed-parameter binomial lattice has only two possible one-step growth rates, and its successive moves are independent. It cannot reproduce heavy-tailed one-step growth or volatility clustering. Constant-parameter GBM permits a continuous range of price movements, but its growth rates are normal and independent, so it also misses those features.

Fitting parameters does not make either model memorize the observed path; the fit summarizes the data within the chosen structure. If later observations disagree with the model, we should examine both the parameter uncertainty and the structural assumptions, because the disagreement alone does not tell us which one caused it.

A useful next step is to ask a specific question: does the forecast assign reasonable probabilities to later outcomes, under a clearly stated evaluation rule? This is why we separate fitting from out-of-sample testing. Updating the parameters may help, but each updated forecast must be tested on observations that were unavailable when it was made.
