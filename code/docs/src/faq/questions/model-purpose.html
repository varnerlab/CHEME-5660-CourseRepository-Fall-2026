What can a price model help us decide?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

A model gives probabilities for a specified decision. We can translate a required gain into a terminal-price threshold, calculate the probability of exceeding it, and examine how assumptions change that answer.

2 Worked explanation

What can we decide if we cannot predict the exact price? Suppose we buy a share for S0=100S_0=100 USD/share and plan to sell after T=1T=1 year. We want the proceeds to exceed an investment growing at the benchmark rate gy=0.02yr−1g_y=0.02\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}. We assume a scheduled sale, no dividends or trading costs, and a constant benchmark rate. Discounting the sale price STS_T gives the scaled net present value:

ρT=−S0+STe−gyTS0. \rho_T=\frac{-S_0+S_Te^{-g_yT}}{S_0}.

The target ρT>0\rho_T>0 means that the discounted proceeds exceed the purchase cost. Solving this inequality tells us which future prices count as successes:

ρT>0⇔ST>S0egyT=100e0.02≈102.02USD/share. \rho_T>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad S_T>S_0e^{g_yT}=100e^{0.02}\approx102.02\ \mathrm{USD/share}.

Where does the probability come from? Under geometric Brownian motion (GBM), the log price ratio at the sale date is normally distributed. Let μg=0.06yr−1\mu_g=0.06\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1} be mean growth and σ=0.20yr−1/2\sigma=0.20\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2} be volatility. The model writes:

ln(ST/S0)=μgT+σTZ,Z∼𝒩(0,1). \ln(S_T/S_0)=\mu_gT+\sigma\sqrt T\,Z, \qquad Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1).

Here ZZ is a standard normal random variable. Substitute the model into the target condition, then subtract the mean and divide by the positive scale σT\sigma\sqrt T:

μgT+σTZ>gyT,Z>(gy−μg)TσT=0.02−0.060.20=−0.20. \begin{aligned} \mu_gT+\sigma\sqrt T\,Z&>g_yT,\\ Z&>\frac{(g_y-\mu_g)T}{\sigma\sqrt T} =\frac{0.02-0.06}{0.20}=-0.20. \end{aligned}

The normal cumulative distribution function Φ(z)\Phi(z) gives the probability to the left of zz. Our target uses the area to the right, so:

ℙ(ρT>0)=1−Φ(−0.20)≈0.5793. \mathbb P(\rho_T>0)=1-\Phi(-0.20)\approx0.5793.

We obtain a 57.9% model probability of beating the benchmark, and a 42.1% probability of finishing at or below it. This supplies a risk comparison for the specified trade. It concerns the sale date; exiting when a price first reaches a boundary is a different trading rule.

How much should we trust the answer? If mean growth were 0.02yr−10.02\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1} instead, the threshold for ZZ would be zero and the success probability would be 50%. Estimated inputs therefore matter. The model also assumes constant parameters and independent, normally distributed log-price increments. Clustering or heavier tails in the data can change the risk beyond what a new mean estimate captures.

To check the forecast, fit on earlier observations and save probabilities for later, fixed-horizon trades before their outcomes are known. Over many comparable cases, compare the forecast probabilities with the frequency of the same event. For a prediction interval at one date, we check whether the price at that date lies inside it. To check a band intended to contain an entire path, every observed price must remain inside its bounds; one crossing is enough to fail that check. The L5a out-of-sample example develops that distinction.

Try it. Keep the parameters above but require a 5% scaled NPV. What changes?
