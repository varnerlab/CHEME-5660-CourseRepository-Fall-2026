Which covariance does the single index model keep or omit?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

The SIM keeps covariance generated by shared market exposure. Its diagonal residual approximation omits cross-asset residual covariance, which can make estimated portfolio risk higher or lower than the sample-covariance result.

2 Worked explanation

The SIM covariance separates a common market contribution from asset-specific residual variances. Its diagonal residual matrix imposes zero cross-asset residual covariance. This is an additional assumption beyond fitting each asset by least squares.

How does the market model supply portfolio inputs? Assume residuals have zero mean, are uncorrelated with market growth, and are uncorrelated across assets. Taking means and covariances gives:

𝛍g=𝛂+𝛃μg,M,𝚺g=σg,M2𝛃𝛃⊤+𝐃g. \boldsymbol\mu_g=\boldsymbol\alpha+\boldsymbol\beta\mu_{g,M},\qquad \mathbf\Sigma_g=\sigma_{g,M}^2\boldsymbol\beta\boldsymbol\beta^{\top}+\mathbf D_g.

Substitute estimates from the same training observations to construct:

𝐠̂SIM=𝛂̂+𝛃̂gM′,𝚺̂g,SIM=sg,M2𝛃̂𝛃̂⊤+𝐃̂g. \hat{\mathbf g}_{\mathrm{SIM}}=\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha}+\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}g_M^{\prime},\qquad \hat{\mathbf\Sigma}_{g,\mathrm{SIM}}=s_{g,M}^2\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}^{\top}+\hat{\mathbf D}_g.

The diagonal contains both market and residual variance. Off-diagonal entries contain only the shared market contribution. Positive residual variances make the fitted covariance positive definite. These inputs replace the empirical ones in L6a’s allocation problem.

Which input changes when we use the SIM? With an intercept and the same training days, α̂i=gi′−β̂igM′\hat\alpha_i=g_i^{\prime}-\hat\beta_i g_M^{\prime}, so 𝐠̂SIM=𝐠′\hat{\mathbf g}_{\mathrm{SIM}}=\mathbf g^{\prime}. The reward inputs agree. The covariance changes because the model removes residual co-movement.

For different assets, the sample covariance splits exactly into:

Σ̂g,ij=β̂iβ̂jsg,M2⏟SIM keeps+Cov̂(𝐫i,𝐫j)⏟SIM omits,i≠j. \hat\Sigma_{g,ij} =\underbrace{\hat\beta_i\hat\beta_j s_{g,M}^2}_{\text{SIM keeps}} +\underbrace{\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(\mathbf r_i,\mathbf r_j)}_{\text{SIM omits}},\qquad i\ne j.

On the diagonal, the SIM divides residual sums of squares by N−2N-2, whereas the sample residual covariance uses N−1N-1. Thus, the SIM residual contribution is larger by the factor (N−1)/(N−2)(N-1)/(N-2), nearly one for thousands of observations.

Build a two-asset covariance. Suppose the fitted betas are (1,1.5)(1,1.5), the market growth variance is 4yr−24\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}, and fitted residual variances are (1,4)yr−2(1,4)\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}. Then:

𝚺̂g,SIM=4[11.51.52.25]+[1004]=[56613]yr−2. \hat{\mathbf\Sigma}_{g,\mathrm{SIM}} =4\begin{bmatrix}1&1.5\\1.5&2.25\end{bmatrix} +\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&4\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}5&6\\6&13\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}.

For fixed weights, portfolio beta is βp=𝐰⊤𝛃\beta_p=\mathbf w^{\top}\boldsymbol\beta. Multiplying the covariance by the weights on both sides gives the general risk decomposition:

Var(gp)=βp2σg,M2+∑iwi2σg,ε,i2. \operatorname{Var}(g_p)=\beta_p^2\sigma_{g,M}^2+\sum_i w_i^2\sigma_{g,\varepsilon,i}^2.

At equal weights, fitted portfolio beta is 0.5(1)+0.5(1.5)=1.250.5(1)+0.5(1.5)=1.25. Substituting the fitted inputs gives:

Var̂SIM(gp)=1.252(4)⏟6.25 market+0.52(1)+0.52(4)⏟1.25 residual=7.5yr−2. \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{\mathrm{SIM}}(g_p) =\underbrace{1.25^2(4)}_{6.25\text{ market}} +\underbrace{0.5^2(1)+0.5^2(4)}_{1.25\text{ residual}} =7.5\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}.

If residual covariance were 1yr−21\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}, keeping these diagonal entries fixed would add the cross term 2(0.5)(0.5)(1)=0.5yr−22(0.5)(0.5)(1)=0.5\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}. The variance would be 8yr−28\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}. Positive residual co-movement increases the risk of these positive-weight holdings.

Which risk can diversification reduce? With |𝒫|\lvert\mathcal P\rvert equally weighted assets and uncorrelated residuals of common variance vv, residual risk is v/|𝒫|v/\lvert\mathcal P\rvert. More generally, a common upper bound σ‾2\bar\sigma^2 gives a bound σ‾2/|𝒫|\bar\sigma^2/\lvert\mathcal P\rvert. Market risk remains βp2σg,M2\beta_p^2\sigma_{g,M}^2, determined by the portfolio beta.

How do the portfolio examples compare allocations? The risky-assets example scores both weight vectors under the sample covariance at common growth floors, then tests both on 2025 prices. The data-optimal weights have at most the sample-covariance risk of the SIM weights at the same floor. Using the same covariance separates the effect of choosing different holdings from the effect of measuring risk differently.
