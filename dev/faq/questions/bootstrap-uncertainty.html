How uncertain are the fitted alpha and beta?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

A standard error describes how much a coefficient estimate would vary across repeated datasets under an error model. A bootstrap makes that variation visible by constructing synthetic datasets and refitting the line to each one.

2 Worked explanation

How much might alpha and beta change with another dataset? The fitted line gives one alpha and one beta, and a new set of observations would move that line. A standard error describes the spread we expect in a fitted coefficient across repeated datasets under our error model. It is not the day-to-day spread of asset growth.

Where does the standard error come from? Conditional on the observed market growth rates, the classical calculation assumes zero-mean errors with constant variance and no correlation across days. With N>2N>2 observations and a full-column-rank design matrix, we estimate the error variance after fitting two coefficients:

sg,ε,i2=∥𝐫i∥22N−2,Cov̂(𝛉̂i)=sg,ε,i2(𝐗̂⊤𝐗̂)−1. s_{g,\varepsilon,i}^2=\frac{\lVert\mathbf r_i\rVert_2^2}{N-2},\qquad \widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_i) =s_{g,\varepsilon,i}^2(\hat{\mathbf X}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf X})^{-1}.

The square roots of the two diagonal entries give SE(α̂i)\operatorname{SE}(\hat\alpha_i) and SE(β̂i)\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_i). Alpha’s standard error has units of inverse years; beta’s is dimensionless.

Read an interval. With thousands of observations, the 95% interval is approximately the estimate plus or minus 1.96 standard errors. Suppose α̂i=0.02yr−1\hat\alpha_i=0.02\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1} with standard error 0.03yr−10.03\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}, and β̂i=1.20\hat\beta_i=1.20 with standard error 0.050.05. Then:

αi:0.02±1.96(0.03)=[−0.0388,0.0788]yr−1,βi:1.20±1.96(0.05)=[1.102,1.298]. \begin{aligned} \alpha_i:&\quad 0.02\pm1.96(0.03)=[-0.0388,0.0788]\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1},\\ \beta_i:&\quad 1.20\pm1.96(0.05)=[1.102,1.298]. \end{aligned}

The alpha interval includes zero, while the beta interval lies above one. Under the regression assumptions, and relative to the size of each estimate, these data determine the market exposure (beta) more precisely than alpha. The estimation example uses the Student-tt critical value, and the bootstrap comparison uses the large-sample Gaussian value. When the errors are also jointly Gaussian, the classical tt interval is exact.

What does the Gaussian bootstrap hold fixed? The uncertainty example keeps 𝐗̂\hat{\mathbf X} fixed, draws independent synthetic residuals from a fitted normal distribution, and adds them to the fitted growth:

𝐲(k)=𝐗̂𝛉̂i+𝛆(k). \mathbf y^{(k)}=\hat{\mathbf X}\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_i+\boldsymbol\varepsilon^{(k)}.

For one day, suppose the fitted growth is 0.14yr−10.14\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1} and the synthetic error draw is −0.20yr−1-0.20\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}. That day’s synthetic observation is then −0.06yr−1-0.06\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}. We repeat this addition for every day, keeping each day’s market observation, and fit a new line to the synthetic data. We then repeat the whole process for each of the K=1,000K=1{,}000 synthetic datasets. The standard deviation of the refitted alphas estimates alpha’s standard error, and likewise for beta. Because the synthetic datasets follow the fitted Gaussian model, the bootstrap should agree with the classical calculation, and that agreement serves as a check. The optional residual-diagnostics example examines heavy tails and dependence across days separately.

Try it. Does increasing KK from 1,000 to 4,000 make the original beta estimate twice as precise?

Show the answer
no. It improves the numerical precision of the bootstrap comparison. The original observations, and the information they carry about beta, are unchanged.
