Why does closing a credit trade early earn less, and why do traders do it anyway?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

Buying a short leg back costs its intrinsic value plus its remaining extrinsic value, so at any share price, closing early earns less than holding to expiration would at that same price. Traders close anyway because the extrinsic value left is all the trade can still earn, while the call side can still lose without limit.

2 Worked explanation

What does closing cost? To close a short leg at time tt, we buy it back at its current premium, its intrinsic value plus its extrinsic value EV(t)E_{V}(t). So if we close a credit trade at a share price SS:

profit if we close=credit grown to t⏟collected at entry−(intrinsic value+EV(t))⏟cost to buy the legs back. \text{profit if we close}=\underbrace{\text{credit grown to }t}_{\text{collected at entry}}-\underbrace{\left(\text{intrinsic value}+E_{V}(t)\right)}_{\text{cost to buy the legs back}}.

At expiration, EV=0E_{V}=0, and the profit at the same share price is the credit grown to TT minus the intrinsic value. The two differ by the extrinsic value still in the legs, plus a little interest on the credit. That is why, in the lecture’s figure, every curve before expiration lies below the profit at expiration.

The lecture’s strangle in numbers. A put at K1=90K_{1}=90 and a call at K2=110K_{2}=110 USD/share are sold 30 days before expiration, with S(0)=100S(0)=100 USD/share, σ=0.40yr−1/2\sigma=0.40\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}, and gy=0.04yr−1g_{y}=0.04\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}. Priced on the CRR tree as American contracts, they bring in 1.00 and 1.44 USD/share, a credit of 2.44 USD/share. Repricing the legs at later dates gives the cost to buy both legs back and the profit if we close. The last column is the profit if we hold to expiration instead and the share price ends at the same value (USD/share):

Days left Share price Cost to close Profit if closed If held
30 100 2.44 0.00 2.45
20 100 1.38 1.06 2.45
10 100 0.39 2.06 2.45
10 108 2.04 0.40 2.45

Both legs are out of the money in every row, so the whole cost to buy back is extrinsic value. To estimate it for a real trade, we read the current quotes for each leg, or reprice each leg on the CRR tree at the current share price, days left, and implied volatility, as here.

Why close before expiration? “Closing early earns less” compares the two at the same share price. The share price at expiration is still unknown, and closing trades the profit we have for the profit we might have.

  • What is left to earn: With 10 days left at S=100S=100, closing locks in 2.06 of the 2.45 USD/share possible. Holding can add at most the 0.39 USD/share of extrinsic value left.
  • What is left to lose: The call side still loses without limit above the upper breakeven, 110+2.45=112.45110+2.45=112.45 USD/share. Near expiration, a small move near a strike also changes the profit by more, because the curves bend toward the kinks at the strikes.
  • A move toward a strike makes this concrete: at S=108S=108 with 10 days left, the call has grown more expensive, and closing keeps only 0.40 USD/share.

This is the reasoning behind rules such as tastylive’s in the lecture: close at half the maximum profit or about 21 days before expiration. If the share price moves toward a short strike, we can close and take the loss, or defend the trade. One defense from L11a is to roll the untested side: buy back the leg the share price has moved away from, and sell a new one closer to the share price for more credit. Either way, we weigh the credit left to earn against what the trade can still lose.
