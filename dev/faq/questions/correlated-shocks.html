How do independent inputs produce correlated movements?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

The assets share random inputs through a loading matrix. Multiplying independent standard normal draws by a matrix A produces covariance AAᵀ, so the loadings determine how the assets move together.

2 Worked explanation

How can independent inputs produce correlated asset movements? Give the assets a shared random input. Suppose their volatility parameters are 0.20 and 0.30yr−1/20.30\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}, with correlation 0.50. The required covariance rate and a loading matrix that produces it are:

𝐂=[0.040.030.030.09]yr−1,𝐀=[0.2000.150.0675]yr−1/2. \mathbf C=\begin{bmatrix}0.04&0.03\\0.03&0.09\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{yr}^{-1}, \qquad \mathbf A=\begin{bmatrix}0.20&0\\0.15&\sqrt{0.0675}\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}.

The first diagonal loading is 0.04=0.20\sqrt{0.04}=0.20. The shared loading is 0.03/0.20=0.150.03/0.20=0.15; the remaining loading is 0.09−0.152=0.0675≈0.259808\sqrt{0.09-0.15^2}=\sqrt{0.0675}\approx0.259808. These choices make 𝐀𝐀⊤=𝐂\mathbf A\mathbf A^\top=\mathbf C.

Let Z1Z_1 and Z2Z_2 be independent standard normal variables. Write 𝐗=𝐀𝐙\mathbf X=\mathbf A\mathbf Z for the two noise contributions before time scaling. Expanding the matrix product gives:

X1=0.20Z1,X2=0.15Z1+0.0675Z2. X_1=0.20Z_1,\qquad X_2=0.15Z_1+\sqrt{0.0675}\,Z_2.

Both assets use Z1Z_1, which produces their covariance. Independence removes the cross term containing Z1Z2Z_1Z_2, and each input has variance one, so:

Var(X1)=0.202=0.04,Var(X2)=0.152+0.0675=0.09,Cov(X1,X2)=0.20(0.15)=0.03. \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(X_1)&=0.20^2=0.04,\\ \operatorname{Var}(X_2)&=0.15^2+0.0675=0.09,\\ \operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_2)&=0.20(0.15)=0.03. \end{aligned}

These values have units yr−1\mathrm{yr}^{-1}. Dividing the covariance by 0.20(0.30)0.20(0.30) recovers correlation 0.50. One draw illustrates the multiplication: if 𝐙=(1,−1)⊤\mathbf Z=(1,-1)^\top, then 𝐗≈(0.20,−0.109808)⊤yr−1/2\mathbf X\approx(0.20,-0.109808)^\top\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}. Positive correlation allows individual draws with opposite signs; it describes the joint distribution across draws.

How do these numbers enter a price step? For asset ii, add mean log growth and the time-scaled noise in the exponent:

Si(t+Δt)=Si(t)exp[μg,iΔt+ΔtXi],μg,i=μi−Cii/2. S_i(t+\Delta t)=S_i(t)\exp\!\left[\mu_{g,i}\Delta t+\sqrt{\Delta t}\,X_i\right], \qquad \mu_{g,i}=\mu_i-C_{ii}/2.

For Δt=1/252\Delta t=1/252 yr, starting prices of 100 USD/share, and both mean growth rates 0.06yr−10.06\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}, the draw above gives next prices about 101.29 and 99.33 USD/share. The same vector is shared across assets within the step; we draw a fresh vector at the next step.

Why use a loading matrix? For independent standard normal inputs, Cov(𝐀𝐙)=𝐀𝐀⊤\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf A\mathbf Z)=\mathbf A\mathbf A^\top. Multiplying by 𝐂\mathbf C itself would produce 𝐂𝐂⊤\mathbf C\mathbf C^\top, with the wrong covariance and units.

Try it. Replace the loading 0.15 by −0.15-0.15.

Show the answer
both variances stay the same because they use squared loadings. Covariance becomes −0.03yr−1-0.03\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1} and correlation becomes −0.50-0.50.
