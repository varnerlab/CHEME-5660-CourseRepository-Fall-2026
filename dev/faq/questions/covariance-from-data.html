How do observations become a covariance matrix?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

Center each asset’s growth-rate observations, multiply deviations from matching dates, and average with the sample denominator. The observation interval then connects growth-rate covariance to the covariance needed in a GBM step.

2 Worked explanation

How do observations become the covariance matrix? Suppose three aligned observations of two assets’ growth rates form the matrix below. Each row is one date and each column is one asset; every entry has units yr−1\mathrm{yr}^{-1}:

𝐆=[122133],𝐠′=[22],𝐆̃=[−100−111]. \mathbf G=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&1\\3&3\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf g^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\2\end{bmatrix},\qquad \tilde{\mathbf G}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\0&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}.

Average each column to obtain its mean, then subtract that mean from every observation in that column. This measures whether the assets move above or below their own means together.

Compute one entry at a time. The first asset’s deviations are (−1,0,1)(-1,0,1), so its sample variance is [(−1)2+02+12]/(3−1)=1yr−2[(-1)^2+0^2+1^2]/(3-1)=1\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}. For the covariance, multiply deviations on matching dates and add:

[(−1)(0)+(0)(−1)+(1)(1)]/2=0.5yr−2. [(-1)(0)+(0)(-1)+(1)(1)]/2=0.5\,\mathrm{yr}^{-2}.

The matrix product performs all these calculations at once:

𝚺̂g=𝐆̃⊤𝐆̃3−1=12[2112]=[10.50.51]yr−2. \hat{\mathbf\Sigma}_g =\frac{\tilde{\mathbf G}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf G}}{3-1} =\frac12\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}1&0.5\\0.5&1\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{yr}^{-2}.

The two variances are on the diagonal; their covariance appears in both off-diagonal positions. Their standard deviations are both one, so the correlation is 0.5/(1⋅1)=0.50.5/(1\cdot1)=0.5.

Which time conversion belongs in GBM? Growth rates divide log returns by the observation interval. For daily spacing Δt=1/252\Delta t=1/252 yr, the model’s covariance rate and the interval log-return covariance are:

𝐂̂=1252[10.50.51]yr−1,𝚺̂r=12522[10.50.51]. \hat{\mathbf C}=\frac1{252}\begin{bmatrix}1&0.5\\0.5&1\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{yr}^{-1},\qquad \hat{\mathbf\Sigma}_r=\frac1{252^2}\begin{bmatrix}1&0.5\\0.5&1\end{bmatrix}.

The GBM step uses Δt𝐂̂\Delta t\,\hat{\mathbf C} as its log-return covariance, which recovers 𝚺̂r\hat{\mathbf\Sigma}_r. This provides a direct check on the time factors. The growth-rate equation gives covariance 𝐂/Δt\mathbf C/\Delta t, so 𝐂̂=Δt𝚺̂g\hat{\mathbf C}=\Delta t\,\hat{\mathbf\Sigma}_g.

Try it. Add 5yr−15\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1} to every first-asset observation.

Show the answer
its mean rises from 2 to 7, but its centered deviations remain (−1,0,1)(-1,0,1). Every covariance entry stays the same. Adding a constant changes the level, not the variability about that level.

These three observations make the arithmetic visible; a useful empirical estimate requires enough aligned data to describe the assets under study. L6a uses the resulting means and covariance to compare allocations.
