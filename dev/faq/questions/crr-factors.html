Where do the CRR up and down factors come from?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

We match the mean and variance of one lattice step to geometric Brownian motion. For a small time step, the size of a move depends only on the volatility, so the same up and down factors serve forecasting and pricing.

2 Worked explanation

What must one step of the tree reproduce? The CRR model makes a down move undo an up move, ud=1ud=1. With a=lnua=\ln u, the growth rate over one step of length Δt\Delta t is +a/Δt+a/\Delta t with the real-world probability pp and −a/Δt-a/\Delta t with probability 1−p1-p. Under geometric Brownian motion, the growth rate over Δt\Delta t has mean μg\mu_{g} and variance σ2/Δt\sigma^{2}/\Delta t. Matching the two moments gives (L9b):

(2p−1)aΔt=μg,4p(1−p)a2Δt2=σ2Δt⇒a2=σ2Δt+μg2Δt2. \frac{(2p-1)\,a}{\Delta t}=\mu_{g}, \qquad \frac{4p(1-p)\,a^{2}}{\Delta t^{2}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\Delta t} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a^{2}=\sigma^{2}\Delta t+\mu_{g}^{2}\Delta t^{2}.

When can we drop the second term? The second term equals the first times (μg/σ)2Δt(\mu_{g}/\sigma)^{2}\Delta t. Take σ=0.30yr−1/2\sigma=0.30\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2} and μg=0.10yr−1\mu_{g}=0.10\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}. With daily steps, Δt=1/365\Delta t=1/365 year, the ratio is 0.0003, and keeping the term would change aa by about 0.015%. With a single one-year step, the ratio is 0.11, and aa would change by 5.4%. Lattice pricing uses many small steps, so Cox, Ross, and Rubinstein keep only the first term, a=σΔta=\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}:

u=eσΔt,d=e−σΔt. u=e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}},\qquad d=e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}.

For the daily step, u=1.01583u=1.01583 and d=0.98442d=0.98442.

Why match real-world moments when we price with q? After the second term is dropped, aa depends on neither μg\mu_{g} nor pp: the size of a move is set by the volatility alone, and the volatility is the same under ℙ\mathbb{P} and ℚ\mathbb{Q} (L9b). One tree therefore serves both questions, and only the up probability changes. For forecasting, the mean condition gives p=12(1+(μg/σ)Δt)=0.5087p=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1+(\mu_{g}/\sigma)\sqrt{\Delta t}\bigr)=0.5087 for the daily step. For pricing, q=(egyΔt−d)/(u−d)q=(e^{g_{y}\Delta t}-d)/(u-d), which is 0.4996 with gy=0.04yr−1g_{y}=0.04\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}. For a small step, q≈12+gy−σ2/22σΔtq\approx\tfrac{1}{2}+\frac{g_{y}-\sigma^{2}/2}{2\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}, the formula for pp with gy−σ2/2g_{y}-\sigma^{2}/2 in place of μg\mu_{g}. The advanced notebook shows that with qq the tree’s mean and variance approach those of geometric Brownian motion under ℚ\mathbb{Q} as the step shrinks.

Does q stay between zero and one? We need d<egyΔt<ud<e^{g_{y}\Delta t}<u, which for the CRR factors becomes |gy|Δt<σ\lvert g_{y}\rvert\sqrt{\Delta t}<\sigma. With daily steps and gy=0.04yr−1g_{y}=0.04\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}, the volatility must exceed 0.041/365=0.0021yr−1/20.04\sqrt{1/365}=0.0021\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}, far below the volatilities in an options chain. The L9b pricing example checks this floor for every contract.

Try it. Halve the volatility to σ=0.15yr−1/2\sigma=0.15\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}, with daily steps and the same μg\mu_{g}. What happens to uu and to the dropped term?
