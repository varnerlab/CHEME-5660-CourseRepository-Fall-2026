Why is a call's Delta close to, but larger than, its probability of exercise?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

Both add up the same in-the-money outcomes at expiration. The probability of exercise under Q weights each outcome by its chance of happening. Delta also weights it by how much the share grew to get there, discounted to today, so outcomes with more up moves count for more. That makes a call’s Delta a little larger, and the gap grows with the volatility times the square root of the time to expiration.

2 Worked explanation

Where does the extra factor come from? On a CRR tree with hh steps, the premium of a call on a share with no dividends is a discounted, probability-weighted sum over the terminal nodes (L10a). Delta is the slope of the premium with respect to today’s share price S0S_{0}. The tree’s uu, dd, and qq do not depend on S0S_{0}, so only the payoffs move. An in-the-money payoff S0ukdh−k−KS_{0}u^{k}d^{h-k}-K rises by ukdh−ku^{k}d^{h-k} for each USD/share added to S0S_{0}, and ukdh−k=ST/S0u^{k}d^{h-k}=S_{T}/S_{0} is the factor by which the share grew to reach node kk. Out-of-the-money payoffs stay at zero. So:

Δc=𝒟T,0−1(gy)∑k∈ITM(hk)qk(1−q)h−k⏟ℚ probability of node kukdh−k⏟ST/S0ℚ(ST>K)=∑k∈ITM(hk)qk(1−q)h−k. \begin{aligned} \Delta_{c}&=\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{T,0}(g_{y})\sum_{k\,\in\,\text{ITM}}\underbrace{\binom{h}{k}q^{k}(1-q)^{h-k}}_{\mathbb{Q}\text{ probability of node }k}\;\underbrace{u^{k}d^{h-k}}_{S_{T}/S_{0}}\\ \mathbb{Q}\left(S_{T}>K\right)&=\sum_{k\,\in\,\text{ITM}}\binom{h}{k}q^{k}(1-q)^{h-k}. \end{aligned}

The probability of exercise (POE) under ℚ\mathbb{Q} counts each in-the-money node by its probability. Delta also weights it by how much the share grew to get there, discounted to today. To get the lecture’s proposition, split the discount factor e−gyhΔte^{-g_{y}h\Delta t} into one factor e−gyΔte^{-g_{y}\Delta t} per step. Each up step then carries the weight q*=que−gyΔtq^{*}=q\,u\,e^{-g_{y}\Delta t}, and each down step the weight (1−q)de−gyΔt=1−q*(1-q)\,d\,e^{-g_{y}\Delta t}=1-q^{*}. The two weights add to one because, under ℚ\mathbb{Q}, the expected share price grows at the risk-free rate. So Delta is itself a probability of finishing in the money, computed with the up probability q*q^{*}.

Why is Delta larger? The tree only has q<1q<1 when u>egyΔtu>e^{g_{y}\Delta t}, so q*=que−gyΔt>qq^{*}=q\,u\,e^{-g_{y}\Delta t}>q. A larger up probability moves weight toward the nodes with the most up moves. For a call, those are exactly the in-the-money nodes, so their total weight rises, and Δc>ℚ(ST>K)\Delta_{c}>\mathbb{Q}(S_{T}>K).

A two-step example. Take S0=100S_{0}=100 USD/share, K=95K=95 USD/share, T=0.5T=0.5 year, h=2h=2 steps of Δt=0.25\Delta t=0.25 year, σ=0.30yr−1/2\sigma=0.30\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}, and gy=0.04yr−1g_{y}=0.04\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}. Then u=e0.15=1.1618u=e^{0.15}=1.1618, d=0.8607d=0.8607, q=0.4959q=0.4959, and q*=que−0.01=0.5705q^{*}=q\,u\,e^{-0.01}=0.5705. The terminal share prices are 134.99, 100.00, and 74.08 USD/share, so the two top nodes are in the money, and each probability is one minus the chance of two down moves:

ℚ(ST>K)=1−(1−q)2=1−0.50412=0.7459,Δc=1−(1−q*)2=1−0.42952=0.8155. \begin{aligned} \mathbb{Q}\left(S_{T}>K\right)&=1-(1-q)^{2}=1-0.5041^{2}=0.7459,\\ \Delta_{c}&=1-\left(1-q^{*}\right)^{2}=1-0.4295^{2}=0.8155. \end{aligned}

Repricing the tree at S0=100.01S_{0}=100.01 and 99.9999.99 USD/share and taking the central difference gives the same Delta, 0.8155. Switching from qq to q*q^{*} raises the top node’s weight from 0.2460 to 0.3254 and lowers the bottom node’s from 0.2541 to 0.1845, so the in-the-money total rises.

How far apart can they be? As the tree steps shrink, Delta becomes N(d+)N(d_{+}) and the POE under ℚ\mathbb{Q} becomes N(d−)N(d_{-}), and d+−d−=σTd_{+}-d_{-}=\sigma\sqrt{T}. For an at-the-money call with S0=K=100S_{0}=K=100 USD/share and gy=0.04yr−1g_{y}=0.04\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}:

Contract σT\sigma\sqrt{T} Delta, N(d+)N(d_{+}) POE under ℚ\mathbb{Q}, N(d−)N(d_{-}) Gap
30 days, σ=0.30\sigma=0.30 0.086 0.532 0.498 0.034
1 year, σ=0.50\sigma=0.50 0.500 0.629 0.433 0.197

So Delta is a good estimate of the POE under ℚ\mathbb{Q} for short contracts on calm shares, and a poor one for long contracts or volatile shares. Both numbers come from the pricing rule ℚ\mathbb{Q}; neither is our forecast. Which probability should we use? compares them with a forecast under ℙ\mathbb{P}.
