What does the net Delta of a covered call or collar tell us?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

Net Delta is the number of shares the whole position behaves like for a small move in the share price. A covered call behaves like fewer shares than it holds, and a collar like fewer still. Near expiration, a collar behaves like the shares between its strikes and like no shares at all beyond them.

2 Worked explanation

What does the number mean? For nn contracts on m=100m=100 shares each, L11b gives:

Δnet=mn(1−Δc)(covered call)Δnet=mn(1+Δp−Δc)(collar) \begin{aligned} \Delta_{\text{net}}&=mn\left(1-\Delta_{c}\right)&&\text{(covered call)}\\ \Delta_{\text{net}}&=mn\left(1+\Delta_{p}-\Delta_{c}\right)&&\text{(collar)} \end{aligned}

Its unit is shares: for a small move of ΔS\Delta S USD/share, the position gains or loses about Δnet⋅ΔS\Delta_{\text{net}}\cdot\Delta S USD, as if it held Δnet\Delta_{\text{net}} shares. The shares contribute mnmn, the short call −mnΔc-mn\,\Delta_{c}, and the long put mnΔpmn\,\Delta_{p}. Because 0≤Δc≤10\leq\Delta_{c}\leq1 and −1≤Δp≤0-1\leq\Delta_{p}\leq0, both options lower the net Delta: the short call gives up part of each rise, and the long put gains when the shares fall.

One collar at entry. Take the 95/110 collar from the collar strikes answer, one contract on 100 shares at S=100S=100 USD/share with three months left, σ=0.25yr−1/2\sigma=0.25\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}, and BSM Deltas Δc=0.268\Delta_{c}=0.268 and Δp=−0.290\Delta_{p}=-0.290:

Δnet=100(1−0.268)=73.2(covered call)Δnet=100(1−0.290−0.268)=44.2(collar) \begin{aligned} \Delta_{\text{net}}&=100\,(1-0.268)=73.2&&\text{(covered call)}\\ \Delta_{\text{net}}&=100\,(1-0.290-0.268)=44.2&&\text{(collar)} \end{aligned}

If the share price rises 2 USD/share, the 100 shares alone gain 200 USD, the covered call about 146 USD, and the collar about 88 USD.

How does it change with the share price and time? Recomputing the BSM Deltas at other share prices gives the net Delta (shares):

Share price 85 95 100 105 110 120
Covered call, 3 months left 97.3 84.9 73.2 59.1 44.3 20.1
Collar, 3 months left 20.0 40.5 44.2 41.8 34.9 17.9
Covered call, 5 days left 100.0 100.0 99.9 94.0 48.7 0.1
Collar, 5 days left 0.0 51.3 96.2 94.0 48.7 0.1

Near expiration, between the strikes, both options are almost worthless and barely move, so the collar behaves like the 100 shares. Beyond a strike, the deep in-the-money option cancels the shares: below 95 USD/share, the put gains what the shares lose, and above 110 USD/share, the call loses what the shares gain. The value is then locked at the floor or the cap, and the net Delta is close to zero. At a strike, the net Delta swings quickly between these values; Gamma measures how fast.

Is this Delta a probability? Not for a position. A single call’s Delta is close to, and slightly above, its probability of exercise under ℚ\mathbb{Q} (why); for a put the match is rougher. Each option’s Delta is also its sensitivity to the share price. The net Delta adds shares and option Deltas, so only the sensitivity meaning carries over: 44.2 is a number of shares, not a probability.

Does a net Delta of zero mean no risk? No. Delta is the slope of the position’s value at today’s share price. Gamma changes it as the share price moves, and Theta, Vega, and Rho change the value through time, volatility, and the rate (L10a). For a large move, we reprice the legs instead of relying on Delta.

Try it. With 5 days left at S=105S=105 USD/share, why do the covered call and the collar have the same net Delta, 94.0?

Show the answer
The put at 95 USD/share is far out of the money, so Δp≈0\Delta_{p}\approx0, and the collar behaves like the covered call. Its call at 110 USD/share still has a small Delta, 0.06.
