When Delta, the risk-neutral POE, and our forecast disagree, which should we use?

  1. Short answer
  2. Worked explanation
  3. Notes and sources

1 Short answer

They answer different questions. Delta and the probabilities under Q describe how the market prices the option. Our forecast under P describes what we think will happen. We decide with our forecast, but it is only as good as our estimate of the drift, the input we know least well.

2 Worked explanation

Three numbers for one call. In the L10a example, we sell an NVDA call with strike K=235K=235 USD/share, 43 days to expiration, a premium of 9.88 USD/share, and an implied volatility of 0.3543. Our forecast ℙ\mathbb{P} is geometric Brownian motion with the historical drift μ̂=0.5788yr−1\hat{\mu}=0.5788\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1} and volatility σ̂=0.3886yr−1/2\hat{\sigma}=0.3886\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}. The risk-neutral ℚ\mathbb{Q} uses the drift gy=0.0413yr−1g_{y}=0.0413\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1} and the implied volatility:

Delta (quoted) POE under ℚ\mathbb{Q} POE under ℙ\mathbb{P}
Probability the call is exercised 0.4833 0.4295 0.6185
Seller’s POP 0.6976 0.5034

What does each number answer?

  • Delta is the hedge ratio the trading platform computes under ℚ\mathbb{Q}. Read as a probability, it sits slightly above the POE under ℚ\mathbb{Q} (why Delta is larger). It is not a worst case for a seller: here our forecast puts the chance of exercise at 0.62, well above Delta.
  • The probabilities under ℚ\mathbb{Q} are the ones the market price is built on. Under ℚ\mathbb{Q}, buying or selling at the market price has an expected NPV of about zero (why we price with q). So the seller’s POP of 0.70 under ℚ\mathbb{Q} is not, by itself, a reason to sell. The seller wins a small amount often and loses a large amount now and then, and under ℚ\mathbb{Q} the two balance. “Risk-neutral” names this pricing rule; it does not say that the trade has no risk.
  • The probabilities under ℙ\mathbb{P} are our own forecast, and we decide with them. Under ℚ\mathbb{Q}, a trade at the market price is worth about zero, so a trade can only look good to us when our forecast differs from ℚ\mathbb{Q}. Be careful about why it differs. ℚ\mathbb{Q} is a pricing rule, not the market’s forecast. If we expect a share to grow faster than the risk-free rate, bullish trades on it will look good under ℙ\mathbb{P}. Part of that advantage is just the extra growth we expect for holding a risky share; it does not mean the option is mispriced. Here our forecast says the call is more likely to be exercised than the market implies, so selling it looks worse to us, not better.

How much should we trust our forecast? In the L11a example, a put credit spread on AMD has a POP of 0.714 under our forecast and 0.540 under ℚ\mathbb{Q}. The gap comes mostly from the drift: our estimate of AMD’s mean growth rate is μ̂g=0.44yr−1\hat{\mu}_{g}=0.44\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}. The drift is the hardest parameter to estimate. For geometric Brownian motion, the L4b drift-uncertainty notebook shows that a mean growth rate fitted to TspanT_{\text{span}} years of prices has a standard error of about 6/5σ̂/Tspan\sqrt{6/5}\,\hat{\sigma}/\sqrt{T_{\text{span}}}. With ten years of AMD’s σ̂=0.47\hat{\sigma}=0.47, that is about 0.16yr−10.16\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}. The much narrower bounds printed with the AMD parameters assume that the errors around the fitted line are unrelated to one another, which is not true for prices that follow geometric Brownian motion. Keeping σ̂\hat{\sigma} fixed and lowering the growth rate shows how much of the advantage depends on it:

Mean growth rate μg\mu_{g} (yr−1\mathrm{yr}^{-1}) 0.44 0.20 0.00
POP under ℙ\mathbb{P} 0.714 0.646 0.584

Even with no growth, the POP stays above 0.540, mostly because the mean growth rate under ℚ\mathbb{Q} is gy−σ2/2=−0.12yr−1g_{y}-\sigma^{2}/2=-0.12\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}, below zero. The wider implied volatility, 0.56 against our 0.47, accounts for only about 0.01. So most of the gap rests on the drift.

A practical reading. Compute the probabilities under both. Where they agree, our model and the market see the trade the same way. Where they disagree, find which input causes the gap, the drift or the volatility, and recompute with a more cautious value. If the trade only looks good with the most favorable drift, it rests on a number we cannot estimate precisely.

Try it. Set our forecast’s drift to gyg_{y} and its volatility to the implied volatility. What POP do we get for the AMD spread, and what does that say about the trade?

Show the answer
The POP under ℚ\mathbb{Q}, 0.540. Our forecast then matches ℚ\mathbb{Q}, so the trade has an expected NPV of about zero for us, and we have no reason to prefer it.
