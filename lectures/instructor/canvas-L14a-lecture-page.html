L14a: A General Factor Model and Paper Trading

In this lecture, we finish building synthetic markets of many stocks. Then we take the first step from a synthetic market to the real one: we send orders for a basket of stocks to a paper trading account.

The two halves answer one question: how do we test a trading method before it risks real money? A synthetic market lets us test the method on many possible market paths. A paper account lets us run it against real market prices with simulated money.

Lecture Videos

  1. Part 1: Introduction, learning objectives, and a review of jump hidden Markov models. We start with the plan for the lecture and its three learning objectives. We then review how a jump hidden Markov model simulates the daily growth rate of one asset. Finally, we review how the sector factor model extends it to many stocks that move together.
  2. Part 2: Build a synthetic market of many stocks. Next, we put the sector factor model to work on real data. We build a universe of SPY and 375 stocks from all 11 sectors, and compare the correlations, volatilities, and market sensitivities of a synthetic path with the real data. We also find what the model misses: in the real data, the sector factors of some pairs of sectors move together.
  3. Part 3: The general factor model and Problem Set 6. The general factor model closes that gap. It adds economic factors, such as the daily change in the 10-year Treasury yield, and keeps the covariance between pairs of sectors. We walk through its fitting and sampling algorithms, including the Cholesky step, and then introduce Problem Set 6.
  4. Part 4: Company profile: Alpaca, and an introduction to the MCP server. With a way to test a trading method on synthetic markets, we turn to placing orders at real market prices. Alpaca, a brokerage built for programs that trade through an application programming interface (API), gives every user a free paper trading account. We define a paper account and review market and limit orders. Our AI assistant talks to Alpaca through a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server, which gives the assistant one tool for each request, such as reading quotes or submitting an order.
  5. Part 5: Paper trading demo. Now we place trades. We ask the assistant whether the market is open and how much the account can spend, read the quotes of a basket of stocks, and place a limit order for each stock. Because a limit order may not fill, we follow each order until it fills or we cancel it.
  6. Part 6: Next time, summary, and key takeaways. First, we preview the next lecture. Every estimate we used was fit once on the 2014 to 2024 data, but a stock's market sensitivity can drift over time. We ask how a trading method should respond to that drift. We then close with a summary and the three key takeaways.

What You Need to Know

  • Checking a synthetic market: A synthetic market built from the sector factor model reproduces the sector blocks of the real correlation matrix and each stock's market sensitivity. It links stocks in different sectors only through the market.
  • A general factor model: A general factor model explains each stock's daily growth rate with the market, economic factors, sector factors, and a stock-specific part. The Cholesky step turns the correlated factors into uncorrelated series, so that independent jump hidden Markov models can draw them. Mixing the draws back restores the covariance between the factors.
  • Paper trading: A paper account trades simulated money at real market prices, including the impact of some market friction. For example, a limit order may not fill. A trading method must check the status of each order before its next decision.

You do not need to memorize the least-squares steps of the fitting algorithm, what each column means after the Cholesky step, or how the MCP server turns a request into calls to Alpaca's API. They are there to help you understand the material.
