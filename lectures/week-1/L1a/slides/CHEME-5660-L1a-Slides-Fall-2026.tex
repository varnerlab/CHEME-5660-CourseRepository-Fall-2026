\begin{frame}{Most of You Already Hold a Portfolio}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  Roughly \hilite{61\%} of U.S. adults hold a retirement account: a 401(k), a
  403(b), or an IRA. For U.S. households, financial markets are the second
  largest component of wealth, after real estate. \dimmed{(2025)}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  If you hold one of those accounts, you are already a market participant.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
    \item That account holds a \hilite{portfolio}: a mix of investments, with
      some percentage in each, chosen by someone.
    \item Choosing that mix is an \hilite{investment decision}. It is
      the decision this course spends four units learning how to make.
    \item Most account holders have \hilite{never made that decision
      explicitly}. A default mix was selected for them and they left it
      in place.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \ink{Leaving the default mix in place is still an investment decision.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Price Paid Versus Value Controlled}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  The options row is not like the other two. The \hilite{USD 37B} is the price
  paid for the options. That price, called the \hilite{premium}, is not the
  total value of the shares those options control.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Each contract is an option on \textbf{100 shares}. At 60.4 million contracts a
  day, the shares underneath are worth about \hilite{USD 4 trillion}:
  \[
    \underbrace{\text{USD } 37\text{B}}_{\text{\footnotesize price paid (premium)}}
    \;\Big/\;
    \underbrace{\text{USD } 4{,}000\text{B}}_{\text{\footnotesize value controlled}}
    \;=\;
    \underbrace{0.92\%}_{\text{\footnotesize about 92 cents per \$100}}
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  If every contract were exercised on the day it traded, that USD 4 trillion of
  stock would change hands, which is more than \hilite{twice} the debt and
  stock rows combined. \dimmed{The previous table reports the price paid
  instead, because that is the cash that actually moves.}

  \slidenote{Careful:}{$3.9\text{M} \times 100 \times 7{,}692 = \$3.0$T of that is one index, settled in cash. It never delivers a share.}
\end{frame}

  The investment choices in this course fall into three broad groups. We name

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{center}\small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \textbf{\ink{Kind}} & \textbf{\ink{What you own}} & \textbf{\ink{Where the course takes it apart}}\\[2pt]
    \hline\\[-7pt]
    \hilite{Debt}        & a promise of payments on stated dates       & Unit 1, weeks 2 and 3\\

  \vspace{0.8em}

  derivative depends on another asset, so its possible outcomes vary most widely.

  \slidenote{Today:}{we use only the first kind, and we do not use its name until later.}

\begin{frame}{The Decision Problem}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  Every investment decision follows the same pattern: you observe the market,
  choose what to do, and see what happens. In a decision model, these are called
  the \hilite{state}, \hilite{action}, and \hilite{reward}.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[>=Latex,font=\sffamily\small,node distance=3.0cm]
    \node[draw=vnink,very thick,rounded corners,fill=vnsoft,

    \node[draw=vnink,very thick,rounded corners,fill=white,
          minimum width=3.6cm,minimum height=1.05cm,align=center,right=of you] (mkt) {the market\\uncertain};
    \draw[vnlink,very thick,->] (you) -- node[above] {choice (action)} (mkt);

  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  To use this model, we must compare payments over time, describe uncertainty,
  find values that depend on others, and choose. These are the four units.

  \slidenote{Careful:}{each arrow in the diagram is a modeling choice.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Validation Ladder}
  \vspace{0.05em}
  Four ways to test an idea. Each stage uses evidence the one below did not see,
  and each can reveal a failure that the earlier stages cannot.

  \vspace{0.45em}
  \begin{center}
    \resizebox{0.97\textwidth}{!}{\input{../figs/validation-ladder.tex}}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.45em}
  A \hilite{backtest} runs on past data. A \hilite{forward test} runs on actual
  current data with no money at stake, using current market data from a service
  such as Alpaca.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \ink{Skipping a stage means discovering a failure later, perhaps with real money at stake.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three Questions We Cannot Answer Yet}
  \vspace{0.15em}
  Most hands went to \textbf{C} or \textbf{D}, which is reasonable given the
  information on the previous slide. That slide left out three things.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item \hilite{Why not C?} It pays the most of the three government rows, but
      you may need the money before year thirty. If you sell early, you sell at
      whatever price interest rates at the time allow. \dimmed{Week 2.}
    \item \hilite{Why not D?} It pays more than any other row. Those borrowers
      pay \hilite{0.71 percentage points} more than the government over the
      same ten years \dimmed{($5.42\% - 4.71\%$)},
      which is what the market charges for the risk of not being paid and for
      holding something harder to sell. \dimmed{Units 2 and 3.}
    \item \hilite{Why not E?} It pays the least of any row, but that comparison
      is not valid: its rate is quoted in different units. \dimmed{Next slide.}
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.35em}
  \ink{Only A, B, and C promise a schedule that is actually certain.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Breakeven Inflation}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  B and E both come from the U.S. government and span ten years. B promises a
  fixed number of dollars, called \hilite{nominal} dollars. E's payments rise
  with inflation, so they are measured in \hilite{real} dollars. The difference
  between their rates is:
  \[
    \underbrace{4.71\%}_{\text{\footnotesize nominal}}
    \;-\;
    \underbrace{2.41\%}_{\text{\footnotesize real}}
    \;=\;
    \underbrace{2.30\%}_{\text{\footnotesize breakeven inflation}}
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  That is the ten-year \hilite{breakeven inflation rate}: the inflation rate at
  which B and E pay you the same amount. The Federal Reserve publishes this rate
  directly, and its value for the same day is \hilite{2.30\%}. It is not a pure
  forecast, because it also reflects how much investors must be paid to accept
  uncertainty about inflation.

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \ink{Market prices contain forecasts. Learning to extract them is a major goal of this course.}

  \slidenote{Source:}{FRED series \texttt{DGS10}, \texttt{DFII10}, \texttt{T10YIE}, as of 2026-08-18}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why You Cannot Rank the Five Yet}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  Every promise on that list comes from the United States government or from
  large companies with long records of paying what they owe. No vote in this
  room was careless.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  You cannot rank those five yet, and the reason is precise:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}
    {\large\ink{You have no rule for comparing dollars that arrive on different dates.}}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \$264 in thirty years and \$10{,}028 in four weeks cannot be compared until we
  state what a dollar on one date is worth on another. We build that rule on
  Thursday, and it is the first thing this course does.
\end{frame}

      different kind of failure.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.3em}
