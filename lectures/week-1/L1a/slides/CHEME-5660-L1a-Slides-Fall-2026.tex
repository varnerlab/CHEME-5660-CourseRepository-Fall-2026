\begin{frame}{You Already Hold a Portfolio}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  Roughly \hilite{61\%} of U.S. adults hold a retirement account: a
  401(k), a 403(b), or an IRA. In household wealth generation, markets are
  second only to real estate. \dimmed{(2025)}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  So the question is not whether to participate. You already do.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
    \item That account holds a \hilite{portfolio}: a set of positions, in some
      proportion, chosen by someone.
    \item Choosing those proportions is an \hilite{allocation decision}, and it
      is the same decision this course spends four units learning to make.
    \item Most people holding one have \hilite{never consciously made it}. A
      default was selected for them and they left it alone.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \ink{Inaction is an allocation. It is just not an argued one.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What That Last Row Controls}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  One row behaves unlike the others. That \hilite{USD 37B} of premium is the
  cash paid, but it is not the size of the position it opens.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Each contract is an option on \textbf{100 shares}. At 60.4 million contracts a
  day, the shares underneath are worth about \hilite{USD 4 trillion}:
  \[
    \underbrace{\text{USD } 37\text{B}}_{\text{\footnotesize premium paid}}
    \;\Big/\;
    \underbrace{\text{USD } 4{,}000\text{B}}_{\text{\footnotesize value controlled}}
    \;=\;
    \underbrace{0.92\%}_{\text{\footnotesize about 92 cents per \$100}}
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  Assume every contract were exercised the day it traded, and that USD 4 trillion
  of stock changes hands: more than \hilite{twice} the debt and equity rows
  combined. \dimmed{Which is why it is not in the table above.}

  \slidenote{Careful:}{$3.9\text{M} \times 100 \times 7{,}692 = \$3.0$T of that is one index, settled in cash. It never delivers a share.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Decision Problem}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  Three kinds of asset, one question. You observe something, you do something,
  and something comes back. The three words for those are \hilite{state},
  \hilite{action}, and \hilite{reward}.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[>=Latex,font=\sffamily\small,node distance=3.0cm]
    \node[draw=vnink,very thick,rounded corners,fill=vnsoft,
          minimum width=3.0cm,minimum height=1.05cm,align=center] (you) {you\\preferences};
    \node[draw=vnink,very thick,rounded corners,fill=white,
          minimum width=3.6cm,minimum height=1.05cm,align=center,right=of you] (mkt) {the market\\uncertain};
    \draw[vnlink,very thick,->] (you) -- node[above] {action} (mkt);
    \draw[vncarnelian,very thick,->,bend left=26] (mkt) to node[below,align=center] {state, reward} (you);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  The loop is easy to draw and hard to close. Closing it needs a way to compare
  payments that arrive on different dates, a way to describe what you do not
  know, a way to price a claim that depends on something else, and a rule for
  choosing. Those are the four units.

  \slidenote{Careful:}{the picture is not the problem. Every arrow in it is a modeling choice.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Validation Ladder}
  \vspace{0.05em}
  Four ways to check a claim. Each rung sees data the one below it did not, and
  each is the \hilite{only} rung able to kill its own claim.

  \vspace{0.45em}
  \begin{center}
    \resizebox{0.97\textwidth}{!}{\input{../figs/validation-ladder.tex}}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.45em}
  A \hilite{backtest} runs on past data. A \hilite{forward test} runs on actual
  current data with no money at stake, which a brokerage API such as Alpaca
  makes possible.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \ink{Skipping a rung relocates the discovery to where it costs money.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three Questions We Cannot Answer Yet}
  \vspace{0.15em}
  Most hands went to \textbf{C} or \textbf{D}, and that is the right instinct
  with the information on the previous slide. Here is what it left out.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item \hilite{Why not C?} It pays the most of the three government rows. But
      you may need the money before year thirty, and then you sell at whatever
      price rates allow. \dimmed{Week 2.}
    \item \hilite{Why not D?} It pays the most, full stop. Those borrowers pay
      \hilite{82 basis points} (0.82 percentage points) more than the government
      for the same timing of money \dimmed{(FRED \texttt{BAMLC0A0CM}, 2026-08-18)}: the price of not being paid, and of owning
      something harder to sell. \dimmed{Units 2 and 3.}
    \item \hilite{Why not E?} It pays the least of any row, and that comparison
      is not even legal: its number is in different units. \dimmed{Next slide.}
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.35em}
  \ink{Every row is written as a certain schedule. Only three of them are.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Forecast by Subtraction}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  B and E are the same issuer at the same horizon. B promises \hilite{nominal}
  dollars; E promises \hilite{real} ones, adjusted for whatever inflation turns
  out to be. Subtracting one from the other leaves exactly the difference
  between those two units:
  \[
    \underbrace{4.71\%}_{\text{\footnotesize nominal}}
    \;-\;
    \underbrace{2.41\%}_{\text{\footnotesize real}}
    \;=\;
    \underbrace{2.30\%}_{\text{\footnotesize breakeven inflation}}
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  That is the ten-year \hilite{breakeven inflation rate}: the inflation rate at
  which B and E pay you the same. The Federal Reserve publishes it directly, and
  it prints \hilite{2.30\%} for the same day. It is not a clean forecast, since
  it also carries what investors charge for bearing inflation risk, but nothing
  else gets you this close in one subtraction.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \ink{Prices contain forecasts. Extracting them is most of what this course does.}

  \slidenote{Source:}{FRED series \texttt{DGS10}, \texttt{DFII10}, \texttt{T10YIE}, as of 2026-08-18}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}
  This lecture located the course, argued that you are already a market
  participant, and read five financial promises as schedules of dated payments.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{Key takeaways}}}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item \textbf{\ink{You are already a portfolio holder.}} Retirement accounts
      make market participation the default, so the allocation question is one
      nearly everyone answers, most of them by inaction.
    \item \textbf{\ink{An instrument is a schedule.}} A bill, a note, a bond, a
      corporate bond, and an inflation-indexed security are labels for schedules
      of dated payments, though not every schedule is equally certain.
    \item \textbf{\ink{A claim is only as good as the rung it survived.}} Theory,
      backtest, forward test, and real money each see different data, and each
      is the only stage able to falsify its own claim.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  On Thursday we build the rule that lets us rank those five schedules.
\end{frame}
