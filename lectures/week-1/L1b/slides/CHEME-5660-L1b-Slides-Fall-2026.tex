% ---- TVM -----------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Time Value of Money}
  A dollar today and a dollar at a future date are \hilite{not directly
  comparable}. Before we compare or add them, we must express both at the same
  valuation date using a stated conversion rule.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Let $P$ be an amount held at time $0$, and let $y$ be a quoted \hilite{nominal
  annual yield} used for valuation. With one compounding interval per year, the
  amount after one year is given by:
  \[
    F_1=\underbrace{(1+y)}_{\mathcal D_{1,0}(y;1)}P .
  \]

  For $y>0$ the factor $\mathcal D_{1,0}(y;1)>1$ moves time-0 dollars forward and
  is the \hilite{forward accumulation factor}; its inverse
  $\mathcal D_{1,0}^{-1}(y;1)<1$ moves future dollars back and is the
  \hilite{discount factor}.

  \slidenote{Careful:}{a later dollar is not \emph{automatically} worth less. That depends on the rate $y$ you declare.}
\end{frame}

% ---- math: accumulation --------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Discrete Compounding and Accumulation Factors}
  Let $j=0,1,2,\ldots$ index the \hilite{compounding periods}, and let
  $i_\ell>-1$ be the \hilite{effective rate} over the interval
  $\ell\rightarrow\ell+1$. An amount $P$ held at time $0$ becomes:
  \[
    F_j=\underbrace{\left[\prod_{\ell=0}^{j-1}(1+i_\ell)\right]}_{\mathcal D_{j,0}(\mathbf i)}P .
  \]

  For nominal annual yield $y$ compounded $n$ times per year, $i_\ell=y/n$ and
  the product becomes:
  \[
    \underbrace{\mathcal D_{j,0}(y;n)=\left(1+\frac{y}{n}\right)^{j}}_{\text{\footnotesize accumulation}},
    \qquad
    \underbrace{\mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n)=\left(1+\frac{y}{n}\right)^{-j}}_{\text{\footnotesize discount}}.
  \]

  \slidenote{Careful:}{a flat $y$ is an \emph{assumption}. In L2b, $y$ varies with the date.}
\end{frame}

% ---- math: continuous ----------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Continuous Compounding}
  Let $g(t)$ be a \hilite{continuously compounded growth rate}, carrying units of
  inverse time, and let $T$ be the horizon. The forward accumulation factor over
  $0\rightarrow T$ is given by:
  \[
    \mathcal D_{T,0}(g)=\exp\left(\int_0^T g(u)\,du\right),
  \]

  which for constant $g$ collapses to:
  \[
    \underbrace{\mathcal D_{T,0}(g)=e^{gT}}_{\text{\footnotesize accumulation}},
    \qquad
    \underbrace{\mathcal D_{T,0}^{-1}(g)=e^{-gT}}_{\text{\footnotesize discount}}.
  \]

  A nominal annual yield $y$ compounded $n$ times per year has the continuously
  compounded equivalent $g_y:=n\log\left(1+y/n\right)$, so
  $\left(1+y/n\right)^{nT}=e^{g_yT}$: these are \hilite{two representations of one
  accumulation rule}, not two different rules.

  \slidenote{Log return:}{the dimensionless product $r=(\Delta t)\,g$. L2a and L3a build on it.}
\end{frame}

% ---- who sets the rate ---------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Who Sets the Valuation Rate?}
  A valuation rate is \hilite{not universal}. It depends on the cash flow, market
  convention, horizon, and the risks being modeled: no single policy rate prices
  every asset.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \eyebrow{The Federal Reserve}
  \vspace{0.25em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
    \item Steers short-term rates through \hilite{interest on reserve balances}
      (IORB), supported by the overnight reverse repo facility; open market
      operations manage the supply of reserves.
    \item The \hilite{FOMC} sets a target range for the federal funds rate, the
      overnight rate on unsecured reserve balances traded between eligible
      institutions.
    \item Other market rates respond to policy and market conditions, but are
      \hilite{not mechanically pegged} to the federal funds rate.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Link:}{\href{https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm}{FOMC calendar and meeting materials}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Valuation Rate Should I Use?}
  There is no universal answer, and this lecture does not need one: here the
  yield $y$ is a \hilite{declared model input}. Choosing it well means matching
  the rate to the cash flow: same currency, same horizon, and an explicit
  statement of any adjustment made for credit, liquidity, or risk.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \eyebrow{Rate type}
      \vspace{0.25em}
      {\small A quoted \hilite{bond yield} is the rate that matches price to
      promised cash flows. An \hilite{equity required return} estimates future
      compensation for risk. They are different quantities.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \eyebrow{Risk type}
      \vspace{0.25em}
      {\small Only \hilite{systematic risk}, which diversification cannot remove,
      is expected to earn compensation. More diversifiable risk does not by
      itself increase expected return. Expected return is not realized return.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ---- net cash flows ------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Net Cash Flows}
  An abstract asset is a \hilite{sequence of dated cash flows} denominated in a
  currency. At cash-flow index $j$, let $\mathbf c_j\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{m}$
  collect $m$ nonnegative magnitudes and let
  $\symbf{\nu}_j\in\{-1,+1\}^{m}$ give their directions, where $+1$ denotes an
  inflow and $-1$ an outflow. The \hilite{signed net cash flow} at index $j$ is
  given by:
  \[
    \bar c_j=\left\langle\mathbf c_j,\symbf{\nu}_j\right\rangle
    =\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{j,i}\,\nu_{j,i}.
  \]

  Over the dates $j=0,1,\ldots,N$, the undiscounted total $\sum_{j=0}^{N}\bar c_j$
  is \hilite{not} a time-0 value: its terms are dollars from different dates.

  \slidenote{Next:}{discount every term to a common date, and the result is net present value.}
\end{frame}

% ---- math: NPV -----------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Net Present Value}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  Let $\bar c_j$ be the \hilite{signed net cash flow} on date $j$, positive for
  an inflow and negative for an outflow, over the dates $j=0,1,\ldots,N$. Discount
  each one to the valuation date with $\mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n)$, \hilite{then}
  add. The net present value at time $0$ is given by:
  \[
    \operatorname{NPV}_0(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n)\,\bar c_j
    =\underbrace{\left\langle \mathcal D_{\star,0}^{-1}(y;n),\ \bar{\mathbf c}\right\rangle}_{\text{\footnotesize time-0 dollars}} .
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  The $\star$ runs over every cash-flow index, so $\mathcal D_{\star,0}^{-1}(y;n)$
  and $\bar{\mathbf c}$ are ordered $(N+1)$-vectors and NPV is a single scalar
  product, which is exactly how we compute it in code.

  \slidenote{Worked example:}{ten years of Model S ownership, $\operatorname{NPV}_0=-\$121{,}484$}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}
  This lecture introduced time-value-of-money calculations and the
  representation of investments as dated cash flows.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \decklabel{Key takeaways}
  \vspace{0.25em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
    \item \textbf{\ink{Dated dollars must be compared at a common valuation date.}}
      A forward accumulation factor carries value forward and its inverse
      discounts it back, so cash flows can be added only once they share a date.
    \item \textbf{\ink{Rate conventions must be stated.}} A nominal yield quoted
      with a compounding frequency and its continuously compounded equivalent
      describe one accumulation rule, so the convention travels with the number.
    \item \textbf{\ink{NPV values an asset relative to a declared rate.}} The sign
      of the result is a statement about the cash-flow model and the discount
      rate you declared.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  These ideas provide the valuation framework used throughout the course.
\end{frame}
