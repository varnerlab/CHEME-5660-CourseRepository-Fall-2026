% ---- TVM -----------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Time Value of Money}
  A dollar today and a dollar at a future date are \hilite{not directly
  comparable}. Before we compare or add them, we must express both at the same
  valuation date using a stated conversion rule.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Let $P$ be an amount held at time $0$, and let $y$ be a quoted
  \textbf{\ink{nominal annual yield}} used for valuation. With one compounding
  interval per year, the amount after one year is given by:
  \[
    F_1=\underbrace{(1+y)}_{\mathcal D_{1,0}(y;1)}P .
  \]

  For $y>0$ the factor $\mathcal D_{1,0}(y;1)>1$ moves time-0 dollars forward and
  is the \textbf{\ink{forward accumulation factor}}; its inverse
  $\mathcal D_{1,0}^{-1}(y;1)<1$ moves future dollars back and is the
  \textbf{\ink{discount factor}}.

  \slidenote{Careful:}{a later dollar is not \emph{automatically} worth less. That depends on the rate $y$ you declare.}
\end{frame}

% ---- math: accumulation --------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Discrete Compounding}
  Let $j=0,1,2,\ldots$ index the \textbf{\ink{compounding periods}} and let
  $i_\ell>-1$ be the \mbox{\hilite{effective periodic rate}} over
  $\ell\rightarrow\ell+1$, collected as $\mathbf i=(i_0,\ldots,i_{j-1})$. An
  amount $P$ held at time $0$ becomes:
  \[
    F_j=\underbrace{\left[\prod_{\ell=0}^{j-1}(1+i_\ell)\right]}_{\mathcal D_{j,0}(\mathbf i)}P .
  \]

  For nominal annual yield $y$ compounded $n$ times per year, $i_\ell=y/n$:
  \[
    \underbrace{\mathcal D_{j,0}(y;n)=\left(1+\frac{y}{n}\right)^{j}}_{\text{\footnotesize accumulation}},
    \qquad
    \underbrace{\mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n)=\left(1+\frac{y}{n}\right)^{-j}}_{\text{\footnotesize discount}}.
  \]

  \vspace{0.05em}
  {\small \hilite{Assumes}: constant $y/n$, end-of-period compounding,
  reinvested interest, $1+y/n>0$.}

  \slidenote{Careful:}{a flat $y$ is an \emph{assumption}. In L2b, one security's yield gives way to a curve of spot rates.}
\end{frame}

% ---- math: continuous ----------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Continuous Compounding}
  Let $g$ be a constant \hilite{continuously compounded growth rate}, carrying
  units of inverse time, and let $T$ be the horizon in years. The forward
  accumulation and discount factors over $0\rightarrow T$ are given by:
  \[
    \underbrace{\mathcal D_{T,0}(g)=e^{gT}}_{\text{\footnotesize accumulation}},
    \qquad
    \underbrace{\mathcal D_{T,0}^{-1}(g)=e^{-gT}}_{\text{\footnotesize discount}}.
  \]

  A nominal annual yield $y$ compounded $n$ times per year has the continuously
  compounded equivalent $g_y:=n\log\left(1+y/n\right)$, so
  $\left(1+y/n\right)^{nT}=e^{g_yT}$: these are
  \textbf{\ink{two representations of one accumulation rule}}, not two different
  rules.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  Convert before you exponentiate: at finite $n$ the quote $y$ differs from
  $g_y$, so $y$ \textbf{\ink{does not}} belong in $e^{yT}$.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  {\small\dimmed{Optional: for a time-varying rate $g(t)$, $\mathcal D_{T,0}(g)=\exp\bigl(\int_0^{T}g(u)\,du\bigr)$.}}

  \slidenote{Log return:}{the dimensionless product $r=(\Delta t)\,g$. L2a and L3a build on it.}
\end{frame}

% ---- who sets the rate ---------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Who Sets the Valuation Rate?}
  A valuation rate is \hilite{not universal}. It depends on the cash flow, market
  convention, horizon, and the risks being modeled: no single policy rate prices
  every asset.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \eyebrow{The Federal Reserve}
  \vspace{0.25em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
    \item Steers short-term rates through \textbf{\ink{interest on reserve balances}}
      (IORB), supported by the overnight reverse repo facility; open market
      operations manage the supply of reserves.
    \item The \textbf{\ink{FOMC}} sets a target range for the federal funds rate, the
      overnight rate on unsecured reserve balances traded between eligible
      institutions.
    \item Other market rates respond to policy and market conditions, but are
      \textbf{\ink{not mechanically pegged}} to the federal funds rate.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Link:}{\href{https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm}{FOMC calendar and meeting materials}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Valuation Rate Should I Use?}
  There is no universal answer, and this lecture does not need one: here the
  yield $y$ is a \hilite{declared model input}. Choosing it well means matching
  the rate to the cash flow: same currency, same horizon, and an explicit
  statement of any adjustment made for credit, liquidity, or risk.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \eyebrow{Three labels for a rate}
      \vspace{0.25em}
      {\small An \textbf{\ink{interest rate}} is paid or received for borrowing
      or lending. A \textbf{\ink{yield}} is tied to an investment's price and its
      cash flows. A \textbf{\ink{discount rate}} is whichever rate a model uses
      to convert future cash flows to present value. One number can carry more
      than one label: pick a bond yield as your valuation rate and that yield is
      now the discount rate.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \eyebrow{Keep $g_y$ apart from $g$}
      \vspace{0.25em}
      {\small You \textbf{\ink{declare}} $y$ and a frequency $n$; its
      continuously compounded equivalent $g_y=n\log\left(1+y/n\right)$ follows,
      so $y$ and $g_y$ are two conventions for one rate rather than competing
      rates. From L2a onward, $g$ denotes an \textbf{\ink{asset's own}} growth
      rate, which is measured from prices rather than declared.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ---- net cash flows ------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Net Cash Flows}
  An abstract asset is a \textbf{\ink{sequence of dated cash flows}} denominated in a
  currency. At cash-flow index $j$, let $\mathbf c_j\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{m}$
  collect $m$ nonnegative magnitudes and let
  $\symbf{\nu}_j\in\{-1,+1\}^{m}$ give their directions, where $+1$ denotes an
  inflow and $-1$ an outflow. The \hilite{signed net cash flow} at index $j$ is
  the usual inner product of the magnitude and direction vectors:
  \[
    \bar c_j=\left\langle\mathbf c_j,\symbf{\nu}_j\right\rangle
    =\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{j,i}\,\nu_{j,i}.
  \]

  Over the dates $j=0,1,\ldots,N$, the undiscounted total $\sum_{j=0}^{N}\bar c_j$
  is \textbf{\ink{not}} a time-0 value: its terms are dollars from different dates.

  \slidenote{Next:}{discount every term to a common date, and the result is net present value.}
\end{frame}

% ---- math: NPV -----------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Net Present Value}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  Let $\bar c_j$ be the \textbf{\ink{signed net cash flow}} on date $j$, positive for
  an inflow and negative for an outflow, over the dates $j=0,1,\ldots,N$. Discount
  each one to the valuation date with $\mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n)$,
  \hilite{then add}. The net present value at time $0$ is given by:
  \[
    \operatorname{NPV}_0(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n)\,\bar c_j
    =\underbrace{\left\langle \mathcal D_{\star,0}^{-1}(y;n),\ \bar{\mathbf c}\right\rangle}_{\text{\footnotesize time-0 dollars}} .
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  The $\star$ runs over every cash-flow index, so $\mathcal D_{\star,0}^{-1}(y;n)$
  and $\bar{\mathbf c}$ are ordered $(N+1)$-vectors and NPV is a single scalar
  product, which is exactly how we compute it in code.

  \slidenote{Worked example:}{ten years of Model S ownership, $\operatorname{NPV}_0\approx-\$121{,}484$}
\end{frame}

% ==== optional advanced material =============================
\begin{frame}{Optional Advanced Material}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  {\normalsize\textbf{\ink{Derivation:}}\par
  \dimmed{CHEME-5660-L1b-Derivation-Discrete-Continuous-Compounding-Fall-2026.ipynb}}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  Optional, not a prerequisite for L2a. Over a horizon $T$, holding $y$ fixed
  while $n$ grows gives a limit discrete compounding only approaches. That is a
  different statement from the exact identity
  $\left(1+y/n\right)^{nT}=e^{g_yT}$, and the shortfall from it is:
  \[
    \delta_n:=1-\frac{\left(1+y/n\right)^{nT}}{e^{yT}}
    \;\approx\;\underbrace{\frac{y^{2}T}{2n}}_{\text{\footnotesize leading estimate}} .
  \]

  \vspace{-0.35em}
  The estimate is \hilite{asymptotic}: it needs both $y/n$ and $y^{2}T/(2n)$
  small, so a low frequency, a long horizon, or a large rate each degrade it.
  A high $n$ alone is not the test.

  \slidenote{Careful:}{at $y=15\%$ and $T=30$ yr the estimate overstates $\delta_n$ by about $27.6\%$ at $n=1$. Use the exact ratio there.}
\end{frame}
