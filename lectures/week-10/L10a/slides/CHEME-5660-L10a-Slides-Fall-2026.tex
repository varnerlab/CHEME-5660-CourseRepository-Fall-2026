\begin{frame}{Monte Carlo Pricing of American Options}
  We simulate share price paths under the risk-neutral pricing rule $\Q$, then estimate the value of holding
  at each date by regressing the discounted cash flows from holding on the
  current share price across paths. Like the lattice, Longstaff and Schwartz
  work backward from expiration:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \hilite{Initialize}: given the share price $S_{0}$, the strike price $K$, the
  payoff function $V(K,S)$ of the call or put, the time to expiration $T$ (years),
  the volatility $\sigma$, the risk-free rate $g_{y}$, the number of exercise dates $h$ after
  today, and the number of paths $M$. Compute $\Delta t=T/h$ and
  $\Dstep=e^{-g_{y}\Delta t}$.
  \slidenote{Reference:}{Longstaff, F. A., and Schwartz, E. S. (2001). Valuing American options by simulation: a simple least-squares approach. \href{https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/14.1.113}{Review of Financial Studies 14(1): 113–147}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pseudocode: Longstaff–Schwartz}
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item \hilite{Simulate}: generate $M$ share price paths under $\Q$ from $S_{0}$
      on the dates $t=0,1,\dots,h$ (expiration), where $S_{i}(t)$ is the share
      price on path $i$ at date $t$.
    \item At expiration, set each path's cash flow to its payoff:
      $Y_{i}(h)\gets V(K,S_{i}(h))$ for $i=1,2,\dots,M$, where $Y_{i}(t)$ is
      path $i$'s cash flow valued at date $t$.
    \item For each date $t=h-1,h-2,\dots,1$ \textbf{do}:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item[a.] Discount each cash flow back one date: $Y_{i}(t)\gets\Dstep\cdot Y_{i}(t+1)$.
        \item[b.] \hilite{Regress}: on the in-the-money paths, those with $V(K,S_{i}(t))>0$, fit the value of holding
          $\hat{C}_{t}(S)=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}S+\beta_{2}S^{2}$ to the pairs
          $\bigl(S_{i}(t),Y_{i}(t)\bigr)$ by least squares.
        \item[c.] \hilite{Decide}: for each in-the-money path, if
          $V(K,S_{i}(t))>\hat{C}_{t}(S_{i}(t))$, \textbf{then}: exercise, so
          $Y_{i}(t)\gets V(K,S_{i}(t))$.
      \end{itemize}
    \item Discount the cash flows to today and average them:
      $\bar{Y}\gets\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{1,0}(g_{y})\cdot\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}Y_{i}(1)$.
    \item The premium is $\PAM\gets\max\bigl\{V(K,S_{0}),~\bar{Y}\bigr\}$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Extrinsic versus Intrinsic Value}
  The premium (price) of an American option can be broken down into two
  components: intrinsic value and extrinsic value.
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item \hilite{Intrinsic value}: the immediate exercise value,
      $\max(0, S(t) - K)$ for a call and $\max(0, K - S(t))$ for a put, where
      $S(t)$ is the current share price.
    \item \hilite{Extrinsic value}: also known as time value, the additional
      value from the potential for future price movements before expiration.
  \end{itemize}
  The extrinsic value $E_{V}(t)$ can be expressed mathematically as:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      E_{V}(t) = \text{Option Price} - \text{Intrinsic Value}\quad\blacksquare
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Extrinsic Value}
  In expanded form, the extrinsic value $E_{V}(t)$ is:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      E_{V}(t) &= \begin{cases}
      \mathcal{P}_{c}(K,S(t)) - \max(0, S(t) - K) & \text{(for Call Options)} \\
      \mathcal{P}_{p}(K,S(t)) - \max(0, K - S(t)) & \text{(for Put Options)}
      \end{cases}
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
  When the option is \hilite{out of the money}, it has no intrinsic value, and
  the entire option price is extrinsic value.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \textbf{\ink{Why do people pay money for something that is ``worthless''?}}
  There is still a chance that the share price moves favorably before
  expiration.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  As time moves towards expiration, the extrinsic value tends to decrease
  (\hilite{time decay}), because there is less time for the share price to
  move favorably.
  \slidenote{Review on your own:}{\href{\repo/CHEME-5660-L10a-Example-ExtrinsicValue-Fall-2026.ipynb}{\dimmed{CHEME-5660-L10a-Example-ExtrinsicValue-Fall-2026.ipynb}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Greeks}
  The \hilite{Greeks} are local sensitivity coefficients that measure how
  American options premiums change with market conditions:
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item \hilite{Delta $\Delta$}: the change in premium for a
      \texttt{+1~USD/share} change in the share price.
    \item \hilite{Theta $\Theta$}: the change in premium for a
      \texttt{1-day decrease} in the lifetime of the contract (time decay).
    \item \hilite{Vega $\nu$}: the change in premium for a \texttt{+1\%} change
      in volatility.
    \item \hilite{Gamma $\Gamma$}: the change in $\Delta$ for a
      \texttt{+1~USD/share} change in the share price.
    \item \hilite{Rho $\rho$}: the change in premium for a \texttt{+1\%} change
      in the risk-free rate, usually minor except for longer-term options.
  \end{itemize}
  Most trading platforms compute these for you!
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Local Taylor Expansion of the Premium}
  Expanding the premium $\Pst(S,\sigma,t,g_{y})$ of a contract of type
  $\star\in\{c,p\}$ at calendar time $t$ to second order in $S$ and first order in the other inputs:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      \Delta\Pst \approx \Delta_{\star}\,\Delta S + \nu_{\star}\,\Delta\sigma
      + \Theta_{\star}\,\Delta t + \rho_{\star}\,\Delta g_{y}
      + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Gamma_{\star}\,(\Delta S)^{2}
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
  \begin{equation*}
    \Delta_{\star}=\frac{\partial\Pst}{\partial S}\qquad
    \nu_{\star}=\frac{\partial\Pst}{\partial \sigma}\qquad
    \Theta_{\star}=\frac{\partial\Pst}{\partial t}\qquad
    \rho_{\star}=\frac{\partial\Pst}{\partial g_{y}}\qquad
    \Gamma_{\star}=\frac{\partial^{2}\Pst}{\partial S^{2}}
  \end{equation*}
  Derivatives are taken at the current inputs, with $\Delta t$ in years and
  $\Delta\sigma$, $\Delta g_{y}$ in decimals. Platforms quote $\Theta_{\star}/365$ per day, and $\nu_{\star}/100$
  and $\rho_{\star}/100$ per percentage point.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Delta and Gamma as Duration and Convexity}
  $\Delta_{\star}$ and $\Gamma_{\star}$ play the roles of a bond's duration
  and convexity: the slope and the curvature of the premium as a function
  of the share price.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \textbf{\ink{Example:}} a long call has $\Delta_{c}=0.50$ and
  $\Gamma_{c}=0.02$ per USD/share.
  \begin{align*}
    \Delta S=+5:\quad \Delta\mathcal{P}_{c} &\approx 0.50\times5+\tfrac{1}{2}(0.02)(5)^{2}=2.50+0.25=2.75~\text{USD/share}\\
    \Delta S=-5:\quad \Delta\mathcal{P}_{c} &\approx -2.50+0.25=-2.25~\text{USD/share}
  \end{align*}
  The Gamma term adds to the premium whichever way the share price moves.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Computing the Greeks of American Options}
  Backward induction on the CRR tree gives no formula to differentiate, so we
  reprice the tree with one input moved up and down by a step $\epsilon$:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      \frac{\partial\Pst}{\partial x} & \approx \frac{\Pst(x+\epsilon)-\Pst(x-\epsilon)}{2\epsilon}\qquad x\in\{S,\sigma,t,g_{y}\}\\
      \Gamma_{\star} & \approx \frac{\Pst(S+\epsilon)-2\,\Pst(S)+\Pst(S-\epsilon)}{\epsilon^{2}}
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Leisen–Reimer Tree}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  Leisen and Reimer choose $q$ so the tree finishes above the strike as often
  as GBM does under $\Q$:
  {\small\setlength{\jot}{1pt}%
  \setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{3pt}\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{3pt}%
  \[
    \begin{alignedat}{2}
      S_{T} & = S_{0}\exp\left[\left(g_{y}-\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)T+\sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z\right] &\quad&\text{\footnotesize GBM under }\Q\text{\footnotesize, with }Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\\
      \ln S_{T} & = \ln S_{0}+\left(g_{y}-\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)T+\sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z &&\text{\footnotesize take the logarithm}\\
      \Q\left(S_{T}>K\right) & = \Q\left(\ln S_{T}>\ln K\right) &&\text{\footnotesize $\ln$ is increasing}\\
      & = \Q\left(\ln S_{0}+\left(g_{y}-\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)T+\sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z>\ln K\right) &&\text{\footnotesize substitute $\ln S_{T}$}\\
      & = \Q\left(Z>\frac{\ln K-\ln S_{0}-\left(g_{y}-\sigma^{2}/2\right)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\right) &&\text{\footnotesize subtract, then divide by $\sigma\sqrt{T}>0$}\\
      & = \Q\left(Z>-d_{-}\right) &&\text{\footnotesize $\ln K-\ln S_{0}=-\ln(S_{0}/K)$}\\
      & = 1-N(-d_{-}) &&\text{\footnotesize $N$ is the standard normal CDF}\\
      \Longrightarrow\quad & \boxed{\Q\left(S_{T}>K\right)=N(d_{-})} &&\text{\footnotesize $N(-z)=1-N(z)$}
    \end{alignedat}
  \]}%
  where $d_{-}=\left[\ln(S_{0}/K)+\left(g_{y}-\sigma^{2}/2\right)T\right]/(\sigma\sqrt{T})$.
  This probability depends only on the share price, so it holds for American contracts too.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Leisen–Reimer Probabilities and Factors}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  With an odd number of steps $h$, the tree is centered on the strike and
  finishes above it when more than half of its moves are up. The Peizer--Pratt
  inversion $H(z;h)$ gives the up probability that makes this happen with
  probability close to $N(z)$:
  {\small\setlength{\jot}{1.5pt}%
  \setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{3pt}\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{3pt}%
  \[
    H(z;h)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\operatorname{sgn}(z)}{2}\sqrt{1-\exp\left[-\left(\frac{z}{h+1/3+0.1/(h+1)}\right)^{2}\left(h+\frac{1}{6}\right)\right]}
  \]
  \[
    \begin{alignedat}{2}
      q & = H(d_{-};h)\qquad q'=H(d_{+};h) &\quad&\text{\footnotesize risk-neutral and second probabilities}\\
      u & = e^{g_{y}\Delta t}\,\frac{q'}{q}\qquad d=e^{g_{y}\Delta t}\,\frac{1-q'}{1-q} &&\text{\footnotesize up and down factors}
    \end{alignedat}
  \]
  where $d_{+}=d_{-}+\sigma\sqrt{T}$ and $\Delta t=T/h$. Like the CRR factors, they grow the expected share price at the risk-free rate:
  \[
    qu+(1-q)d=q\,e^{g_{y}\Delta t}\frac{q'}{q}+(1-q)\,e^{g_{y}\Delta t}\frac{1-q'}{1-q}=e^{g_{y}\Delta t}\left(q'+1-q'\right)=e^{g_{y}\Delta t}
  \]}%
  \hilite{No corners}: $d_{\pm}$ depend on $S_{0}$, and so do the factors. The
  strike stays between the two middle terminal nodes as $S_{0}$ moves.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Greeks on the Leisen–Reimer Tree}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \hilite{Initialize}: given $S_{0}$, $K$, the payoff function $V(K,S)$ of the
  call or put, $T$ (years), $\sigma$, $g_{y}$, an odd number of time steps $h$,
  and a step $\epsilon_{x}$ for each input $x\in\{S_{0},\sigma,T,g_{y}\}$, in
  the units of that input. Let $\mathcal{P}(S_{0},\sigma,T,g_{y})$ be the
  premium that steps 1--4 compute from a set of inputs.
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item Compute $\Delta t\gets T/h$, then
      $d_{-}\gets\left[\ln(S_{0}/K)+\left(g_{y}-\sigma^{2}/2\right)T\right]/(\sigma\sqrt{T})$
      and $d_{+}\gets d_{-}+\sigma\sqrt{T}$.
    \item Compute the probabilities $q\gets H(d_{-};h)$ and $q'\gets H(d_{+};h)$.
    \item Compute the factors $u\gets e^{g_{y}\Delta t}\,q'/q$ and
      $d\gets e^{g_{y}\Delta t}\,(1-q')/(1-q)$.
    \item Compute the premium with the CRR pricing pseudocode, using these $u$,
      $d$, and $q$: $\mathcal{P}(S_{0},\sigma,T,g_{y})\gets\hat{V}_{0,0}$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pseudocode: Leisen–Reimer Greeks}
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \setcounter{enumi}{4}
    \item For each input $x\in\{S_{0},\sigma,T,g_{y}\}$ \textbf{do}:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item[a.] Compute $\mathcal{P}(x+\epsilon_{x})$ and $\mathcal{P}(x-\epsilon_{x})$
          with steps 1--4, moving only $x$. The factors depend on every input,
          so each evaluation rebuilds the tree.
        \item[b.] Compute the central difference:
          $\partial\mathcal{P}/\partial x\gets\left[\mathcal{P}(x+\epsilon_{x})-\mathcal{P}(x-\epsilon_{x})\right]/(2\epsilon_{x})$.
      \end{itemize}
    \item Compute $\Delta\gets\partial\mathcal{P}/\partial S_{0}$,
      $\nu\gets\partial\mathcal{P}/\partial\sigma$,
      $\rho\gets\partial\mathcal{P}/\partial g_{y}$, and
      $\Theta\gets-\partial\mathcal{P}/\partial T$, because calendar time moves
      forward as the time to expiration falls.
    \item Compute Gamma from the three share price evaluations:
      $\Gamma\gets\left[\mathcal{P}(S_{0}+\epsilon_{S_{0}})-2\,\mathcal{P}(S_{0})+\mathcal{P}(S_{0}-\epsilon_{S_{0}})\right]/\epsilon_{S_{0}}^{2}$.
  \end{enumerate}
  \hilite{Output}: the premium $\mathcal{P}(S_{0},\sigma,T,g_{y})$ and the Greeks
  $\Delta$, $\Gamma$, $\Theta$, $\nu$, and $\rho$.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Before reporting a Greek, recompute it with about twice as many time steps,
  still odd, and smaller steps $\epsilon_{x}$. Report it once it stops changing.
  \slidenote{Example 2:}{\href{\repo/CHEME-5660-L10a-Example-Greeks-PutCall-Fall-2026.ipynb}{\dimmed{CHEME-5660-L10a-Example-Greeks-PutCall-Fall-2026.ipynb}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Secret Lives of Delta}
  Besides measuring the premium change for a \texttt{+1~USD/share} move in the
  share price, $\Delta$ has two other interpretations:
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
    \item \hilite{Hedge ratio}: for every $n$ contracts held ($n<0$ if the
      contracts are sold), hold $-100\cdot n\cdot\Delta$ shares to be
      \hilite{delta-neutral}, i.e., insensitive to small share price moves.
    \item \hilite{Probability of exercise}: $\Delta$ is often used as an
      estimate of the probability that the option expires in-the-money. A call
      with $\Delta=0.7$ suggests a 70\% chance.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Probabilities of Exercise and Profit}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \hilite{Probability of exercise (POE)}: the probability that the contract
  finishes in-the-money at expiration $t=T$:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      \text{POE} & = \begin{cases}
      \mathbb{P}\left(S_{T} > K + 0.01\right) & \text{(for Call Options)} \\
      \mathbb{P}\left(S_{T} < K - 0.01\right) & \text{(for Put Options)}
      \end{cases}
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
  where $S_{T}$ is the share price at expiration and $K$ is the strike price.\par
  \textbf{\ink{Conjecture:}} $\Delta\approx\text{POE}$. Example 3 checks it.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \hilite{Probability of profit (POP)}: the probability that the contract's
  profit at expiration is positive:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{\text{POP}=\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{P}_{\mathcal{C}} > 0\right)}
  \end{equation*}
  where $\hat{P}_{\mathcal{C}}$ is the profit of the (single or composite)
  contract at expiration.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Computing POE and POP by Simulation}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  POE and POP are forecasts, so we compute them under our belief $\mathbb{P}$:
  GBM with our estimates of the drift $\mu$ and the volatility $\sigma$.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \hilite{Initialize}: given $S_{0}$, $K$, the premium $\mathcal{P}$ of the call
  or put, $T$ (years), $\mu$ and $\sigma$ under $\mathbb{P}$, $g_{y}$, and the
  number of samples $M$. Compute the breakeven share price
  $S^{\ddagger}=K+\mathcal{D}_{T,0}(g_{y})\cdot\mathcal{P}$ for a call, or
  $S^{\ddagger}=K-\mathcal{D}_{T,0}(g_{y})\cdot\mathcal{P}$ for a put, where
  $\mathcal{D}_{T,0}(g_{y})=e^{g_{y}T}$.
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item For each sample $i=1,2,\dots,M$ \textbf{do}:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item[a.] Draw a sample from the standard normal distribution: $Z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$.
        \item[b.] Compute the share price at expiration:
          $S_{i}(T)\gets S_{0}\exp\left[\left(\mu-\sigma^{2}/2\right)T+\sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z_{i}\right]$.
      \end{itemize}
    \item Compute the cumulative distribution function, the fraction of samples
      at or below each share price $x$:
      $\hat{F}(x)\gets\frac{1}{M}\cdot\#\left\{i:S_{i}(T)\leq x\right\}$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pseudocode: POE and POP}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
    \setcounter{enumi}{2}
    \item Compute the probability of exercise. If the contract is a call,
      \textbf{then} $\text{POE}\gets1-\hat{F}(K+0.01)$. Otherwise,
      $\text{POE}\gets\hat{F}(K-0.01)$.
    \item Compute the probability of profit:
      \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.25em}
        \item If we bought a call or sold a put, \textbf{then} the profit is
          positive above the breakeven: $\text{POP}\gets1-\hat{F}(S^{\ddagger})$.
        \item If we sold a call or bought a put, \textbf{then} the profit is
          positive below the breakeven: $\text{POP}\gets\hat{F}(S^{\ddagger})$.
        \item If the contract is composite and its profit changes sign at more
          than one share price, \textbf{then} count the profitable samples:
          $\text{POP}\gets\frac{1}{M}\cdot\#\left\{i:\hat{P}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(S_{i}(T)\right)>0\right\}$.
      \end{itemize}
  \end{enumerate}
  \hilite{Output}: the probability of exercise $\text{POE}$ and the probability
  of profit $\text{POP}$.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  With $\mu=g_{y}$ and $\sigma$ set to the implied volatility, the same steps
  give the probabilities under $\Q$. These are the probabilities inside the
  market price, not our forecast.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Is Delta Close to the POE?}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  With no dividends, a call's American and European premiums are equal.
  Unrolling the European backward induction on the CRR tree:
  {\small\setlength{\jot}{1pt}%
  \setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{2pt}\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{2pt}%
  \[
    \mathcal{P}_{c}=\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{T,0}(g_{y})\sum_{k=0}^{h}\binom{h}{k}q^{k}(1-q)^{h-k}\max\left(S_{0}u^{k}d^{h-k}-K,~0\right)
  \]
  The factors do not depend on $S_{0}$, so a payoff has slope $u^{k}d^{h-k}$ in
  $S_{0}$ if its node is in the money, and zero otherwise. Define
  $q^{*}=q\,u\,e^{-g_{y}\Delta t}$. Dividing $qu+(1-q)d=e^{g_{y}\Delta t}$ by
  $e^{g_{y}\Delta t}$ gives $(1-q)\,d\,e^{-g_{y}\Delta t}=1-q^{*}$, so $q^{*}$
  is also a probability. Then Delta is:
  \[
    \begin{alignedat}{2}
      \Delta_{c} & = \frac{\partial\mathcal{P}_{c}}{\partial S_{0}}=\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{T,0}(g_{y})\sum_{k\,\in\,\text{ITM}}\binom{h}{k}q^{k}(1-q)^{h-k}\,u^{k}d^{h-k} &\quad&\text{\footnotesize only ITM payoffs have a slope}\\
      & = \sum_{k\,\in\,\text{ITM}}\binom{h}{k}\left(q\,u\,e^{-g_{y}\Delta t}\right)^{k}\left((1-q)\,d\,e^{-g_{y}\Delta t}\right)^{h-k} &&\text{\footnotesize $\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{T,0}(g_{y})=e^{-g_{y}h\Delta t}$, split over $h$ steps}\\
      & = \sum_{k\,\in\,\text{ITM}}\binom{h}{k}\left(q^{*}\right)^{k}\left(1-q^{*}\right)^{h-k} &&\text{\footnotesize substitute $q^{*}$ and $1-q^{*}$}
    \end{alignedat}
  \]}%
  where ITM is the set of in-the-money terminal nodes.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Delta Is a Probability of Exercise}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  The risk-neutral POE of the call is the same sum with $q$ in place of $q^{*}$:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \Delta_{c}=\sum_{k\,\in\,\text{ITM}}\binom{h}{k}\left(q^{*}\right)^{k}\left(1-q^{*}\right)^{h-k}
    \qquad
    \Q\left(S_{T}>K\right)=\sum_{k\,\in\,\text{ITM}}\binom{h}{k}q^{k}(1-q)^{h-k}}
  \end{equation*}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.1em}
    \item \hilite{Delta is larger than the risk-neutral POE}: $u>e^{g_{y}\Delta t}$,
      so $q^{*}>q$. On the Leisen–Reimer tree, $q^{*}$ is the second probability $q'$.
    \item \hilite{When is Delta close?} Each step adds about
      $\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}$ to the up probability, so the gap
      grows with $\sigma\sqrt{T}$. Delta is close for short-dated contracts.
    \item \hilite{Our forecast and American puts}: under $\mathbb{P}$, the up
      probability is the real-world $p$, so the forecast POE can sit above or
      below Delta. Early exercise adds to $|\Delta_{p}|$, so the match is approximate.
  \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}
  Today we priced American options by simulation, split the premium into
  intrinsic and extrinsic value, and introduced the Greeks.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{Key takeaways}}}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.15em}
    \item \hilite{Estimating and decomposing the American premium}:
      Longstaff--Schwartz regresses the value of holding on the share price.
      Intrinsic value is the immediate exercise value, and extrinsic value is the
      potential for favorable price movement.
    \item \hilite{The Greeks quantify option price sensitivity}: they are the
      partial derivatives in a local Taylor expansion of the premium, which we
      estimate for American options by repricing a binomial tree. The
      Leisen--Reimer tree avoids the corners of the CRR premium.
    \item \hilite{Delta serves three purposes}: the rate of change in the
      premium, the hedge ratio for delta-neutral positions, and an
      approximation of the POE. On the tree, a call's Delta is exactly a POE
      computed with a slightly larger up probability. POP reads the projected
      share price distribution at the breakeven.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
