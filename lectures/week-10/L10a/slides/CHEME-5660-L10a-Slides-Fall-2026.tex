\begin{frame}{Monte Carlo Pricing of American Options}
  We simulate share price paths under the risk-neutral pricing rule $\Q$, then estimate the value of holding
  at each date by regressing the discounted cash flows from holding on the
  current share price across paths. Like the lattice, Longstaff and Schwartz
  work backward from expiration:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \hilite{Initialize}: given the share price $S_{0}$, the strike price $K$, the
  payoff function $V(K,S)$ of the call or put, the time to expiration $T$ (years),
  the volatility $\sigma$, the risk-free rate $g_{y}$, the number of exercise dates $h$ after
  today, and the number of paths $M$. Compute $\Delta t=T/h$ and
  $\Dstep=e^{-g_{y}\Delta t}$.
  \slidenote{Reference:}{Longstaff, F. A., and Schwartz, E. S. (2001). Valuing American options by simulation: a simple least-squares approach. \href{https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/14.1.113}{Review of Financial Studies 14(1): 113–147}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pseudocode: Longstaff–Schwartz}
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item \hilite{Simulate}: generate $M$ share price paths under $\Q$ from $S_{0}$
      on the dates $t=0,1,\dots,h$ (expiration), where $S_{i}(t)$ is the share
      price on path $i$ at date $t$.
    \item At expiration, set each path's cash flow to its payoff:
      $Y_{i}(h)\gets V(K,S_{i}(h))$ for $i=1,2,\dots,M$, where $Y_{i}(t)$ is
      path $i$'s cash flow valued at date $t$.
    \item For each date $t=h-1,h-2,\dots,1$ \textbf{do}:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item[a.] Discount each cash flow back one date: $Y_{i}(t)\gets\Dstep\cdot Y_{i}(t+1)$.
        \item[b.] \hilite{Regress}: on the in-the-money paths, those with $V(K,S_{i}(t))>0$, fit the value of holding
          $\hat{H}_{t}(S)=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}S+\beta_{2}S^{2}$ to the pairs
          $\bigl(S_{i}(t),Y_{i}(t)\bigr)$ by least squares.
        \item[c.] \hilite{Decide}: for each in-the-money path, if
          $V(K,S_{i}(t))>\hat{H}_{t}(S_{i}(t))$, \textbf{then}: exercise, so
          $Y_{i}(t)\gets V(K,S_{i}(t))$.
      \end{itemize}
    \item Discount the cash flows to today and average them:
      $\bar{Y}\gets\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{1,0}(g_{y})\cdot\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}Y_{i}(1)$.
    \item The premium is $\PAM\gets\max\bigl\{V(K,S_{0}),~\bar{Y}\bigr\}$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Intrinsic and Extrinsic Value}
  The premium (price) of an American option can be broken down into two
  components: intrinsic value and extrinsic value.
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item \hilite{Intrinsic value}: the immediate exercise value, what we
      would collect by exercising today, $\max(0, S(t) - K)$ for a call and
      $\max(0, K - S(t))$ for a put, where
      $S(t)$ is the current share price (the red dashed line).
    \item \hilite{Extrinsic value}: also known as time value, the rest of the
      premium (the gap above the red dashed line), from the potential for
      future price movements before expiration.
  \end{itemize}
  The extrinsic value $E_{V}(t)$ can be expressed mathematically as:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      E_{V}(t) = \text{Option Price} - \text{Intrinsic Value}\quad\blacksquare
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Extrinsic Value}
  In expanded form, the extrinsic value $E_{V}(t)$ is:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      E_{V}(t) &= \begin{cases}
      \mathcal{P}_{c}(K,S(t)) - \max(0, S(t) - K) & \text{(for Call Options)} \\
      \mathcal{P}_{p}(K,S(t)) - \max(0, K - S(t)) & \text{(for Put Options)}
      \end{cases}
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
  \hilite{Out of the money} (point c) or at the money (point a), there is no
  intrinsic value, so the entire option price is extrinsic value, largest at a.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  As time moves towards expiration, the extrinsic value tends to decrease
  (\hilite{time decay}), because there is less time for the share price to
  move favorably. The points drop toward the red dashed line as the DTE
  shrinks.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \textbf{\ink{Why do people pay money for something that is ``worthless''?}}
  There is still a chance that the share price moves favorably before
  expiration.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Would You Ever Exercise Early?}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.15em}
    \item \hilite{A call}: not without dividends. Exercising buys the shares at
      $K$~USD/share. Holding is better: we pay $K$ later and earn interest on
      it, and we don't buy if the share price falls below $K$.
    \item \hilite{A put}: yes, far in the money. Exercising sells the shares at
      $K$~USD/share, and collecting that cash now beats waiting.
  \end{itemize}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.37\textheight,keepaspectratio]{../figs/Fig-L10a-Premium-vs-Moneyness.pdf}
  \end{center}
  So the call curves stay above the red dashed line (by at least the interest
  on $K$) and the put curves meet it. An American call has the same premium as
  a European call.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What If the Share Pays a Dividend?}
  A dividend changes the answer for a call. The dividend goes to those who own
  the shares before the
  \href{https://www.optionseducation.org/referencelibrary/faq/options-exercise}{\hilite{ex-dividend date}},
  not to call holders.

  \medskip
  So if the dividend is larger than the extrinsic value we give up by exercising
  early, which is small far in the money, we exercise the trading day before the
  ex-dividend date and collect the dividend.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Greeks}
  The \hilite{Greeks} are local sensitivity coefficients that measure how
  American options premiums change with market conditions:
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item \hilite{Delta $\Delta$}: the change in premium for a
      \texttt{+1~USD/share} change in the share price.
    \item \hilite{Theta $\Theta$}: the change in premium for a
      \texttt{1-day decrease} in the lifetime of the contract (time decay).
    \item \hilite{Vega $\nu$}: the change in premium for a \texttt{+1\%} change
      in volatility.
    \item \hilite{Gamma $\Gamma$}: the change in $\Delta$ for a
      \texttt{+1~USD/share} change in the share price.
    \item \hilite{Rho $\rho$}: the change in premium for a \texttt{+1\%} change
      in the risk-free rate, usually minor except for longer-term options.
  \end{itemize}
  Most trading platforms compute these for you!
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Local Taylor Expansion of the Premium}
  Expanding the premium $\Pst(S,\sigma,t,g_{y})$ of a contract of type
  $\star\in\{c,p\}$ at calendar time $t$ to second order in $S$ and first order in the other inputs:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      \Delta\Pst &\approx \Delta_{\star}\,\Delta S + \nu_{\star}\,\Delta\sigma
      + \Theta_{\star}\,\Delta t + \rho_{\star}\,\Delta g_{y}
      + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Gamma_{\star}\,(\Delta S)^{2}\\
      &= \underbrace{\bigl(\Delta_{\star} + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Gamma_{\star}\,\Delta S\bigr)\Delta S}_{\text{share price}}
      + \underbrace{\nu_{\star}\,\Delta\sigma}_{\text{volatility}}
      + \underbrace{\Theta_{\star}\,\Delta t}_{\text{time}}
      + \underbrace{\rho_{\star}\,\Delta g_{y}}_{\text{rate}}
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
  \begin{equation*}
    \Delta_{\star}=\frac{\partial\Pst}{\partial S}\qquad
    \nu_{\star}=\frac{\partial\Pst}{\partial \sigma}\qquad
    \Theta_{\star}=\frac{\partial\Pst}{\partial t}\qquad
    \rho_{\star}=\frac{\partial\Pst}{\partial g_{y}}\qquad
    \Gamma_{\star}=\frac{\partial^{2}\Pst}{\partial S^{2}}
  \end{equation*}
  Derivatives are taken at the current inputs. One day is $\Delta t=1/365$ years, so the
  premium changes by $\Theta_{\star}/365$. One percentage point is $\Delta\sigma=0.01$, so it
  changes by $\nu_{\star}/100$ (or $\rho_{\star}/100$ for the rate). Trading platforms quote
  these values as Theta, Vega, and Rho.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Delta and Gamma}
  Delta $\Delta_{\star}$ and Gamma $\Gamma_{\star}$ are the slope and the curvature
  of the premium as a function of the share price.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \textbf{\ink{Example:}} a long call has $\Delta_{c}=0.50$ and
  $\Gamma_{c}=0.02$ per USD/share.
  \begin{align*}
    \Delta S=+5:\quad \Delta\mathcal{P}_{c} &\approx \bigl(0.50+\tfrac{1}{2}(0.02)(5)\bigr)\times5=0.55\times5=2.75~\text{USD/share}\\
    \Delta S=-5:\quad \Delta\mathcal{P}_{c} &\approx (0.50-0.05)\times(-5)=-2.25~\text{USD/share}
  \end{align*}
  Gamma adds $\tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma_{c}(\Delta S)^{2}=0.25$~USD/share to the
  Delta-only $\pm2.50$, so the call gains more going up and loses less going down.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Computing the Greeks of American Options}
  Backward induction on the CRR tree gives no formula to differentiate, so we
  use a \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_difference\#Basic_types}{\hilite{central finite difference}}, repricing the tree with
  one input moved up and down by $\epsilon$:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      \frac{\partial\Pst}{\partial x} & \approx \frac{\Pst(x+\epsilon)-\Pst(x-\epsilon)}{2\epsilon}\qquad x\in\{S,\sigma,t,g_{y}\}\\
      \Gamma_{\star} & \approx \frac{\Pst(S+\epsilon)-2\,\Pst(S)+\Pst(S-\epsilon)}{\epsilon^{2}}
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
  For technical reasons, the CRR tree does not estimate $\Gamma_{\star}$ or the
  other Greeks well, so we use the \hilite{Leisen--Reimer tree}, a modification
  of the CRR tree that gives better estimates of the Greeks.
  \slidenote{Example 2:}{\href{\repo/CHEME-5660-L10a-Example-Greeks-PutCall-Fall-2026.ipynb}{\dimmed{CHEME-5660-L10a-Example-Greeks-PutCall-Fall-2026.ipynb}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Secret Lives of Delta}
  Besides measuring the premium change for a \texttt{+1~USD/share} move in the
  share price, $\Delta$ has two other interpretations:
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
    \item \hilite{Share equivalent}: $n$ contracts ($n<0$ if sold) gain or
      lose as much as $100\cdot n\cdot\Delta$ shares. Holding
      $-100\cdot n\cdot\Delta$ shares makes the position
      \hilite{delta-neutral}, so Delta is also the \hilite{hedge ratio}.
    \item \hilite{Probability of exercise}: Delta is often used as an
      estimate of the probability that the option expires in-the-money. A call
      with $\Delta=0.7$ suggests a 70\% chance.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Probability of Exercise}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \hilite{Probability of exercise (POE)}: the probability that the contract
  finishes in-the-money at expiration $t=T$:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      \text{POE} & = \begin{cases}
      \mathbb{P}\left(S_{T} > K + 0.01\right) & \text{(for Call Options)} \\
      \mathbb{P}\left(S_{T} < K - 0.01\right) & \text{(for Put Options)}
      \end{cases}
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
  where $S_{T}$ is the share price at expiration and $K$ is the strike price.\par
  \medskip
  \textbf{\ink{Why the extra 0.01~USD/share?}} OCC automatically exercises an
  expiring equity option
  \href{https://www.optionseducation.org/referencelibrary/faq/options-exercise}{\hilite{in the money by 0.01~USD/share or more}}.
  The POE uses the same threshold.\par
  \medskip
  \textbf{\ink{Conjecture:}} $\Delta\approx\text{POE}$. Example 3 checks it.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Is Delta Close to the POE?}
  \textbf{\ink{Proposition:}} for a call on a share with no dividends, on an
  $h$-step CRR tree, Delta and the POE are sums over the same in-the-money
  terminal nodes $\text{ITM}=\left\{k : S_{0}u^{k}d^{h-k}>K\right\}$. Only the up probability differs,
  $q^{*}=q\,u\,e^{-g_{y}\Delta t}$ replaces $q$:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \Delta_{c}=\sum_{k\,\in\,\text{ITM}}\binom{h}{k}\left(q^{*}\right)^{k}\left(1-q^{*}\right)^{h-k}
    \qquad
    \Q\left(S_{T}>K\right)=\sum_{k\,\in\,\text{ITM}}\binom{h}{k}q^{k}(1-q)^{h-k}}
  \end{equation*}
  \vspace{-0.8em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.1em}
    \item \hilite{Delta is larger than the risk-neutral POE}: because
      $u>e^{g_{y}\Delta t}$, $q^{*}>q$.
    \item \hilite{When is Delta close?} The gap grows with the volatility and the
      time to expiration. Delta is close for short-dated contracts.
    \item \hilite{Our forecast and American puts}: under $\mathbb{P}$, the up
      probability is $p$, so the POE can sit above or below Delta. Early
      exercise adds to $|\Delta_{p}|$.
  \end{itemize}
  \slidenote{Derivation:}{\href{\repo/advanced/delta_poe/CHEME-5660-L10a-Derivation-Delta-POE-Fall-2026.ipynb}{\dimmed{advanced/delta\_poe/CHEME-5660-L10a-Derivation-Delta-POE-Fall-2026.ipynb}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Probability of Profit}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \hilite{Probability of profit (POP)}: the probability that the contract's
  profit at expiration is positive:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{\text{POP}=\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{P}_{\mathcal{C}} > 0\right)}
  \end{equation*}
  where $\hat{P}_{\mathcal{C}}$ is the profit of the contract at expiration.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  The POE and POP are forecasts, so we compute them under our belief $\mathbb{P}$:
  GBM with our estimates of the drift $\mu$ and the volatility $\sigma$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Computing POE and POP by Simulation}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \hilite{Initialize}: given $S_{0}$, $K$, the premium $\mathcal{P}$ of the call
  or put, $T$ (years), $\mu$ and $\sigma$ under $\mathbb{P}$, $g_{y}$, and the
  number of samples $M$. Compute the breakeven share price
  $S^{\ddagger}=K+\mathcal{D}_{T,0}(g_{y})\cdot\mathcal{P}$ for a call, or
  $S^{\ddagger}=K-\mathcal{D}_{T,0}(g_{y})\cdot\mathcal{P}$ for a put, where
  $\mathcal{D}_{T,0}(g_{y})=e^{g_{y}T}$.
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item For each sample $i=1,2,\dots,M$ \textbf{do}:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item[a.] Draw a sample from the standard normal distribution: $Z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$.
        \item[b.] Compute the share price at expiration:
          $S_{i}(T)\gets S_{0}\exp\left[\left(\mu-\sigma^{2}/2\right)T+\sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z_{i}\right]$.
      \end{itemize}
    \item Compute the cumulative distribution function, the fraction of samples
      at or below each share price $x$:
      $\hat{F}(x)\gets\frac{1}{M}\cdot\#\left\{i:S_{i}(T)\leq x\right\}$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pseudocode: POE and POP}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
    \setcounter{enumi}{2}
    \item Compute the probability of exercise. If the contract is a call,
      \textbf{then} $\text{POE}\gets1-\hat{F}(K+0.01)$. Otherwise,
      $\text{POE}\gets\hat{F}(K-0.01)$.
    \item Compute the probability of profit:
      \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.25em}
        \item If we bought a call or sold a put, \textbf{then} the profit is
          positive above the breakeven: $\text{POP}\gets1-\hat{F}(S^{\ddagger})$.
        \item If we sold a call or bought a put, \textbf{then} the profit is
          positive below the breakeven: $\text{POP}\gets\hat{F}(S^{\ddagger})$.
      \end{itemize}
  \end{enumerate}
  \hilite{Output}: the probability of exercise $\text{POE}$ and the probability
  of profit $\text{POP}$.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  With $\mu=g_{y}$ and $\sigma$ set to the implied volatility, the same steps
  give the probabilities under $\Q$. These are the probabilities inside the
  market price, not our forecast.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}
  Today we priced American options by simulation, split the premium into
  intrinsic and extrinsic value, and introduced the Greeks.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{Key takeaways}}}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.15em}
    \item \hilite{Estimating and decomposing the American premium}:
      Longstaff--Schwartz regresses the value of holding on the share price.
      Extrinsic value is largest at the money and grows with the time to
      expiration. Without dividends, exercising a call early never pays, but
      exercising a deep in-the-money put can.
    \item \hilite{The Greeks quantify option price sensitivity}: they are the
      coefficients of a local Taylor expansion of the premium, which we
      estimate by finite differences on a binomial tree.
    \item \hilite{Delta serves three purposes}: the change in the premium per
      USD/share move, the hedge ratio, and an estimate of the POE. On the tree,
      a call's Delta is a POE computed with a slightly larger up probability.
      We simulate share prices at expiration to compute the POE and POP.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
