\begin{frame}{Concept Review: Are Treasury Securities Really Risk-Free?}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \hilite{No.} Treasuries carry low default risk as obligations of the U.S.
  government, which is why declared rates are so often taken from them. The
  label is conditional; two remaining risks matter today.

  \vspace{0.45em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
    \item \hilite{Default risk}: the issuer fails to pay. The United States has
      never repudiated its debt (a 1979 operational failure did delay some bill
      payments); other sovereigns have: Greece restructured in 2012, Russia
      defaulted in 1998.
    \item \hilite{Interest-rate risk}: a change in required yields changes the
      secondary-market value of fixed promised cash flows, hitting an
      institution that marks to market even without a sale.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Today:}{interest-rate risk is the one we can compute. Its size depends on maturity, coupon, yield, and the shock.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Case Study: Interest Rate Risk and Silicon Valley Bank}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  SVB held a large portfolio of \hilite{long-maturity fixed-rate} government
  securities bought while rates were low. As the Federal Reserve raised rates
  through 2022 and 2023, the value of those securities fell.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item The losses were unrealized, so a hold-to-maturity story obscured them.
    \item In early March 2023 uninsured depositors withdrew funds. SVB announced
      a capital raise to cover realized bond losses, and the bank failed.
    \item The FDIC took control on March 10, 2023, with roughly \$209 billion in
      assets: then the second largest failure of an insured bank on record, now
      the third.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.15em}
  Falling bond values and deposit withdrawals reinforced each other;
  interest-rate risk mattered, but was not the only cause.

  \slidenote{Overview:}{\href{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7tNt7WaP4iA}{the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank, from PBS}}
\end{frame}

% ---- Malkiel theorems stated up front ------------------------
\begin{frame}{Malkiel's Bond-Pricing Theorems}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  Our destination: five classical results, stated now and proved as the tools
  arrive. Each assumes an \hilite{option-free} security with fixed positive cash
  flows, holds the other contract terms fixed, and compares maturities between
  bonds priced \hilite{at par} ($c=y$).

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item Bond prices move \hilite{inversely} to bond yields.
    \item \hilite{Longer maturity} means more sensitivity to a yield change.
    \item The sensitivity gain from added maturity \hilite{diminishes} as
      maturity lengthens.
    \item Equal yield moves produce \hilite{asymmetric} price changes: a
      decrease raises the price by more than an equal increase lowers it.
    \item \hilite{Higher coupon rates} mean less sensitivity, in percentage
      terms.
  \end{enumerate}

  \slidenote{Reference:}{Malkiel (1962), \emph{Expectations, Bond Prices, and the Term Structure of Interest Rates}, QJE 76(2), 197\textendash218.}
\end{frame}

% ---- zero-coupon bill ----------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Zero-Coupon Treasury Bill}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  Start with the simplest security: a bill pays nothing until maturity, then
  returns its face value.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Let $V_P>0$ be the face value, $T>0$ the remaining maturity in years, $n$ the
  compounding intervals per year, and $y$ the nominal annual yield. With
  per-period growth factor $1+y/n$, the L2a discount factor is
  $\mathcal D_{T,0}^{-1}(y;n)=\left(1+y/n\right)^{-nT}$ and the price is:
  \[
    V_B=\underbrace{\left(1+y/n\right)^{-nT}}_{\text{\footnotesize discount}}V_P .
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \hilite{Standing assumption:} $y>0$ throughout, as in the Treasury data we
  use; we flag every result that depends on it.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Deriving the Price Sensitivity}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  Three inputs could move the price: the yield $y$, the coupon rate $c$, and the
  maturity $T$. A bill's contract fixes $c=0$, so with $V_P$ and $n$ held fixed
  only $y$ and $T$ remain in the total differential:
  \[
    dV_B
    =-\underbrace{V_P\left(1+y/n\right)^{-nT}\left(\frac{T}{1+y/n}\right)}_{V_{B,y}}dy
    \;-\;\underbrace{V_P\left(1+y/n\right)^{-nT}\bigl(n\log\left(1+y/n\right)\bigr)}_{V_{B,T}}dT .
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \hilite{Why no $dc$ term:} in the coupon-bearing family of the next section,
  $\partial V_B/\partial c=(V_P/n)\sum_{j}\mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n)>0$, strictly positive even
  at $c=0$. The derivative is not zero; the contract requires $dc=0$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Does a Change in Maturity Mean?}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  A bill promises one payment, so $T$ enters only through the exponent $nT$ and
  $\partial V_B/\partial T$ is well defined. But $dT$ can describe two different
  comparisons:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.55em}
    \item \hilite{Changing the contractual maturity}: a 6-month versus a 12-month
      bill compares securities issued on different terms. Smooth for a bill,
      where only the exponent changes; not for a coupon security, where it adds
      or removes scheduled payments.
    \item \hilite{The passage of time}: holding one bill, calendar time advances
      and remaining maturity falls, so $dT=-dt$. Smooth for a bill over the
      whole holding period; for a coupon security, only between coupon dates.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Later:}{both distinctions return when we price coupon-bearing notes and bonds.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Yield and Maturity Sensitivities}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  Read $V_{B,y}$ and $V_{B,T}$ as \hilite{magnitudes}: the derivatives are their
  negatives, hence the minus signs above. For $y>0$ both are positive:
  \[
    V_{B,y}=\underbrace{V_P(1+y/n)^{-nT}}_{=\,V_B}\underbrace{\left(\frac{T}{1+y/n}\right)}_{>\,0},
    \qquad
    V_{B,T}=\underbrace{V_P(1+y/n)^{-nT}}_{=\,V_B}\underbrace{\bigl(n\log(1+y/n)\bigr)}_{=\,g_y},
  \]
  where $g_y:=n\log(1+y/n)$ is the continuously compounded equivalent of $y$ from L2a.

  \vspace{0.1em}
  With $1+y/n>0$, the first magnitude is positive for any $T>0$. The second
  needs $y>0$: $g_y$ carries the sign of $y$, so at $y=0$ maturity stops
  mattering, and for $-n<y<0$ a longer bill would be worth \emph{more}.

  \slidenote{Careful:}{negative nominal yields have occurred in other sovereign markets. State the condition.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How Yield and Maturity Change a Bill's Price}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{Decreasing in the yield}}}
  \vspace{0.2em}

  A higher yield discounts the face value more heavily, so $V_B\downarrow$ as
  $y\uparrow$. Buy a 52-week bill at auction; if the yield moves from 2\% to
  3\%, the secondary-market price falls below what you paid.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{Decreasing in the time to maturity}}}
  \vspace{0.2em}

  At a fixed positive yield, $V_B\downarrow$ as $T\uparrow$. But while you
  \emph{hold} the bill, remaining maturity falls, $dT<0$: six months in, at an
  unchanged yield, the price is \emph{higher} than you paid, because fewer
  periods remain to discount. At $y=0$, every discount factor is one and the
  price does not move.

\end{frame}

% ---- coupon notes and bonds ----------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Coupon-Bearing Treasury Notes and Bonds}
  \vspace{-0.7em}
  Now let the security pay coupons. Let $c$ be the annual coupon rate, so each
  payment is $C:=(c/n)V_P$, and let $N:=nT$ count the remaining payment dates,
  indexed by $j=1,\ldots,N$. From L2a:
  \[
    V_B=\mathcal D_{N,0}^{-1}(y;n)\,V_P+C\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n),
    \qquad \mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n)=\left(1+y/n\right)^{-j}.
  \]
  \vspace{-1.0em}

  Holding that schedule fixed, the yield and coupon sensitivities are:
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \frac{\partial V_B}{\partial y}
        &=-\frac{1}{n(1+y/n)}\left[NV_P\,\mathcal D_{N,0}^{-1}(y;n)
          +C\sum_{j=1}^{N}j\,\mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n)\right]<0,\\[0.2em]
      \frac{\partial V_B}{\partial c}
        &=\frac{V_P}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n)>0 .
    \end{aligned}
  \]

  \slidenote{Careful:}{$N$ is a discrete schedule index. Differentiating a finite sum whose upper limit is $nT$ with respect to a smooth $T$ is not a valid total differential.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How Bond Features Change Its Price}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.55em}
    \item \hilite{Decreasing in the yield}: now \emph{both} the coupons and the
      final principal are discounted more heavily, so $V_B\downarrow$ as
      $y\uparrow$.
    \item \hilite{Increasing in the coupon rate, but not proportional to it}:
      $\partial V_B/\partial c$ does not depend on $c$, and the
      discounted par payment survives even at $c=0$. Doubling the coupon
      doubles the coupon stream, not the price.
    \item \hilite{Maturity does not set the price direction by itself}: a
      \hilite{discount bond} ($c<y$) drifts up toward par as maturity
      shortens, a \hilite{premium bond} ($c>y$) drifts down toward par, and a
      \hilite{par bond} ($c=y$) sits at par on a coupon date.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Careful:}{Malkiel's maturity result is about \emph{sensitivity to a yield change}, not about the price level.}
\end{frame}

% ---- duration and convexity ----------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Duration and Convexity}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  Derivatives give the direction of a price change; risk management also needs
  its size, in units comparable across positions.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  We assume an \hilite{option-free} security: its promised cash flows do not
  change when yields change. Conventional noncallable Treasuries qualify;
  callable, inflation-indexed, and mortgage-backed securities do not.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Let $X_j\ge0$ be the dollar cash flow at period $j=1,\ldots,N$, with at least
  one $X_j>0$. One price formula covers bills and coupon securities alike:
  \[
    V_B(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_j\,\mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n).
  \]
  A note pays $X_j=C$ for $j<N$ and $X_N=C+V_P$; a bill pays $X_j=0$ for $j<N$
  and $X_N=V_P$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two Derivatives, One Shape}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  Differentiate the price twice with respect to the yield, holding the cash flows
  $X_j$ and the schedule length $N$ fixed:
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \frac{dV_B}{dy}&=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{N}j\,X_j\left(1+y/n\right)^{-j-1}<0,\\[0.2em]
      \frac{d^2V_B}{dy^2}&=\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}j(j+1)X_j\left(1+y/n\right)^{-j-2}>0 .
    \end{aligned}
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  The strict signs follow from $1+y/n>0$ and at least one $X_j>0$: no term of
  either sum is negative, and at least one term of each is positive.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  So the price-yield curve is \hilite{decreasing} and \hilite{strictly convex}.
  The second fact, \hilite{positive convexity}, is what makes the price response
  asymmetric.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Two Risk Measures}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  Dividing each derivative by the price $V_B(y)>0$ removes position size and
  leaves two local risk measures, the \hilite{modified duration} and the
  \hilite{convexity}:
  \[
    D_{\mathrm{mod}}(y):=-\frac{1}{V_B(y)}\frac{dV_B}{dy},
    \qquad
    K(y):=\frac{1}{V_B(y)}\frac{d^2V_B}{dy^2} .
  \]
  Both are positive by the signs just established. With $y$ a decimal per year,
  $D_{\mathrm{mod}}$ has units of years and $K$ units of years squared.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \hilite{Modified is not Macaulay.} The \hilite{Macaulay duration}
  $D_{\mathrm{mac}}$ is the present-value-weighted average time to payment, and
  $D_{\mathrm{mod}}=D_{\mathrm{mac}}/(1+y/n)$. For a bill, every dollar arrives at
  maturity, so $D_{\mathrm{mac}}=T$ and $D_{\mathrm{mod}}=T/(1+y/n)$: exactly the
  ratio $V_{B,y}/V_B$ derived earlier.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Duration Gives the Slope; Convexity Adds Curvature}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  A second-order Taylor expansion about the current yield, divided by the price,
  turns the two measures into a repricing estimate. For a shock $\Delta y$, with
  $\Delta V_B:=V_B(y+\Delta y)-V_B(y)$:
  \[
    \frac{\Delta V_B}{V_B}\approx
    \underbrace{-D_{\mathrm{mod}}\,\Delta y}_{\text{\footnotesize slope}}
    +\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}K(\Delta y)^2}_{\text{\footnotesize curvature}} .
  \]
  Because $K>0$, the curvature term is positive for a shock of \emph{either} sign:
  it adds to the gain and subtracts from the loss.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \hilite{The convention.} $\Delta y$ is a decimal change in the annual yield. One
  \hilite{basis point} is $10^{-4}$, so a 50-basis-point shock is
  $\Delta y=0.0050$.

  \slidenote{Careful:}{a 50-basis-point move in the \emph{policy rate} does not imply $\Delta y=0.0050$ for a given security.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimate a Bond's Price Change}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  Take a note with $D_{\mathrm{mod}}=7.2$ years and $K=65$ years$^2$, and shock
  its own yield by $+50$ basis points, so $\Delta y=0.0050$:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
    \item slope term: $-7.2\times0.0050=-0.0360$
    \item curvature term: $\tfrac{1}{2}\times65\times(0.0050)^2=+0.0008$
    \item estimate: a $\hilite{-3.52\%}$ price change, where the straight line
      alone would have said $-3.60\%$
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  Both measures are \hilite{local}: derivatives at the current yield, so the
  estimate degrades as $\Delta y$ grows. For moves the size of those that hurt
  SVB, reprice the whole schedule at the new yield rather than extrapolating
  from duration.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Duration Is Used For}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  Beyond the single-security estimate, duration is useful for two reasons:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.55em}
    \item \hilite{A common scale}: securities with different coupons, maturities,
      and prices land on one scale, so the interest-rate risk of a 2-year note
      and a 10-year note can be compared directly.
    \item \hilite{A portfolio measure}: the duration of a portfolio is the
      market-value-weighted average of the durations of the securities it holds,
      so the \$500 million portfolio from the scenario reprices in one step
      rather than security by security.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  The same properties support risk management: offsetting positions can bring a
  portfolio's net duration near zero, while a bank that funds long-duration
  securities with short-duration deposits, as SVB did, carries a duration
  mismatch.
\end{frame}

% ---- Malkiel -------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Revisiting Malkiel's Theorems}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  The machinery is in place: return to the five theorems from the start of the
  lecture. Ground rules: option-free, fixed positive cash flows, other terms
  held fixed, and maturity comparisons between bonds \hilite{selling at par},
  $c=y$. The par restriction is not a technicality.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  Substituting $C=(c/n)V_P$ and summing the geometric series puts the coupon
  bond in closed form. Differentiating that at fixed $c$, then setting
  $c=y$, gives the modified duration of a par bond:
  \[
    \frac{V_B}{V_P}=\frac{c}{y}+\left(1-\frac{c}{y}\right)\left(1+y/n\right)^{-N}
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    D_{\mathrm{mod}}(N)=\frac{1-\left(1+y/n\right)^{-N}}{y} .
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \hilite{Order matters.} Differentiate at fixed $c$, \emph{then} set $c=y$:
  the reverse differentiates along the par locus, where the price is pinned at
  $V_P$, and returns zero. Assume $y>0$ throughout.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Malkiel's Theorems 1, 2, and 3}
  \vspace{0.15em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
    \item \hilite{Theorem 1}, prices move inversely to yields: this is the sign
      $dV_B/dy<0$, or equivalently $D_{\mathrm{mod}}>0$. Every promised payment is
      positive, so raising $y$ discounts each more heavily.
    \item \hilite{Theorem 2}, longer maturity increases yield sensitivity:
      $D_{\mathrm{mod}}(N)=\bigl(1-(1+y/n)^{-N}\bigr)/y$ is strictly increasing in
      $N$. A par bond starts at $V_B=V_P$ at every maturity, so the dollar
      sensitivity $-dV_B/dy=V_PD_{\mathrm{mod}}(N)$ rises too: starting at par
      aligns the percentage and dollar rankings.
    \item \hilite{Theorem 3}, the gain diminishes: one extra period raises
      modified duration by
      $D_{\mathrm{mod}}(N+1)-D_{\mathrm{mod}}(N)=(1+y/n)^{-N-1}/n$, and successive
      increments shrink by the constant factor $(1+y/n)^{-1}<1$, so
      $D_{\mathrm{mod}}$ climbs toward the ceiling $1/y$.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Note:}{$1/y$ is the modified duration of a \emph{perpetuity}, a security that pays its coupon forever and never repays principal.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Malkiel's Theorems 4 and 5}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item \hilite{Theorem 4}, price movements are asymmetric: a yield decrease
      raises the price by more than an equal increase lowers it, from the same
      starting yield. The reason is \hilite{positive convexity}. In the
      second-order estimate the slope term flips sign with the shock while the
      curvature term $\tfrac12K(\Delta y)^2$ stays positive. Nonlinearity alone
      would not do it: a concave curve gives the opposite asymmetry.
    \item \hilite{Theorem 5}, higher-coupon bonds are less sensitive: this is a
      statement about \emph{percentage} sensitivity, and it needs $N\ge2$. A
      larger coupon moves present value to earlier dates, lowering the weighted
      average payment time and so lowering $D_{\mathrm{mod}}$. In \emph{dollars}
      it runs the other way: more cash flow means a larger $-dV_B/dy$.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Excluded edge:}{at $N=1$ everything arrives on one date and $D_{\mathrm{mod}}=1/\left[n(1+y/n)\right]$, however large the coupon.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Malkiel Compares Bonds at Par}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  Outside the par comparison, percentage and dollar sensitivities can rank
  securities differently.
  For a zero-coupon bill, $D_{\mathrm{mod}}=T/(1+y/n)$ grows without bound, but the
  dollar sensitivity is a product of a growing and a shrinking factor:
  \[
    -\frac{dV_B}{dy}=V_P\,\underbrace{\frac{T}{1+y/n}}_{\text{\footnotesize growing}}
    \underbrace{\left(1+y/n\right)^{-nT}}_{\text{\footnotesize shrinking}} .
  \]
  So it rises, peaks, and falls. With $V_P=100$, $n=1$, $y=5\%$ it peaks near
  $T\approx20.5$ years at about 718 USD per unit of yield, then falls to about
  541 by $T=40$.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  A 40-year bill is far more sensitive than a 20-year bill in \hilite{percentage}
  terms and \emph{less} sensitive in \hilite{dollars}. Only the par comparison
  guarantees the two rankings agree.

  \slidenote{Example:}{\dimmed{CHEME-5660-L2b-Example-Treasury-Duration-Convexity-Fall-2026.ipynb}, which tests Theorems 4 and 5.}
\end{frame}

% ---- yield curve ---------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{From One Yield to the Yield Curve}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \hilite{Yield to maturity} is the single internal rate that reproduces one
  security's price from its promised cash flows: useful for quoting and
  comparison, but not the full term structure.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  A \hilite{spot rate} applies to one payment date, so a spot curve supplies one
  discount factor per date. A \hilite{par yield} is the coupon rate that makes a
  hypothetical security trade at par under that curve; the published Treasury
  yield curve is a \hilite{par yield curve}. Writing $D(0,t_j)$ for the
  time-0 discount factor for maturity $t_j$, the curve-based price is:
  \[
    V_B=\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_j\,D(0,t_j).
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  Level, slope, and curvature describe how discounting varies by maturity; by
  themselves they do not identify a forecast of future short rates.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Recession Predictor?}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  In most periods long-maturity yields exceed short-maturity yields, so the par
  yield curve slopes upward. When that ordering flips, the curve is
  \hilite{inverted}.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  Inversions have a famous track record: an inverted long-minus-short spread,
  such as the \hilite{10-year minus 3-month} yield, has preceded every U.S.
  recession of the past half century.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  The record comes with qualifiers: false positives occur, lead times run from
  months to years, and the reading depends on the maturity pair. The optional
  par-yield example computes the spread from Treasury data and asks what it
  says now.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}
  This lecture developed the core tools for describing and managing the
  interest-rate risk of fixed-income securities.

  \vspace{0.45em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{Key takeaways}}}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item \textbf{\ink{Bond prices decrease as yields increase.}} For a fixed
      stream of positive cash flows, the price-yield curve is decreasing and
      convex.
    \item \textbf{\ink{Duration and convexity measure local price sensitivity.}}
      Duration is the first-order response to a yield change; convexity corrects
      the straight line for curvature.
    \item \textbf{\ink{Malkiel's theorems hold under stated assumptions.}} The
      par restriction is what aligns the percentage and dollar sensitivity
      rankings.
    \item \textbf{\ink{One yield is not a yield curve.}} Spot rates value each
      payment date, and par yields restate that curve as coupon rates.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
