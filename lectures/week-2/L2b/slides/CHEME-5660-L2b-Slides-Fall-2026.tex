\begin{frame}{Concept Review: Are Treasury Securities Really Risk-Free?}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \hilite{No.} Treasuries carry low default risk because they are obligations of
  the U.S. government, which is why the rate you declare is so often taken from
  them. That label is conditional. Two of the surviving risks matter today.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
    \item \hilite{Default risk}: the issuer fails to pay. The United States has
      never repudiated its debt, though a 1979 operational failure did delay
      payment on some maturing bills. Other sovereigns have defaulted outright:
      Greece restructured its privately held debt in 2012, Russia defaulted in
      1998.
    \item \hilite{Interest-rate risk}: if required yields change, the
      secondary-market value of a fixed promised cash flow changes. An
      institution that marks positions to market is affected even without a sale.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Today:}{interest-rate risk is the one we can compute. Its size depends on maturity, coupon, yield, and the shock.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Case Study: Interest Rate Risk and Silicon Valley Bank}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  SVB held a large portfolio of \hilite{long-maturity fixed-rate} government
  securities bought while rates were low. As the Federal Reserve raised rates
  through 2022 and 2023, the value of those securities fell.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
    \item The losses were unrealized, so a hold-to-maturity story obscured them.
    \item In early March 2023 uninsured depositors withdrew funds. SVB announced
      a capital raise to cover realized bond losses, and the bank failed.
    \item The FDIC took control on March 10, 2023, with roughly \$209 billion in
      assets: then the second largest failure of an insured bank on record, now
      the third.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  Rate sensitivity was one component of a coupled balance-sheet and liquidity
  problem, not a complete explanation by itself.

  \slidenote{Overview:}{\href{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7tNt7WaP4iA}{the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank, from PBS}}
\end{frame}

% ---- zero-coupon bill ----------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Zero-Coupon Treasury Bill}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  Start with the simplest security: a bill that pays nothing until maturity and
  then returns its face value. It is the hydrogen atom of fixed income.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Let $V_P>0$ be the face value, $T>0$ the remaining maturity in years, $n$ the
  number of compounding intervals per year, and $y$ the nominal annual yield used
  to discount the single promised payment. The per-period factor $1+y/n$ is the
  growth factor over one interval, so the L2a discount factor is
  $\mathcal D_{T,0}^{-1}(y;n)=\left(1+y/n\right)^{-nT}$. The price is given by:
  \[
    V_B=\underbrace{\left(1+y/n\right)^{-nT}}_{\text{\footnotesize discount}}V_P .
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \hilite{Standing assumption:} $y>0$ throughout this lecture, as it has been for
  the Treasury data we use. Several results below fail without it, and we flag
  each place where it does real work.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Deriving the Price Sensitivity}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  Three quantities could move the price: the yield $y$, the annual coupon rate
  $c$, and the maturity $T$. A bill's contract fixes $c=0$ permanently,
  so $dc=0$ and that channel is closed. Holding $V_P$ and $n$ fixed, the
  remaining two channels give the total differential:
  \[
    dV_B
    =-\underbrace{V_P\left(1+y/n\right)^{-nT}\left(\frac{T}{1+y/n}\right)}_{V_{B,y}}dy
    \;-\;\underbrace{V_P\left(1+y/n\right)^{-nT}\bigl(n\log\left(1+y/n\right)\bigr)}_{V_{B,T}}dT .
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \hilite{Stated carefully:} the coupon channel does \emph{not} drop out because a
  bill pays no coupons. The coupon-bearing family of the next section has
  $\partial V_B/\partial c=(V_P/n)\sum_{j}\mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n)>0$, which stays strictly
  positive even when evaluated at $c=0$. A bill is not insensitive to
  coupons: it is a security whose coupon cannot move.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Is the Maturity Channel Legitimate?}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  A bill promises one payment, so its price depends on $T$ only through the
  exponent $nT$. No intermediate payment can be added or removed, so
  $\partial V_B/\partial T$ is well defined here. But two experiments hide behind
  the symbol $dT$:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.55em}
    \item \hilite{Changing the contractual maturity}: comparing a 6-month bill
      with a 12-month bill compares two securities issued on different terms.
      Smooth for a bill, because only the exponent changes. Not smooth for a
      coupon security, where it adds or removes scheduled payments.
    \item \hilite{The passage of time}: holding one bill, calendar time $t$
      advances while remaining maturity falls, so $dT=-dt$ along the position.
      Smooth for a bill over the whole holding period. For a coupon security,
      smooth only between coupon dates.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Later:}{both distinctions return when we price coupon-bearing notes and bonds.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Two Sensitivity Magnitudes}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  Read $V_{B,y}$ and $V_{B,T}$ as \hilite{magnitudes}, not the derivatives
  themselves: the derivatives are their negatives, which is why the minus signs
  appear above. Under the standing assumption $y>0$, both are positive:
  \[
    V_{B,y}=\underbrace{V_P(1+y/n)^{-nT}}_{=\,V_B}\underbrace{\left(\frac{T}{1+y/n}\right)}_{>\,0},
    \qquad
    V_{B,T}=\underbrace{V_P(1+y/n)^{-nT}}_{=\,V_B}\underbrace{\bigl(n\log(1+y/n)\bigr)}_{=\,g_y},
  \]
  where $g_y:=n\log(1+y/n)$ is the continuously compounded equivalent of $y$ from L2a.

  \vspace{0.1em}
  Given a well-posed price, $1+y/n>0$, the first magnitude is positive for any
  $T>0$. The second needs $y>0$: $g_y$ carries the sign of $y$, so at $y=0$
  maturity stops mattering, and for $-n<y<0$ a longer bill would be worth
  \emph{more} and trade above par.

  \slidenote{Careful:}{negative nominal yields have occurred in other sovereign markets. State the condition.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What This Says to Us: the Bill}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{Decreasing in the yield}}}
  \vspace{0.2em}

  Holding maturity fixed, a higher yield discounts the face value more heavily,
  so $V_B\downarrow$ as $y\uparrow$. Buy a 52-week bill at auction; if the yield
  moves from 2\% to 3\%, the secondary-market price falls below what you paid.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{Decreasing in the time to maturity}}}
  \vspace{0.2em}

  Holding the yield fixed at a positive value, $V_B\downarrow$ as $T\uparrow$. But
  while you \emph{hold} the bill, calendar time advances and the remaining
  maturity falls, so $dT<0$: six months in, with the yield unchanged, the price is
  \emph{higher} than you paid, because fewer periods remain to discount. At $y=0$
  exactly, every discount factor is one and the price does not move at all.

  \slidenote{Optional extension:}{\dimmed{advanced/malkiel/}, which develops the full maturity comparison on Treasury bill auction data.}
\end{frame}

% ---- coupon notes and bonds ----------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Coupon-Bearing Treasury Notes and Bonds}
  \vspace{-0.7em}
  Now let the security pay coupons. Let $c$ be the annual coupon rate, so each
  payment is $C:=(c/n)V_P$, and let $N:=nT$ count the remaining payment dates,
  indexed by $j=1,\ldots,N$. From L2a:
  \[
    V_B=\mathcal D_{N,0}^{-1}(y;n)\,V_P+C\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n),
    \qquad \mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n)=\left(1+y/n\right)^{-j}.
  \]
  \vspace{-1.0em}

  Holding that schedule fixed, the yield and coupon sensitivities are:
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \frac{\partial V_B}{\partial y}
        &=-\frac{1}{n(1+y/n)}\left[NV_P\,\mathcal D_{N,0}^{-1}(y;n)
          +C\sum_{j=1}^{N}j\,\mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n)\right]<0,\\[0.2em]
      \frac{\partial V_B}{\partial c}
        &=\frac{V_P}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n)>0 .
    \end{aligned}
  \]

  \slidenote{Careful:}{$N$ is a discrete schedule index. Differentiating a finite sum whose upper limit is $nT$ with respect to a smooth $T$ is not a valid total differential.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What This Says to Us: Notes and Bonds}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.55em}
    \item \hilite{Decreasing in the yield}: now \emph{both} the coupons and the
      final principal are discounted more heavily, so $V_B\downarrow$ as
      $y\uparrow$.
    \item \hilite{Increasing in the coupon rate, but not proportional to it}:
      $\partial V_B/\partial c$ does not depend on $c$, and the
      discounted par payment survives even at $c=0$. Doubling the coupon
      doubles the coupon stream, not the price.
    \item \hilite{Maturity does not set the price direction by itself}: a
      \hilite{discount bond} ($c<y$) drifts up toward par as maturity
      shortens, a \hilite{premium bond} ($c>y$) drifts down toward par, and a
      \hilite{par bond} ($c=y$) sits at par on a coupon date.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Careful:}{Malkiel's maturity result is about \emph{sensitivity to a yield change}, not about the price level.}
\end{frame}

% ---- duration and convexity ----------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Duration and Convexity}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  The derivatives so far answer \emph{which direction}. Risk management needs
  \emph{how much}, in units comparable across positions of different size.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Everything here assumes an \hilite{option-free} security: one whose promised
  cash flows are fixed in advance and do not change when yields change.
  Conventional noncallable Treasuries qualify; callable, inflation-indexed, and
  mortgage-backed securities do not.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Let $N$ be the number of remaining periods and $X_j\ge0$ the dollar cash flow at
  period $j$, with at least one $X_j>0$. Then one expression covers both
  securities:
  \[
    V_B(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_j\,\mathcal D_{j,0}^{-1}(y;n).
  \]
  A note pays $X_j=C$ for $j<N$ and $X_N=C+V_P$; a bill pays $X_j=0$ for $j<N$
  and $X_N=V_P$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two Derivatives, One Shape}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  Differentiate the price twice with respect to the yield, holding the cash flows
  $X_j$ and the schedule length $N$ fixed:
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \frac{dV_B}{dy}&=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{N}j\,X_j\left(1+y/n\right)^{-j-1}<0,\\[0.2em]
      \frac{d^2V_B}{dy^2}&=\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}j(j+1)X_j\left(1+y/n\right)^{-j-2}>0 .
    \end{aligned}
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  The strict signs follow from $1+y/n>0$ and from at least one $X_j$ being strictly
  positive: no term of either sum is negative, and at least one term of each is
  positive.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  So the price-yield curve is \hilite{decreasing} and \hilite{strictly convex}.
  That second fact is called \hilite{positive convexity}, and it is what makes the
  price response asymmetric.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Two Risk Measures}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  Dividing each derivative by the price removes the size of the position and
  leaves two local risk measures. With $V_B(y)>0$ the price at yield $y$, the
  \hilite{modified duration} and the \hilite{convexity} are given by:
  \[
    D_{\mathrm{mod}}(y):=-\frac{1}{V_B(y)}\frac{dV_B}{dy},
    \qquad
    K(y):=\frac{1}{V_B(y)}\frac{d^2V_B}{dy^2} .
  \]
  Both are positive by the signs just established. With $y$ a decimal per year,
  $D_{\mathrm{mod}}$ has units of years and $K$ units of years squared.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \hilite{Modified is not Macaulay.} The \hilite{Macaulay duration}
  $D_{\mathrm{mac}}$ is the present-value-weighted average time to payment, and
  $D_{\mathrm{mod}}=D_{\mathrm{mac}}/(1+y/n)$. For a bill, every dollar arrives at
  maturity, so $D_{\mathrm{mac}}=T$ and $D_{\mathrm{mod}}=T/(1+y/n)$, which is exactly
  the ratio $V_{B,y}/V_B$ we derived earlier.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Using Them: the Second-Order Price Approximation}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  A second-order Taylor expansion of $V_B(y)$ about the current yield, divided by
  the price, turns the two measures into a repricing estimate. For a yield change
  $\Delta y$, with $\Delta V_B:=V_B(y+\Delta y)-V_B(y)$, the approximate
  fractional price change is given by:
  \[
    \frac{\Delta V_B}{V_B}\approx
    \underbrace{-D_{\mathrm{mod}}\,\Delta y}_{\text{\footnotesize slope}}
    +\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}K(\Delta y)^2}_{\text{\footnotesize curvature}} .
  \]
  Because $K>0$, the curvature term is positive for a shock of \emph{either} sign:
  it adds to the gain and subtracts from the loss.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \hilite{The convention.} $\Delta y$ is a decimal change in the annual yield. One
  \hilite{basis point} is $10^{-4}$, so a 50-basis-point shock is
  $\Delta y=0.0050$.

  \slidenote{Careful:}{a 50-basis-point move in the \emph{policy rate} does not imply $\Delta y=0.0050$ for a given security.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Using Them: a Worked Repricing Estimate}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  Take a note with $D_{\mathrm{mod}}=7.2$ years and $K=65$ years$^2$, and shock
  its own yield by $+50$ basis points, so $\Delta y=0.0050$:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
    \item slope term: $-7.2\times0.0050=-0.0360$
    \item curvature term: $\tfrac{1}{2}\times65\times(0.0050)^2=+0.0008$
    \item estimate: a $\hilite{-3.52\%}$ price change, where the straight line
      alone would have said $-3.60\%$
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  Both measures are \hilite{local}: they are derivatives at the current yield, so
  the estimate degrades as $\Delta y$ grows. For the multiple-percentage-point
  moves of 2022 and 2023 that hurt SVB, the honest calculation is to reprice the
  whole schedule at the new yield, not to extrapolate from duration.
\end{frame}

% ---- Malkiel -------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Connection to Malkiel's Bond Pricing Theorems}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  Two ground rules. Every theorem assumes an option-free security with fixed
  positive cash flows and holds the other contract terms fixed. And the maturity
  theorems compare bonds \hilite{selling at par}, $c=y$, measuring changes
  from that starting point. That restriction is not a technicality.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  Substituting $C=(c/n)V_P$ and summing the geometric series puts the coupon
  bond in closed form. Differentiating that at fixed $c$, then setting
  $c=y$, gives the modified duration of a par bond:
  \[
    \frac{V_B}{V_P}=\frac{c}{y}+\left(1-\frac{c}{y}\right)\left(1+y/n\right)^{-N}
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    D_{\mathrm{mod}}(N)=\frac{1-\left(1+y/n\right)^{-N}}{y} .
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \hilite{Order matters.} Differentiate at fixed $c$, and only \emph{then}
  set $c=y$. Setting $c=y$ first differentiates along the par locus,
  where the price is pinned at $V_P$, and returns zero. Assume $y>0$ throughout.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Malkiel's Theorems 1, 2, and 3}
  \vspace{0.15em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
    \item \hilite{Theorem 1}, prices move inversely to yields: this is the sign
      $dV_B/dy<0$, or equivalently $D_{\mathrm{mod}}>0$. Every promised payment is
      positive, so raising $y$ discounts each more heavily.
    \item \hilite{Theorem 2}, longer maturity increases yield sensitivity:
      $D_{\mathrm{mod}}(N)=\bigl(1-(1+y/n)^{-N}\bigr)/y$ is strictly increasing in
      $N$. A par bond starts at $V_B=V_P$ at every maturity, so the dollar
      sensitivity $-dV_B/dy=V_PD_{\mathrm{mod}}(N)$ rises too: starting at par
      aligns the percentage and dollar rankings.
    \item \hilite{Theorem 3}, the gain diminishes: one extra period raises
      modified duration by
      $D_{\mathrm{mod}}(N+1)-D_{\mathrm{mod}}(N)=(1+y/n)^{-N-1}/n$, and successive
      increments shrink by the constant factor $(1+y/n)^{-1}<1$, so
      $D_{\mathrm{mod}}$ climbs toward the ceiling $1/y$.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Note:}{$1/y$ is the modified duration of a \emph{perpetuity}, a security that pays its coupon forever and never repays principal.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Malkiel's Theorems 4 and 5}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item \hilite{Theorem 4}, price movements are asymmetric: a yield decrease
      raises the price by more than an equal increase lowers it, from the same
      starting yield. The reason is \hilite{positive convexity}. In the
      second-order estimate the slope term flips sign with the shock while the
      curvature term $\tfrac12K(\Delta y)^2$ stays positive. Nonlinearity alone
      would not do it: a concave curve gives the opposite asymmetry.
    \item \hilite{Theorem 5}, higher-coupon bonds are less sensitive: this is a
      statement about \emph{percentage} sensitivity, and it needs $N\ge2$. A
      larger coupon moves present value to earlier dates, lowering the weighted
      average payment time and so lowering $D_{\mathrm{mod}}$. In \emph{dollars}
      it runs the other way: more cash flow means a larger $-dV_B/dy$.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Excluded edge:}{at $N=1$ everything arrives on one date and $D_{\mathrm{mod}}=1/\left[n(1+y/n)\right]$, however large the coupon.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the Par Restriction Is Doing}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  Outside the par comparison, the percentage and dollar statements part company.
  For a zero-coupon bill, $D_{\mathrm{mod}}=T/(1+y/n)$ grows without bound, but the
  dollar sensitivity is a product of a growing and a shrinking factor:
  \[
    -\frac{dV_B}{dy}=V_P\,\underbrace{\frac{T}{1+y/n}}_{\text{\footnotesize growing}}
    \underbrace{\left(1+y/n\right)^{-nT}}_{\text{\footnotesize shrinking}} .
  \]
  So it rises, peaks, and falls. With $V_P=100$, $n=1$, $y=5\%$ it peaks near
  $T\approx20.5$ years at about 718 USD per unit of yield, then falls to about
  541 by $T=40$.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  A 40-year bill is far more sensitive than a 20-year bill in \hilite{percentage}
  terms and \emph{less} sensitive in \hilite{dollars}. Only the par comparison
  guarantees the two rankings agree.

  \slidenote{Example:}{\dimmed{CHEME-5660-L2b-Example-Treasury-Duration-Convexity-Fall-2026.ipynb}, which tests Theorems 4 and 5.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{From One Yield to the Yield Curve}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \hilite{Yield to maturity} is the single internal rate that reproduces one
  security's price from its promised cash flows. It is useful for quoting and
  comparison, but it is not the full term structure.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  A \hilite{spot rate} applies to one payment date, so a spot curve supplies one
  discount factor per date. A \hilite{par yield} is the coupon rate that makes a
  hypothetical security trade at par under that curve. Writing $D(0,t_j)$ for the
  time-0 discount factor for maturity $t_j$, the curve-based price is:
  \[
    V_B=\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_j\,D(0,t_j).
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  Curve level, slope, and curvature describe how discounting varies by maturity.
  They do not, by themselves, identify a unique forecast of future short rates.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}
  This lecture developed the core tools used to describe and manage the
  interest-rate risk of fixed-income securities.

  \vspace{0.45em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{Key takeaways}}}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item \textbf{\ink{Bond prices decrease as yields increase.}} For a fixed
      stream of positive cash flows, the price-yield relationship is decreasing
      and convex.
    \item \textbf{\ink{Duration and convexity measure local price sensitivity.}}
      Duration gives the first-order response to a yield change, and convexity
      corrects that straight line for curvature.
    \item \textbf{\ink{One yield is not a yield curve.}} Yield to maturity
      summarizes one security with a single rate, while spot rates and discount
      factors value cash flows at specific maturities and par yields are implied
      by that term structure.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Together these form a practical fixed-income risk toolkit.
\end{frame}
