% ==== market structure =======================================
\begin{frame}{Exchanges, Stocks, and ETFs}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  Before measuring an equity return, separate the \hilite{marketplace} from the
  \hilite{securities} traded there. These objects interact, but they are not
  interchangeable.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.6em}
    \item An \hilite{exchange} is an organized venue that matches orders to buy
      and sell securities.
    \item An \hilite{order} is the instruction a broker submits on an investor's
      behalf; order types differ in how they set the price.
    \item A \hilite{stock} is a residual ownership claim on a corporation.
    \item An \hilite{exchange-traded fund}, or ETF, is a security whose shares
      represent an interest in a portfolio and trade during the day.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Today:}{a market price is the price available at a particular time and trading venue.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{\large Exchanges Connect Buyers and Sellers}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  An exchange brings orders together and supports \hilite{price discovery}, the
  process by which trading establishes a market price, and \hilite{liquidity},
  the ability to trade without moving that price excessively.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
    \item Exchanges impose listing standards and trading rules; after execution,
      separate clearing and settlement infrastructure completes the transfer of
      securities and funds.
    \item U.S. equities trade across more than a dozen competing exchanges; the
      three largest are NYSE, NASDAQ, and Cboe BZX.
    \item A broker may route one order to a venue or divide it among venues,
      subject to available prices, liquidity, and the order's instructions.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Source:}{\href{https://www.sec.gov/about/divisions-offices/division-trading-markets/national-securities-exchanges}{SEC list of registered national securities exchanges}; the list also includes options-only venues.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Orders Are Instructions to Trade}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  An order names the security, the direction (buy or sell), the quantity, and
  how the price may be set. The common order types differ in how they trade off
  \hilite{immediacy} against \hilite{price control}.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.6em}
    \item \textbf{\ink{Market order:}} executes as soon as possible at the best
      price currently available. Execution is nearly certain; the price is not.
    \item \textbf{\ink{Limit order:}} executes only at a stated limit price or
      better: at or below it for a buy, at or above it for a sell. The price is
      controlled; execution is not guaranteed.
    \item \textbf{\ink{Stop and stop-limit orders:}} wait until the price reaches
      a trigger level, then become a market order or a limit order.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{More order types:}{\href{https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/how-stock-markets-work/types-orders}{Investor.gov, Types of Orders}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{From Order Books to the NBBO}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  Limit orders that do not execute immediately rest on the venue's
  \hilite{order book}, the collection of outstanding buy and sell limit orders
  for a security.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
    \item The highest resting buy price is the venue's displayed \hilite{bid},
      the lowest resting sell price is its displayed \hilite{ask}, and the
      difference is the bid-ask spread.
    \item An arriving market order to buy trades at the ask; one to sell trades
      at the bid.
    \item Each venue keeps its own book, so each venue displays its own bid and
      ask. The \hilite{National Best Bid and Offer}, or NBBO, reports the
      highest displayed bid and the lowest displayed ask across the national
      market system.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  A broker must fill a customer order at the national best price or better: a
  buyer pays no more than the NBBO ask, and a seller receives no less than the
  NBBO bid.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Stocks Are Claims on Firms}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  A stock, also called a share or \hilite{equity security}, represents fractional
  ownership in a corporation. It is a residual claim, not a promise of fixed
  repayment.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{What the shareholder may receive}}}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
    \item Voting rights and cash distributions such as dividends.
    \item A claim on remaining assets only after contractual creditors are paid.
    \item A capital gain if another investor later pays a higher share price.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Limited liability ordinarily caps the shareholder's loss at the invested
  amount. It does not protect the share price from falling to zero.

  \slidenote{Source:}{\href{https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/stocks}{U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, Stocks}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{ETFs Are Claims on Portfolios}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  An ETF pools assets into a portfolio and issues shares that trade on an
  exchange throughout the day. Each share represents an interest in that
  portfolio.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.55em}
    \item A broad-market fund such as SPY, which tracks the Standard \& Poor's
      500 index, can provide diversified exposure; sector, leveraged, inverse,
      and single-stock ETFs may be highly concentrated.
    \item The \hilite{net asset value}, or NAV, is the fund's assets minus its
      liabilities, divided by shares outstanding.
    \item An ETF has both a market price and a NAV per share. They are usually
      close, but the ETF can trade at a premium or discount to NAV.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  Like a stock, an ETF produces an observable sequence of market prices.
\end{frame}

% ==== reward =================================================
\begin{frame}{Reward}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  We measure price reward with the \hilite{continuously compounded growth rate},
  denoted by $g$. Let $S_{j-1}^{(i)}>0$ and $S_j^{(i)}>0$ be consecutive prices
  for firm $i$, separated by $\Delta t_j>0$ years. The price evolution is:
  \[
    S_j^{(i)}=S_{j-1}^{(i)}\exp\!\left(g_{j,j-1}^{(i)}\Delta t_j\right).
  \]

  Solving for the rate gives:
  \[
    g_{j,j-1}^{(i)}=
    \underbrace{\frac{1}{\Delta t_j}\log\!\left(\frac{S_j^{(i)}}{S_{j-1}^{(i)}}\right)}_{\text{\footnotesize growth rate}}.
  \]

  The growth rate has units of inverse years. Its sign records whether the price
  increased, decreased, or remained unchanged. The notebook computes $g$ from
  closing-price series, so dividends and other cash distributions are not
  included.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Growth Rates and Log Returns}
  Let $g_t$ be the continuously compounded growth rate over an interval of
  length $\Delta t_t>0$ years. The corresponding \hilite{log return} $r_t$ is
  dimensionless and is given by:
  \[
    \underbrace{r_t=(\Delta t_t)g_t}_{\text{\footnotesize log return}}
    =\log\!\left(\frac{S_t}{S_{t-1}}\right).
  \]

  Log returns add across adjacent intervals. For positive prices
  $S_0,S_1,\ldots,S_T$, the cumulative log return is:
  \[
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}r_t
    =\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\Delta t_t)g_t}_{\text{\footnotesize time-weighted growth}}
    =\log\!\left(\frac{S_T}{S_0}\right).
  \]

  Use $g$ to compare annualized rates. Convert to $r$ before aggregating across
  time.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Excess Growth Compares an Asset with a Benchmark}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  Let $g_t$ be the equity growth rate and let $g_{f,t}$ be a declared benchmark
  growth rate over the same interval of length $\Delta t_t$ years. The
  \hilite{excess growth rate} and excess log return are given by:
  \[
    \underbrace{\bar g_t=g_t-g_{f,t}}_{\text{\footnotesize excess growth}},
    \qquad
    \underbrace{\bar r_t=(\Delta t_t)\bar g_t}_{\text{\footnotesize excess log return}}.
  \]

  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.55em}
    \item $\bar g_t>0$: the equity price grew faster than the benchmark.
    \item $\bar g_t<0$: the equity price grew more slowly than the benchmark.
    \item $\bar g_t=0$: both produced the same growth rate over the interval.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Careful:}{the benchmark must use the same currency, horizon, and time units. A positive excess rate is not a guarantee of positive wealth.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Algorithm}
  Let $\mathcal D_i=\{S_1^{(i)},\ldots,S_T^{(i)}\}$ be a positive price series
  for firm $i$, let $\Delta t>0$ be the interval in years, and let $g_f$ be the
  benchmark growth rate.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
    \item For $t=2,\ldots,T$, look up $S_{t-1}^{(i)}$ and $S_t^{(i)}$.
    \item Compute the excess growth observation:
      \[
        \bar g_t^{(i)}\gets
        \underbrace{\frac{1}{\Delta t}\log\!\left(\frac{S_t^{(i)}}{S_{t-1}^{(i)}}\right)}_{g_t^{(i)}}-g_f.
      \]
    \item Store $\bar g_t^{(i)}$. The $T$ prices produce $T-1$ observations.
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  Set $g_f=0$ when the required output is the growth rate rather than the excess
  growth rate.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Common Patterns Across Many Assets}
  Let $\mathbf G\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ collect centered growth rates for $m$
  dates and $n$ assets. Its singular value decomposition factors $\mathbf G$
  into date patterns $\mathbf u_j$ (the columns of $\mathbf U$), asset patterns
  $\mathbf v_j$ (the columns of $\mathbf V$), and nonnegative weights $\sigma_j$
  (the diagonal of $\boldsymbol\Sigma$):
  \[
    \mathbf G=\mathbf U\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf V^{\mathsf T}
    =\sum_{j=1}^{\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf G)}
      \underbrace{\sigma_j\mathbf u_j\mathbf v_j^{\mathsf T}}_{\text{\footnotesize rank-one pattern}},
    \qquad \sigma_1\geq\sigma_2\geq\cdots\geq0.
  \]

  Large singular values identify common patterns that account for much of the
  variation across assets.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  SVD finds patterns in the data; it does not explain their economic causes.
  Results depend on centering, scaling, missing-data treatment, and the selected
  assets.

\end{frame}

% ==== risk ===================================================
\begin{frame}{Risk}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  Risk is uncertainty about loss or gain. A common dispersion measure is the
  sample standard deviation of the growth rate, denoted by $\sigma_g$.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  Suppose $T$ prices produce the $T-1$ observations
  $g_2,g_3,\ldots,g_T$. Let $g'$ be their sample mean. The sample volatility is:
  \[
    g'=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=2}^{T}g_t,
    \qquad
    \sigma_g=
    \underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{1}{T-2}\sum_{t=2}^{T}(g_t-g')^2}}_{\text{\footnotesize growth-rate volatility}}.
  \]

  The statistic has the same inverse-year units as $g$. It measures dispersion
  around the sample mean, not tail probability, drawdown, liquidity, or the
  magnitude of a specific loss.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Other Risk Measures}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  Choose a risk measure that matches the type of loss relevant to the decision.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.6em}
    \item \hilite{How large the loss can get}: a Value at Risk level is passed
      only with a stated small probability; Expected Shortfall is the average
      loss on the days that pass it.
    \item \hilite{Downside and drawdown}: downside deviation counts only returns
      below a target; maximum drawdown is the largest drop from a past peak to a
      later low.
    \item \hilite{Risk against a benchmark}: beta is how much the asset moves
      when the market moves; tracking error is the spread of the gap between the
      return and its benchmark.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Ask:}{what type of loss matters, over what horizon, and will this statistic detect it?}
\end{frame}

% ==== stylized facts =========================================
\begin{frame}{Stylized Facts of Equity Growth Rates}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  A \hilite{stylized fact} is a recurring empirical pattern observed across many
  assets or markets. It is not an exact law for every ticker and sample. It is a
  pattern that a useful model should be able to reproduce.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.6em}
    \item \hilite{Heavy tails}: large movements occur more often than a fitted
      Gaussian model predicts.
    \item \hilite{Weak linear autocorrelation}: signed growth rates often have
      little serial linear dependence at ordinary liquid-market horizons.
    \item \hilite{Volatility clustering}: large movement magnitudes occur near
      other large movements, and calm observations near other calm observations.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Sources:}{\href{https://doi.org/10.1080/713665670}{Cont (2001)}; \href{https://www.jstor.org/stable/2350970}{Mandelbrot (1963)}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Heavy Tailed Return Distributions}
  \vspace{-0.2em}
  A power-law tail decays more slowly than an exponential tail. Let $X$ be a
  random variable, let $\alpha>0$ be its \hilite{tail index}, and let $L(x)$ be
  slowly varying. A two-sided power-law tail satisfies:
  \[
    \mathbb P(|X|>x)\sim
    \underbrace{L(x)x^{-\alpha}}_{\text{\footnotesize tail probability}}
    \qquad\text{as }x\rightarrow\infty.
  \]

  A smaller $\alpha$ means slower decay and a heavier tail. The classical strict
  thresholds identify moments that fail to exist for a pure power-law tail:
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item $\alpha<1$: the mean fails to exist.
    \item $\alpha<2$: the variance fails to exist.
    \item $\alpha<4$: the fourth moment fails to exist.
  \end{itemize}

  \slidenote{Careful:}{at the boundary values $1$, $2$, and $4$, moment existence depends on finer tail detail. Heavy-tailed does not always mean power-law.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Hill's Estimator of the Tail Index}
  Hill's estimator applies to positive observations. For signed growth rates,
  analyze one tail or the magnitude series $|g|$. Let
  $X_{(1)}\geq X_{(2)}\geq\cdots\geq X_{(n)}>0$ be descending order statistics,
  and let $k<n$ select the upper-order observations. The estimate is:
  \[
    \widehat\alpha_k=
    \underbrace{\left[\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}
      \log\!\left(\frac{X_{(i)}}{X_{(k+1)}}\right)\right]^{-1}}_{\text{\footnotesize estimated tail index}}.
  \]

  \vspace{0.3em}
  The cutoff $k$ balances bias and variance. One arbitrary cutoff is not a
  diagnostic: compute $\widehat\alpha_k$ over a range of $k$ values and look for
  a stable region.

  \slidenote{Companion example:}{the stylized-facts notebook uses a Hill stability plot and treats $k=\lfloor\sqrt n\rfloor$ as a reproducible reference, not a uniquely correct cutoff.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Autocorrelation}
  Let $g_2,\ldots,g_T$ be growth-rate observations with sample mean $g'$, and
  let $k$ be a lag measured in sampling intervals. Define the empirical
  autocovariance and autocorrelation by:
  \[
    \widehat R(k)=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=2}^{T-k}(g_t-g')(g_{t+k}-g'),
    \qquad
    \underbrace{\rho(k)=\frac{\widehat R(k)}{\widehat R(0)}}_{\text{\footnotesize autocorrelation}}.
  \]

  This biased normalization divides by the full sample size at every lag, so
  $\rho(0)=1$ and $|\rho(k)|\leq 1$. For many equity samples,
  $\rho(k)$ is near zero at nonzero lags: past signed growth provides little
  \hilite{linear} help in predicting future signed growth.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  Near-zero autocorrelation does \hilite{not} establish independence. Dependence
  can remain in growth magnitude, trading microstructure, or other nonlinear
  functions of the series.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Diagnosing Volatility Clustering}
  Let $g_2^{(i)},\ldots,g_T^{(i)}$ be the growth-rate series for firm $i$, and let
  $\overline{|g|_i}$ be the mean growth-rate magnitude. Magnitudes weight extreme
  days less heavily than squares and give a cleaner estimate. The empirical
  autocorrelation of growth-rate magnitudes is:
  \[
    \widehat\rho_i(k)=
    \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{t=2}^{T-k}
      \left[|g_t^{(i)}|-\overline{|g|_i}\right]
      \left[|g_{t+k}^{(i)}|-\overline{|g|_i}\right]}
    {\displaystyle\sum_{t=2}^{T}
      \left[|g_t^{(i)}|-\overline{|g|_i}\right]^2}.
  \]

  Volatility clustering appears when $\widehat\rho_i(k)>0$ across many lags and
  fades slowly toward zero. A model can therefore match weak signed-growth ACF
  and still fail because it assumes fixed, independent volatility.

  \slidenote{Why it matters:}{conditional volatility changes risk forecasts, option values, hedge ratios, and capital allocation.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}
  This lecture introduced equity securities, continuously compounded growth
  rates, and recurring empirical patterns in financial return data.

  \vspace{0.45em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{Key takeaways}}}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item \textbf{\ink{The price depends on how and where you trade.}} Each
      venue keeps its own book, so the fill depends on the order type and the
      venue; the NBBO is the benchmark for comparing quotes across venues.
    \item \textbf{\ink{One series gives both reward and risk.}} The growth rate
      says how fast the price grew per year, the log return says how much it
      moved over one interval, and volatility is the spread of those numbers.
    \item \textbf{\ink{Real returns are not independent or Normal.}} Large moves
      are far too common for a Normal distribution, and while direction is close
      to unpredictable, size is persistent.
  \end{itemize}
