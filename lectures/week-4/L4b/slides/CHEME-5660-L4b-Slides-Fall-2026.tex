\begin{frame}{Concept Review: N-Ary Tree Models}
  A binomial lattice allows only two price movements per step. If two possible
  futures are not enough, an \hilite{N-ary lattice} allows $m$ branches with
  factors $f_1,\ldots,f_m$ and probabilities $p_1,\ldots,p_m$.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item A recombining node is indexed by its branch-count vector; its
      probability is multinomial (L4a).
    \item More branches make the set of possible prices finer (at the cost of
      more states); a smaller time step brings the steps closer together.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  Today's idea: replace the discrete set of prices with a \hilite{continuous}
  one and the discrete steps with continuous time. Refinement alone is not
  enough; with the right scaling the lattice converges to a continuous model,
  and that model is geometric Brownian motion.
  \slidenote{Example:}{\dimmed{CHEME-5660-L4a-Example-N-Aray-Lattice-Fall-2026.ipynb}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Single Asset Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM)}
  Let $S(t)>0$ be the share price at time $t$ on a horizon $0\le t\le T$. Let
  $\mu\in\mathbb{R}$ (units: inverse years) be a constant arithmetic drift,
  $\sigma>0$ (units: inverse years to the one-half power) a constant
  \hilite{volatility}, and $dW(t)$ the increment of a Wiener process (next
  frame). Geometric Brownian motion (GBM) is a continuous-time random walk whose
  drift and noise are both proportional to the price:
  \[
    \frac{dS(t)}{S(t)}
    =\underbrace{\mu\,dt}_{\text{drift}}
    +\underbrace{\sigma\,dW(t)}_{\text{noise}}.
  \]

  \vspace{0.3em}
  We will see that the log price is a Brownian motion with drift. We reserve
  $g$ for an observed continuously compounded growth rate. The drift $\mu$ and
  a growth rate $g$ share inverse-time units, but they are different
  quantities; the relationship is made precise below.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Wiener Process}
  A Wiener process $\{W(t),\,0\le t\le T\}$ is a continuous real-valued
  stochastic process with three properties:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.6em}
    \item The noise vanishes at zero: $W(0)=0$ with probability one.
    \item Increments over disjoint intervals
      $W(t_1)-W(t_0),\ldots,W(t_k)-W(t_{k-1})$ are independent for any
      $0\le t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_k\le T$.
    \item Each increment is normal with mean zero and variance equal to the
      elapsed time, $W(t)-W(s)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,t-s)$ for $0\le s<t\le T$.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  The third property lets us write any increment as a scaled standard normal:
  \[
    W(t)-W(s)=\sqrt{t-s}\;Z,\qquad Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).
  \]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Analytical Solution}
  Take $t_0=0$ and let $S_0$ be today's price. Solving the SDE with It\^o
  calculus gives the share price at a fixed future time $T$:
  \[
    \boxed{S_T=S_0\exp\!\Bigl[
      \underbrace{\bigl(\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\bigr)T}_{\text{mean log growth}}
      +\underbrace{\sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z}_{\text{shock}}\Bigr],
    \qquad Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).}
  \]

  Thus the log price ratio $\ln(S_T/S_0)$ is normal with mean
  $(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T$ and variance $\sigma^2T$, and the price $S_T$ is
  \hilite{lognormal} and strictly positive.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  The $-\sigma^2/2$ term comes out of the It\^o calculus in the derivation
  notebook; it is the \hilite{half-variance correction} we meet again below.

  \slidenote{Derivation:}{\dimmed{CHEME-5660-L4b-GBM-Solution-Derivation-Fall-2026.ipynb}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Mean and Variance of the GBM Price}
  From the lognormal moments, the expectation and variance of $S_T$ are:
  \[
    \underbrace{\E(S_T)}_{\text{mean price}}=S_0e^{\mu T},
    \qquad
    \underbrace{\Var(S_T)}_{\text{price variance}}
    =S_0^2e^{2\mu T}\bigl(e^{\sigma^2T}-1\bigr).
  \]

  The expected price grows at the arithmetic drift $\mu$. The median price
  $S_0e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T}$ grows at the smaller rate $\mu-\sigma^2/2$.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  The gap is the \hilite{half-variance correction}: the lognormal distribution
  is right skewed, so its mean sits above its median.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Discrete Time GBM Model}
  We observe prices on a grid $t_j=j\Delta t$, $j=0,1,\ldots,N$, with time step
  $\Delta t$ (years) and horizon $T=N\Delta t$. Applying the fixed-horizon
  solution over one step gives the \hilite{one-step transition}:
  \[
    \boxed{S_{t_j}=S_{t_{j-1}}\exp\!\Bigl[
      \bigl(\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\bigr)\Delta t
      +\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_j\Bigr],\qquad j=1,\ldots,N,}
  \]
  with $Z_1,\ldots,Z_N$ \hilite{independent} standard normals. For constant
  $\mu$ and $\sigma$ this is exact at the grid points, not a numerical
  time-stepping approximation.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  Jumping directly from $S_0$ to $t_j$ uses the cumulative shock
  $\tilde Z_j=j^{-1/2}\sum_{i\le j}Z_i$:
  \[
    S_{t_j}=S_0\exp\!\Bigl[\bigl(\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\bigr)t_j
      +\sigma\sqrt{t_j}\,\tilde Z_j\Bigr].
  \]
  $\tilde Z_j$ is standard normal at each $j$ but \hilite{not} independent
  across $j$ (correlation $\sqrt{t_j/t_k}$ for $j<k$): use it for one horizon,
  use the transition for paths.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Monte Carlo Simulation of GBM}
  Given $S_0$, $\mu$, $\sigma$, $\Delta t$, $N$ steps, and $M$ paths, store the
  simulated prices in $\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times M}$, one path per
  column, rows indexed by $j=0,\ldots,N$.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  For each sample path (each column of $\mathbf{S}$):
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
    \item Set the price in row $j=0$ to $S_0$.
    \item For $j=1,\ldots,N$: draw $Z_j\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and apply the
      one-step transition:
      \[
        S_{t_j}\gets S_{t_{j-1}}\exp\!\Bigl[
          \bigl(\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\bigr)\Delta t
          +\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_j\Bigr].
      \]
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  The result is $M$ possible future trajectories of $N$ steps each. Their
  spread is process uncertainty; the uncertainty in $\mu$ and $\sigma$ is a
  separate layer.
  \slidenote{Implementation:}{the \texttt{sample(...)} function in the course package.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimation of GBM Parameters}
  Divide the one-step transition by $S_{t_{j-1}}$, take logarithms, and divide
  by $\Delta t$. The \hilite{one-step growth rate} $g_j$ over
  $t_{j-1}\to t_j$, the same quantity we computed from data in L3a, is:
  \[
    \boxed{g_j\equiv\frac{1}{\Delta t}\ln\frac{S_{t_j}}{S_{t_{j-1}}}
    =\underbrace{\bigl(\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\bigr)}_{\text{mean growth }\gbar}
    +\underbrace{\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}}_{\text{growth-rate std}}\,Z_j.}
  \]

  Because the $Z_j$ are independent standard normals, growth rates over equally
  spaced, non-overlapping steps are independent and normal with mean
  $\gbar\equiv\mu-\sigma^2/2$ and variance $\sigma^2/\Delta t$.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
    \item \hilite{Drift versus growth}: $\mu$ is the SDE drift; the mean growth
      rate $\gbar$ is what a growth-rate series measures. They differ by the
      half-variance correction.
    \item \hilite{Risk}: $\sigma$ sets the growth-rate standard deviation
      $\sigma/\sqrt{\Delta t}$ and the one-step log-return standard deviation
      $\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Volatility}
  From $N+1$ prices on the grid we form $N$ growth rates $g_1,\ldots,g_N$. Their
  sample mean $g'$ and sample standard deviation $\sigma_g$ (our L3a risk
  measure) are:
  \[
    g'=\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^Ng_j,
    \qquad
    \underbrace{\sigma_g}_{\text{growth-rate std}}
    =\sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^N\bigl(g_j-g'\bigr)^2}.
  \]

  (Denominator $N-1$: $N$ observations less the one mean estimated from them.)
  But $\sigma_g$ is \hilite{not} the GBM volatility. Since
  $\Var(g_j)=\sigma^2/\Delta t$, $\sigma_g$ estimates $\sigma/\sqrt{\Delta t}$,
  and the volatility estimate is:
  \[
    \boxed{\hat\sigma=\sigma_g\sqrt{\Delta t}.}
  \]

  For daily data $\Delta t=1/252$: divide the daily growth-rate standard
  deviation by $\sqrt{252}$, or equivalently multiply the daily log-return
  standard deviation by $\sqrt{252}$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Drift}
  Under GBM the arithmetic drift is $\mu=\gbar+\sigma^2/2$. Given an estimate
  $\widehat{\gbar}$ of the mean growth rate, the drift estimate is:
  \[
    \underbrace{\hat\mu}_{\text{SDE drift}}
    =\underbrace{\widehat{\gbar}}_{\text{mean growth}}
    +\underbrace{\tfrac12\hat\sigma^2}_{\text{correction}}.
  \]

  Two common ways to estimate $\gbar$:
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.55em}
    \item \hilite{Method 1, average growth rate}: $\widehat{\gbar}=g'$, the
      sample mean, which is also the maximum likelihood estimate under GBM.
    \item \hilite{Method 2, linear regression}: regress log price on time; the
      slope estimates $\gbar$, but the residuals are a Brownian path, so the
      ordinary standard errors and residual checks do not apply.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  The drift is the harder estimate: mean growth is small relative to
  growth-rate noise, so both methods carry substantial sampling error.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Linear Regression: The Model}
  Take the logarithm of the cumulative GBM solution at grid point $t_j$:
  \[
    \ln S_{t_j}
    =\underbrace{\ln S_0}_{\text{intercept}}
    +\underbrace{\gbar\,t_j}_{\text{trend}}
    +\underbrace{\sigma W(t_j)}_{\text{error}},
    \qquad j=0,\ldots,N.
  \]

  The noise has mean zero, so the \hilite{expected} log price is a line with
  slope $\gbar$: regressing log price on time recovers that line, and the
  Wiener term is the error term.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  That error is a Brownian path: neighboring values are strongly correlated,
  its variance $\sigma^2t_j$ grows along the sample, and the fitted residuals
  inherit both features. Least squares still gives a sensible slope, but
  textbook standard errors, confidence intervals, and residual-normality tests
  assume independent, identically distributed errors and are not calibrated
  here.
  \slidenote{Example 1:}{the Anderson--Darling normality test on these residuals rejects: a warning about the test's assumptions, not a verdict on GBM.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Linear Regression: The Normal Equations}
  Stack the $N+1$ log prices as an overdetermined system
  $\hat{\mathbf X}\boldsymbol\theta+\boldsymbol\epsilon=\mathbf y$, where
  $\boldsymbol\theta$ holds the intercept $\ln S_0$ (left free, not pinned
  to day one) and the slope $\gbar$, $\boldsymbol\epsilon$ is the error
  vector, and the augmented design matrix $\hat{\mathbf X}$ carries a column
  of ones:
  \[
    \hat{\mathbf X}=
    \begin{bmatrix}1&t_0\\1&t_1\\\vdots&\vdots\\1&t_N\end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    \mathbf y=
    \begin{bmatrix}\ln S_{t_0}\\\ln S_{t_1}\\\vdots\\\ln S_{t_N}\end{bmatrix}.
  \]

  The least-squares estimate is the solution of the normal equations:
  \[
    \boxed{\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}
    =(\hat{\mathbf X}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf X})^{-1}\hat{\mathbf X}^{\top}\mathbf y.}
  \]
  Its second component is the regression estimate $\widehat{\gbar}$.
  \slidenote{Example 1:}{\dimmed{CHEME-5660-L4b-Example-Parameters-SAGBM-Fall-2026.ipynb}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Stylized Facts}
  Does GBM capture the empirical regularities of asset growth rates?

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.7em}
    \item \hilite{Heavy tails}: empirical growth rates have heavier tails than a
      normal distribution, so extreme moves are more likely than a Gaussian
      predicts.
    \item \hilite{Absence of autocorrelation}: if prices follow a random walk,
      growth rates over non-overlapping intervals are uncorrelated, and there is
      no predictability to exploit.
    \item \hilite{Volatility clustering}: large moves tend to follow large moves
      and small moves follow small moves, so volatility alternates between
      high and low regimes.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  We test each against the growth-rate law we just derived.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Growth Over a Horizon Under GBM}
  Define the \hilite{horizon-average growth rate} over $0\to T$ as
  $g_{T,0}\equiv(1/T)\ln(S_T/S_0)$, in the same to-from subscript order as the
  discount factor $\mathcal D_{T,0}$. Substituting the GBM solution and dividing
  by $T$ gives:
  \[
    \boxed{\underbrace{g_{T,0}}_{\text{horizon-average growth}}
    =\gbar+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{T}}\,Z,\qquad Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1).}
  \]

  So $g_{T,0}$ is normal with mean $\gbar$ and variance $\sigma^2/T$; the
  one-step case is $T=\Delta t$.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  The uncertainty of the horizon-average growth rate shrinks as $T$ grows while
  its mean stays put. The price does not settle down: $\ln(S_T/S_0)=T\,g_{T,0}$
  has variance $\sigma^2T$, which grows with $T$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Heavy Tails and Volatility Clustering}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \textbf{\ink{Heavy tails: not captured.}} One-step and horizon-average growth
  rates are both normal under GBM. The lognormal price has a long right tail,
  but that is not a heavy-tailed \hilite{growth-rate} distribution.

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \textbf{\ink{Volatility clustering: not captured.}} The volatility $\sigma$ is
  constant, so there is no mechanism for high- and low-volatility periods to
  alternate.

  \vspace{1.0em}
  Both failures follow from the same assumption: independent Gaussian increments
  with a constant variance rate. That assumption is what makes the model exactly
  solvable.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Autocorrelation of One-Step Growth Rates}
  From the growth-rate law, $g_j=\gbar+(\sigma/\sqrt{\Delta t})Z_j$ with
  independent shocks. The autocovariance at lag $\tau\ge1$ is:
  \[
    \Cov(g_j,g_{j+\tau})
    =\frac{\sigma^2}{\Delta t}\,\Cov(Z_j,Z_{j+\tau})=0,
  \]
  because $Z_j$ and $Z_{j+\tau}$ are independent. With
  $\Var(g_j)=\sigma^2/\Delta t$, the autocorrelation function is:
  \[
    \boxed{\rho_g(\tau)=\frac{\Cov(g_j,g_{j+\tau})}{\Var(g_j)}=0,\qquad\tau\ge1.}
  \]

  \textbf{\ink{Absence of autocorrelation: captured}} for one-step growth rates
  over non-overlapping intervals. This says nothing about squared growth rates,
  whose dependence (L3a) GBM also cannot produce.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{GBM Trade Rule}
  Buy $n_0>0$ shares at $S_0$ at time $0$; sell all $n_0$ shares at $S_T$ at
  the scheduled exit $T=N\Delta t$, $N\ge1$ (frictionless: no costs, price
  return only). With
  annual continuously compounded benchmark growth rate $g_b$, $\Dacc=e^{g_bT}$ is the accumulation factor and
  $\Ddis=e^{-g_bT}$ discounts the sale to time 0:
  \[
    \operatorname{NPV}(g_b,T)
    =\underbrace{-n_0S_0}_{\text{entry (now)}}
    +\underbrace{n_0S_T\,\Ddis}_{\text{exit (future)}}.
  \]

  Dividing by the initial investment gives the \hilite{scaled NPV}, a
  dimensionless present-value return on the share cost:
  \[
    \underbrace{\rho_T}_{\text{scaled NPV}}
    :=\frac{\operatorname{NPV}(g_b,T)}{n_0S_0}
    =\Bigl(\frac{S_T}{S_0}\Bigr)e^{-g_bT}-1.
  \]

  This is the L4a rule with one change: $S_T$ now follows GBM instead of a
  binomial lattice.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Substitute the GBM Solution}
  Replacing $S_T$ by the GBM solution puts all of the randomness in one
  standard normal $Z$:
  \[
    \boxed{\rho_T
    =\exp\!\Bigl[\bigl(\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\bigr)T+\sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z\Bigr]
      \underbrace{e^{-g_bT}}_{\text{discount}}-1,
    \qquad Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1).}
  \]

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.55em}
    \item \hilite{Short holding period}: for a few days or weeks and a realistic
      benchmark, $|g_b|T$ is small, $e^{-g_bT}\approx1$, and
      $\rho_T\approx S_T/S_0-1$ is the fractional price change.
    \item \hilite{Longer holding period}: whenever $|g_b|T$ is not small, keep
      the discount factor.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  Either way $\rho_T$ is a continuous random variable driven by $Z$, so a target
  probability is a one-line standardization.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Cumulative Probability of a Trade Exit}
  Let $\rho_\star>-1$ be a target (under GBM $S_T>0$, so $\rho_T>-1$
  always). We ask for the probability that $\rho_T$ at the scheduled exit
  \hilite{strictly exceeds} the target. Moving the non-random terms to the
  right:
  \[
    \Pmeasure(\rho_T>\rho_\star)
    =\Pmeasure\!\Bigl(\ln\tfrac{S_T}{S_0}>\ln(1+\rho_\star)+g_bT\Bigr).
  \]

  Under GBM, $\ln(S_T/S_0)$ is normal with mean $(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T$ and
  standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt T$. Standardizing both sides:
  \[
    \Pmeasure(\rho_T>\rho_\star)
    =\Pmeasure\!\left(
      \underbrace{\frac{\ln(S_T/S_0)-(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T}}_{Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)}
      >\frac{\ln(1+\rho_\star)+g_bT-(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T}\right).
  \]

  As in L4a, this is the scheduled terminal exit; a monitored take-profit or
  stop-loss is a first-passage question.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Closed-Form Target Probability}
  A standard normal exceeds $z$ with probability $1-\Phi(z)$, where $\Phi$ is
  the standard normal cumulative distribution function. Therefore:
  \[
    \boxed{\Pmeasure(\rho_T>\rho_\star)
    =1-\Phi\!\left(
      \frac{\ln(1+\rho_\star)+g_bT-\bigl(\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\bigr)T}
           {\sigma\sqrt T}\right).}
  \]

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
    \item The probability uses the \hilite{real-world} $\mu$ and $\sigma$
      estimated from data. A risk-neutral drift, which we will use to price
      derivatives later, changes the measure and no longer answers a
      probability-of-profit question.
    \item For a short holding period $g_bT\approx0$ and the benchmark drops out
      of the numerator.
    \item A target of $-1$ or below is cleared with probability one.
  \end{itemize}
  \slidenote{Example 2:}{\dimmed{CHEME-5660-L4b-Example-GBM-NPV-TradeRule-Fall-2026.ipynb}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}
  Today we introduced geometric Brownian motion as our first continuous-time
  price model, estimated its parameters from growth-rate data, tested it
  against the stylized facts, and derived a closed-form probability for a
  scheduled trade.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{Key takeaways}}}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
    \item \hilite{GBM is a tractable lognormal model}: exact solution, exact
      one-step simulation with independent normal shocks, mean growing at the
      drift.
    \item \hilite{Drift and growth are different parameters}: the growth-rate
      series gives $\gbar$ and, through $\sigma_g\sqrt{\Delta t}$, the
      volatility; the drift follows from the half-variance correction.
    \item \hilite{One stylized fact passed, two failed}: growth rates are
      uncorrelated but Gaussian with constant volatility; the terminal target
      probability is one minus a standard normal CDF.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.4em}

  portfolios.
