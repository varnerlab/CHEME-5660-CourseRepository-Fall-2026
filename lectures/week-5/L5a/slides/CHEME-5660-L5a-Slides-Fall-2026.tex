\begin{frame}{Objectives for Today}
  Today we extend geometric Brownian motion from one asset to many correlated
  assets, estimate the covariance that couples them from data, and introduce a
  way to sample portfolio weights.

\begin{frame}{Concept Review: Single Asset GBM Model}
  Let $S(t)>0$ be the share price. With arithmetic drift $\mu$ (inverse years),
  volatility $\sigma>0$ (inverse years to the one-half power), and Wiener
  increment $dW(t)$, single asset GBM is:
  \[
    \frac{dS(t)}{S(t)}=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW(t).
  \]

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
    \item \hilite{Drift versus growth}: the mean growth rate is
      $\gbar=\mu-\sigma^2/2$, the expected one-step growth rate; $\mu$ and
      $\gbar$ share units and differ by the half-variance correction.
    \item \hilite{Risk}: $\sigma$ sets the growth-rate standard deviation
      $\sigma/\sqrt{\Delta t}$; from data, $\hat\sigma=\sigma_g\sqrt{\Delta t}$.
    \item \hilite{Constancy}: both are assumed constant; the out-of-sample example
      shows what that costs.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Concept Review: The One-Step Transition and Out of Sample}
  On a grid $t_j=j\Delta t$ of $N$ steps the price advances by the exact one-step
  transition of L4b, with independent standard normal shocks $Z_j$:
  \[
    \boxed{S_{t_j}=S_{t_{j-1}}\exp\!\Bigl[\gbar\,\Delta t+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_j\Bigr],
    \qquad j=1,\ldots,N.}
  \]
  For one fixed horizon $T$ the same solution reads
  $S_T=S_0\exp[\gbar T+\sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z]$; the one-step form is what simulates
  paths.

  \vspace{0.2em}
  In L4b we estimated $\gbar$ and $\sigma$ from data the model had already seen.
  Example 1 asks the model about prices it has \hilite{not} seen: parameters
  from 2014 to 2024, predictions for all of 2025, and a count of how often
  the observed price stays inside the model's bands.
  \slidenote{Example 1:}{\dimmed{CHEME-5660-L5a-Example-OOS-SAGBM-Fall-2026.ipynb}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Catch-up: The GBM Trade Rule}
  If we did not reach the trade-rule probability at the end of L4b, we pick it
  up now. Buy $n_0$ shares at $S_0$, sell at $S_T$ after a holding period $T>0$,
  with benchmark growth rate $g_b$; the scaled NPV is
  $\rho_T=\operatorname{NPV}(g_b,T)/(n_0S_0)=(S_T/S_0)e^{-g_bT}-1$. For a target
  $\rho_\star>-1$, with $\Phi$ the standard normal cumulative distribution
  function, single asset GBM gives the probability of clearing it in closed
  form:
  \[
    \boxed{\Pmeasure(\rho_T>\rho_\star)
    =1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{\ln(1+\rho_\star)+g_bT-\gbar\,T}{\sigma\sqrt T}\right).}
  \]

  \vspace{0.4em}
  The derivation is the GBM Trade Rule section of the L4b lecture, and the L4b
  trade-rule example evaluates it for a firm in our dataset. Everything else
  today extends the price model itself, from one asset to many.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Multiple Asset Geometric Brownian Motion (MAGBM) Model}
  Independently simulated assets never move together, and co-movement is the
  point of a portfolio. Consider assets $\mathcal P=\{1,\ldots,M\}$ with share
  prices $S_i(t)$ and drifts $\mu_i$, a \hilite{covariance rate}
  $\bC\in\mathbb R^{M\times M}$ (inverse years, $C_{ii}=\sigma_i^2$), a
  \hilite{loading matrix} $\bA$ with $\bA\bA^{\top}=\bC$, and $M$ independent
  Wiener processes $W_\ell(t)$:
  \[
    \frac{dS_i(t)}{S_i(t)}
    =\underbrace{\mu_i\,dt}_{\text{drift}}
    +\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}A_{i\ell}\,dW_\ell(t)}_{\text{correlated noise}},
    \qquad i\in\mathcal P.
  \]

  It\^o's lemma on $\ln S_i$ gives each asset its own half-variance correction:
  the mean growth rate is $\gbar_i=\mu_i-C_{ii}/2$, exactly the single asset
  case with $\sigma_i^2=C_{ii}$.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  Indices from here on: $i,j$ assets, $\ell$ shock coordinates, $k$ time periods.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why the \texorpdfstring{$\bA\bA^{\top}$}{AAT} Factorization?}
  A factorization $\bA\bA^{\top}=\bC$ is a \hilite{Cholesky decomposition} when
  $\bA$ is lower triangular; the symmetric square root $\bC^{1/2}$ is another
  valid choice. Any covariance factor with $\bA\bA^{\top}=\bC$ will do.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
    \item \hilite{Independent noise becomes correlated noise}: the $W_\ell$ are
      independent, but multiplying their increments by $\bA$ mixes them into
      the co-movement we observe.
    \item \hilite{It reproduces the covariance rate}: over $dt$, the noise terms
      of assets $i$ and $j$ have covariance:
      \[
        \Cov\!\Bigl(\textstyle\sum_\ell A_{i\ell}\,dW_\ell,\ \sum_\ell A_{j\ell}\,dW_\ell\Bigr)
        =\textstyle\sum_\ell A_{i\ell}A_{j\ell}\,dt=(\bA\bA^{\top})_{ij}\,dt=C_{ij}\,dt.
      \]
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  So $\bC$ is the covariance per unit time, and the factorization is what makes
  the simulated assets have the covariance we asked for.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Exact One-Step Transition}
  Let $\bZ\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I_M)$, with $\mathbf I_M$ the
  $M\times M$ identity, be a vector of independent standard normal shocks, drawn
  \hilite{fresh at every step} and \hilite{shared by all assets} within that
  step. Over a step $\Delta t$:
  \[
    \boxed{S_i(t+\Delta t)=S_i(t)\exp\!\Bigl[
      \underbrace{\gbar_i\,\Delta t}_{\text{mean growth}}
      +\underbrace{\sqrt{\Delta t}\,(\bA\bZ)_i}_{\text{correlated shock}}\Bigr],
    \qquad i\in\mathcal P,}
  \]
  with $(\bA\bZ)_i=\sum_\ell A_{i\ell}Z_\ell$.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
    \item Exact at the grid points for constant parameters; not a time-stepping
      approximation.
    \item Redraw $\bZ$ at every step: a path is built from independent
      increments. One fixed horizon $T$ can instead be sampled directly with a
      single shock vector scaled by $\sqrt T$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Scaling Rule}
  Divide the transition by $S_i(t)$ and take logarithms: the one-step log return
  is $r_i=\ln(S_i(t+\Delta t)/S_i(t))$ and the growth rate is $g_i=r_i/\Delta t$.
  Collect them in vectors $\mathbf r$ and $\bg=\mathbf r/\Delta t$, the drifts in
  $\boldsymbol\mu$, and the mean growth rates in $\bar{\bg}$:
  \[
    \bg=\bar{\bg}+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}\bA\bZ
    \;\Longrightarrow\;
    \E[\bg]=\bar{\bg}=\boldsymbol\mu-\tfrac12\operatorname{diag}(\bC),
    \;\;
    \underbrace{\Cov(\bg)}_{\text{growth-rate}}=\tfrac{\bC}{\Delta t},
    \;\;
    \underbrace{\Cov(\mathbf r)}_{\text{log-return}}=\bC\,\Delta t.
  \]

  Three different matrices, related by powers of $\Delta t$: growth-rate
  covariance (inverse years squared), log-return covariance (dimensionless), and
  the covariance rate $\bC$ (inverse years). Keeping them apart is the
  multi-asset version of L4b's $\hat\sigma=\sigma_g\sqrt{\Delta t}$.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  The model needs $\bC$, and $\bC$ comes from data.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Empirical Covariance Matrix}
  Take $N\ge2$ equally spaced, aligned periods of growth rates for the $M$ firms
  in $\mathcal P$; $g^{(i)}_k$ is firm $i$'s growth rate in period $k$, and
  $g'_i=\frac1N\sum_kg^{(i)}_k$ its sample mean. The empirical growth-rate
  covariance $\Sg\in\mathbb R^{M\times M}$ has entries:
  \[
    \hat\Sigma_{g,ij}=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}
    \underbrace{\bigl(g^{(i)}_k-g'_i\bigr)}_{\text{deviation}}
    \bigl(g^{(j)}_k-g'_j\bigr),\qquad i,j\in\mathcal P.
  \]

  The diagonal $\hat\Sigma_{g,ii}$ is firm $i$'s growth-rate variance, so
  $\sqrt{\hat\Sigma_{g,ii}}$ is L3a's $\sigma_g$ for that firm (inverse years),
  \hilite{not} the volatility. The sign of an off-diagonal entry is the direction
  of the relationship; its magnitude depends on both firms' scales.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Correlation}
  The standardized strength of a relationship is the \hilite{correlation}
  $\rho_{ij}$ of firms $i$ and $j$, defined whenever both variances are
  positive:
  \[
    \boxed{\rho_{ij}=\frac{\hat\Sigma_{g,ij}}{\sqrt{\hat\Sigma_{g,ii}\,\hat\Sigma_{g,jj}}}\in[-1,1]
    \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
    \hat\Sigma_{g,ij}=\rho_{ij}\sqrt{\hat\Sigma_{g,ii}\,\hat\Sigma_{g,jj}}.}
  \]

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
    \item Correlation is what to quote when comparing pairs; covariance is what
      the model needs.
    \item Zero correlation means no \hilite{linear} relationship in this sample;
      it does not imply independence.
    \item Correlations are the same for the growth-rate covariance, the log-return
      covariance, and the covariance rate: the scaling cancels in the ratio.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Data Matrix and the Sample Covariance}
  Arrange the growth rates in a \hilite{data matrix} $\bG\in\mathbb R^{N\times M}$
  (rows are periods, columns are firms) and let $\bg'=(g'_1,\ldots,g'_M)^{\top}$
  be the vector of sample means. With $\mathbf 1\in\mathbb R^N$ the vector of
  ones, the centered matrix and the covariance are:
  \[
    \tilde{\bG}=\bG-\mathbf 1\,\bg'^{\top},
    \qquad
    \boxed{\Sg=\frac{1}{N-1}\tilde{\bG}^{\top}\tilde{\bG}.}
  \]

  \vspace{0.3em}
  $\mathbf 1\,\bg'^{\top}$ is an outer product: an $N\times M$ matrix whose every
  row is the vector of sample means, so subtracting it centers each column.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  One matrix product replaces $M(M+1)/2$ pairwise sums.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{From the Growth-Rate Covariance to the GBM Covariance Rate}
  The scaling rule says $\Cov(\bg)=\bC/\Delta t$, so the estimate of the
  covariance rate the model needs is:
  \[
    \boxed{\Ch=\Delta t\,\Sg.}
  \]

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
    \item Its diagonal returns the L4b volatilities firm by firm:
      $\hat\sigma_i=\sqrt{\hat C_{ii}}=\sqrt{\Delta t}\sqrt{\hat\Sigma_{g,ii}}$.
    \item The log-return covariance is $\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}_r=\Delta t^2\,\Sg=\Delta t\,\Ch$.
    \item Daily data, $\Delta t=1/252$: multiplying a daily log-return covariance
      by 252 estimates $\Ch$; it does not estimate $\Sg$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Covariance Matrix Properties}
  Every covariance estimate must satisfy these, and they are worth checking
  numerically:

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.55em}
    \item \hilite{Elements}: diagonal entries are variances (non-negative);
      off-diagonal entries are covariances whose sign gives the direction.
    \item \hilite{Symmetry}: $\hat\Sigma_{g,ij}=\hat\Sigma_{g,ji}$, directly from
      the definition; $\Ch=\Delta t\,\Sg$ inherits it.
    \item \hilite{Positive semidefinite}: for any weight vector
      $\mathbf v\in\mathbb R^M$ the quadratic form is a squared length:
      \[
        \mathbf v^{\top}\Sg\mathbf v=\frac{1}{N-1}\lVert\tilde{\bG}\mathbf v\rVert_2^2\ge0,
      \]
      so a portfolio variance can never come out negative, and the L5b
      optimization rests on this.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Caveats Before Calling the Estimate Simulation-Ready}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \textbf{\ink{Only semidefinite.}} Duplicated assets, an exact linear
  dependence, or too few observations make $\Sg$ singular; since $\tilde{\bG}$
  has rank at most $N-1$, it is always singular once $M\ge N$. An unpivoted
  Cholesky factorization then fails; an eigenvalue or singular-value square root
  still works. Diagonal ``jitter'' restores invertibility but changes the model
  and must be documented.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \textbf{\ink{Sampling error.}} $M(M+1)/2$ distinct entries from $N$
  observations are noisy when $M$ is not small relative to $N$; pairwise
  estimates shift across windows and correlations change in stressed markets.
  Shrinkage and factor models trade flexibility for stability.
  \slidenote{Example 2:}{\dimmed{CHEME-5660-L5a-Example-CovarianceMatrix-Fall-2026.ipynb}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Portfolio Weights and Dirichlet Sampling}
  Let $W_0>0$ be initial wealth and $\bw=(w_1,\ldots,w_M)^{\top}$ the initial
  dollar fractions placed in each asset. Fully invested and long only:
  $w_i\ge0$, $\sum_iw_i=1$, so $\bw$ lies on the \hilite{simplex}
  $\Delta^{M-1}$ (a segment for two assets, a triangle for three).

  \vspace{0.3em}
  Buying $n_i=w_iW_0/S_i(0)$ fractional shares and holding them, wealth at time
  $t$ is:
  \[
    \boxed{W_t=\sum_{i\in\mathcal P}n_iS_i(t)=W_0\sum_{i\in\mathcal P}w_i\,\frac{S_i(t)}{S_i(0)}.}
  \]

  \vspace{0.3em}
  The weights drift: the fraction in asset $i$ at time $t$ is
  $w_i(t)=n_iS_i(t)/W_t$. A constant-weight portfolio that trades back to $\bw$
  is a different, rebalanced strategy with its own turnover and costs.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Dirichlet Sampling of the Simplex}
  Let $\boldsymbol\alpha=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_M)$ with concentration
  parameters $\alpha_i>0$ and $\alpha_0=\sum_i\alpha_i$. A random vector
  $\mathbf W\sim\operatorname{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol\alpha)$ lies in the interior
  of $\Delta^{M-1}$ (positive weights summing to one) with:
  \[
    \E[W_i]=\frac{\alpha_i}{\alpha_0},\qquad
    \Var(W_i)=\frac{\alpha_i(\alpha_0-\alpha_i)}{\alpha_0^2(\alpha_0+1)},\qquad
    \Cov(W_i,W_j)=-\frac{\alpha_i\alpha_j}{\alpha_0^2(\alpha_0+1)}\ (i\ne j).
  \]

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
    \item Covariances are negative: the weights compete for one unit of wealth.
    \item Every $\alpha_i=1$: constant density with respect to volume on the
      simplex, not uniform in each component.
    \item Symmetric $\alpha_i=\alpha$: below one favors allocations near the
      boundary relative to uniform; large $\alpha$ clusters near equal weights
      $1/M$; unequal $\alpha_i$ tilt toward the assets with larger concentration.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Sampling Is Not Optimization}
  A Dirichlet draw is a \hilite{candidate} weight vector; it becomes good or bad
  only when an objective is evaluated for it. The simplest: to first order the
  one-step growth rate of a portfolio held at weights $\bw$ is $g_p=\bw^{\top}\bg$
  (a one-period, fixed-weight statistic), whose estimated variance is:
  \[
    \underbrace{\bw^{\top}\Sg\bw}_{\text{portfolio growth-rate variance}}\ge0.
  \]

  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item Sampling \hilite{explores} the simplex; the concentration vector is the
      dial that says where.
    \item L5b \hilite{optimizes}: it turns the estimated means and covariance into
      the minimum-variance portfolio; its portfolio example simulates
      buy-and-hold wealth with correlated MAGBM paths for a chosen $\bw$.
  \end{itemize}

\end{frame}
