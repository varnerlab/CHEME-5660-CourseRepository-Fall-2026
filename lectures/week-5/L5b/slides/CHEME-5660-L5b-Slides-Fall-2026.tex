% ==== concept review =========================================
\begin{frame}{Concept Review: MAGBM and the Covariance Matrix}
  L5a: $M$ assets with mean growth rates $\gbar_i$, covariance rate $\bC$ with
  factor $\bA\bA^{\top}=\bC$, one shock vector $\bZ$ per step shared by all:
  \[
    S_i(t+\Delta t)=S_i(t)\exp\!\Bigl[\gbar_i\,\Delta t+\sqrt{\Delta t}\,(\bA\bZ)_i\Bigr],
    \qquad
    \bg=\bar{\bg}+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}\bA\bZ
    \;\Longrightarrow\;
    \Cov(\bg)=\tfrac{\bC}{\Delta t}.
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item From $N$ days of data: sample means $\bg'$ (estimate of $\bar{\bg}$),
      $\Sg=\tilde{\mathbf G}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf G}/(N-1)$ with $\tilde{\mathbf G}$
      the centered growth-rate data matrix, $\Ch=\Delta t\,\Sg$,
      $\rho_{ij}=\hat\Sigma_{g,ij}/\sqrt{\hat\Sigma_{g,ii}\hat\Sigma_{g,jj}}$.
    \item \hilite{Positive semidefinite by construction}:
      $\mathbf v^{\top}\Sg\mathbf v=\lVert\tilde{\mathbf G}\mathbf v\rVert_2^2/(N-1)\ge0$,
      so today's minimization is convex; $N\gg M$ makes it positive definite.
  \end{itemize}
  \slidenote{Catch-up:}{\dimmed{../L5a/CHEME-5660-L5a-Example-CovarianceMatrix-Fall-2026.ipynb} (Task 2, if not reached in L5a)}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Concept Review: Portfolio Wealth and the Trade Rule}
  For initial wealth $W_0$ and weights $\bw$ on the long-only simplex
  ($w_i\ge0$, $\sum_iw_i=1$), buy $n_i=w_iW_0/S_i(0)$ shares and hold them:
  \[
    W_t=\sum_{i\in\mathcal P}n_iS_i(t),
    \qquad
    \rho_T=\frac{W_T}{W_0}\,e^{-g_bT}-1,
  \]
  with $g_b$ the benchmark growth rate and $T$ the holding period (L4b's scaled
  NPV, with wealth in place of the share position). A target $\rho_\star$ turns
  it into a probability, estimated by simulation for a portfolio because a sum
  of correlated lognormals has no closed form.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  L5a explored $\bw$ with Dirichlet draws and preferred no point of the simplex.
  \hilite{Today we optimize.}
\end{frame}

% ==== MPT ====================================================
\begin{frame}{Modern Portfolio Theory: Portfolio Reward}
  Markowitz (1952): an asset is not judged in isolation; what matters is how its
  growth rate moves with the rest of the portfolio. Take $M$ risky assets held
  at weights $\bw$ ($\sum_iw_i=1$) over one period, with growth rates $\bg$,
  mean $\bar{\bg}=\E[\bg]$, and covariance $\Sig=\Cov(\bg)$. The portfolio
  growth rate over the period is the weighted growth rate, so its reward is:
  \[
    g_p=\bw^{\top}\bg
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \boxed{\E[g_p]=\sum_{i\in\mathcal P}w_i\,\E[g_i]=\bw^{\top}\bar{\bg}}
  \]
  estimated from data by $\bw^{\top}\bg'$; units inverse years.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  Reward is linear in the weights. Risk is not, and that asymmetry is the whole
  subject.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the Objective Is and Is Not}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.6em}
    \item One period at weights $\bw$: exact simple return
      $R_p=\sum_iw_i(e^{g_i\Delta t}-1)$, exact log growth rate
      $\Delta t^{-1}\ln\bigl(\sum_iw_ie^{g_i\Delta t}\bigr)$; to first order in
      $\Delta t$ both give $\bw^{\top}\bg$. So $g_p=\bw^{\top}\bg$ is the
      \hilite{linearized} one-period growth rate.
    \item Over many periods, the log growth of buy-and-hold wealth $W_t$ is
      \hilite{not} the weighted sum of the assets' log growth rates: the weights
      drift, and the logarithm of a sum is not the sum of logarithms (L5a).
    \item MPT is a statement about this one-period statistic. The frontier, the
      tangent portfolio, and the capital allocation line all inherit that scope.
    \item Example 1 therefore evaluates the optimized weights afterwards with the
      buy-and-hold wealth rule: an exact, and different, calculation.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Modern Portfolio Theory: Portfolio Risk}
  The risk of the portfolio is the variance of its growth rate, which collects
  every asset variance and every pairwise covariance:
  \[
    \boxed{\Var(g_p)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal P}\sum_{j\in\mathcal P}w_iw_j
      \underbrace{\Cov(g_i,g_j)}_{=\,\rho_{ij}\sqrt{\Sigma_{g,ii}\Sigma_{g,jj}}}
      =\bw^{\top}\Sig\bw}
  \]
  with $\sigma_{g,p}=\sqrt{\Var(g_p)}$ the portfolio's growth-rate standard
  deviation (inverse years; the volatility would be $\sigma_{g,p}\sqrt{\Delta t}$).
  Two assets at weights $w$ and $1-w$, $0<w<1$:
  \[
    \Var(g_p)=w^2\Sigma_{g,11}+(1-w)^2\Sigma_{g,22}
      +2w(1-w)\,\rho_{12}\sqrt{\Sigma_{g,11}\Sigma_{g,22}}.
  \]
  At $\rho_{12}=1$ a perfect square: mixing buys nothing. For every
  $\rho_{12}<1$ the standard deviation falls below the weighted average; if
  $\rho_{12}$ is below the ratio of the smaller to the larger standard deviation,
  some mixture beats either asset alone.
  \hilite{Diversification is a statement about covariance}, not about the number
  of holdings.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Growth Rates versus Returns}
  Everything can be written with log returns $\mathbf r=\Delta t\,\bg$ instead:
  $\E[r_p]=\Delta t\,\E[g_p]$ (dimensionless),
  $\Var(r_p)=\Delta t^2\,\Var(g_p)$, and
  $\boldsymbol\Sigma_r=\Delta t^2\Sig$ (L5a's scaling rule).

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
    \item The optimizer does not care: multiplying the covariance by any positive
      constant, and the means and target by any (other) positive constant,
      leaves the minimizing weights unchanged.
    \item Every reported number does change, so the units of the risk axis, the
      growth axis, and the Sharpe ratio must be stated.
    \item Daily data: $\Sg=252\,\Ch$. Growth-rate variances look large next to
      annual covariance-rate entries; that factor is the unit conversion, not an
      estimation failure.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ==== minimum-variance portfolios ============================
\begin{frame}{The Global Minimum-Variance Portfolio}
  No growth requirement at all: the fully invested portfolio with the smallest
  variance. Shorts allowed, budget constraint only, $\Sig$ symmetric positive
  definite, $\bone\in\mathbb R^M$ the vector of ones:
  \[
    \boxed{
    \min_{\bw}\ \tfrac12\bw^{\top}\Sig\bw
    \ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ \bone^{\top}\bw=1
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \bw_{\mathrm{GMV}}=\frac{\Sig^{-1}\bone}{\bone^{\top}\Sig^{-1}\bone},
    \qquad
    \sigma^2_{g,\mathrm{GMV}}=\frac{1}{\bone^{\top}\Sig^{-1}\bone}}
  \]

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
    \item The one half is a convenience; it cancels in the derivation and does
      not move the minimizer.
    \item Nothing keeps the weights non-negative. In Example 1 the closed form
      shorts several volatile names to hedge the others.
    \item Depends on $\Sig$ only, not on $\bar{\bg}$: the reason it is a
      standard baseline (last frames).
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Derivation: The Lagrangian}
  Introduce a multiplier $\lambda\in\mathbb R$ for the budget constraint:
  \[
    \mathcal L(\bw,\lambda)=\tfrac12\bw^{\top}\Sig\bw-\lambda\bigl(\bone^{\top}\bw-1\bigr).
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
    \item Stationarity: $\Sig\bw-\lambda\bone=\mathbf 0$, so
      $\bw=\lambda\Sig^{-1}\bone$.
    \item Budget: $\bone^{\top}\bw=1$ gives
      $\lambda=(\bone^{\top}\Sig^{-1}\bone)^{-1}$ and the boxed weights.
    \item Substituting back:
      $\bw^{\top}\Sig\bw=\lambda^2\,\bone^{\top}\Sig^{-1}\bone=\lambda$, the boxed
      variance.
    \item $\Sig\succ0$ makes the objective strictly convex, so the stationary
      point is the unique minimum. $\blacksquare$
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Long-Only Problem}
  Add $w_i\ge0$ for all $i$ (with the budget constraint, no leverage either): a
  \hilite{convex quadratic program}, positive semidefinite quadratic objective,
  linear constraints, minimizer possibly on a boundary with weights exactly
  zero. No closed form; the course package solves it in JuMP with an
  interior-point method:
  \[
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      \text{minimize}\ \ &\bw^{\top}\Sg\bw\\
      \text{subject to}\ \ &\hat{\bg}^{\top}\bw\ge g_\star,\qquad
      \bone^{\top}\bw=1,\qquad 0\le w_i\le1\ \ \forall i\in\mathcal P
    \end{aligned}}
  \]
  with $\hat{\bg}$ the estimated mean growth rates and $g_\star$ the investor's
  \hilite{target growth rate}, entering as a floor.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  Set $g_\star$ at or below the smallest single-asset mean growth rate and every
  long-only portfolio meets the floor: the long-only GMV portfolio, whose variance can never beat the
  closed form's (smaller feasible set). Position limits, turnover, lots, taxes,
  and costs enter the same way.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Target-Growth Problem and the Frontier}
  Classical form: an \hilite{equality} target $\bar{\bg}^{\top}\bw=g_\star$; the
  minimum-variance portfolios indexed by $g_\star$ form the
  \hilite{minimum-variance frontier}. Shorts allowed, with
  $a=\bone^{\top}\Sig^{-1}\bone$, $b=\bone^{\top}\Sig^{-1}\bar{\bg}$,
  $c=\bar{\bg}^{\top}\Sig^{-1}\bar{\bg}$, $d=ac-b^2$ (positive unless all means
  are equal):
  \[
    \sigma_g^2(g_\star)=\frac{a\,g_\star^2-2b\,g_\star+c}{d},
  \]
  a hyperbola in the $(\sigma_g,g)$ plane with vertex at the GMV portfolio
  ($g_\star=b/a$, $\sigma_g^2=1/a$).

  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
    \item \hilite{Efficient frontier}: the upper branch, at or above the GMV
      growth rate; nothing feasible has at least as much growth and no more
      variance with one strict improvement. The lower branch is dominated by
      the point directly above it.
    \item Every fully invested $\bw$ lands on or to the right of the hyperbola
      (the \hilite{attainable region}); long-only, the frontier lies on or to
      the right of it and ends at the best single asset.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ==== risk-free asset ========================================
\begin{frame}{Adding a Risk-Free Asset: The Capital Allocation Line}
  A horizon-matched risk-free asset (a Treasury bill or zero-coupon STRIP held
  to maturity) has known growth rate $g_f$, zero variance, and zero covariance
  with everything. Put a fraction $w_f$ in it and $1-w_f$
  in a risky portfolio $p$ with $\E[g_p]$ and $\sigma_{g,p}>0$; the complete
  portfolio $c$ has:
  \[
    \E[g_c]=g_f+(1-w_f)\bigl(\E[g_p]-g_f\bigr),
    \qquad
    \sigma_{g,c}=|1-w_f|\,\sigma_{g,p}.
  \]
  For $w_f\le1$, eliminate $w_f$ ($1-w_f=\sigma_{g,c}/\sigma_{g,p}$):
  \[
    \boxed{\E[g_c]=g_f+
      \underbrace{\frac{\E[g_p]-g_f}{\sigma_{g,p}}}_{\text{Sharpe ratio }\SR_p}
      \;\sigma_{g,c}}
  \]
  A straight line from $(0,g_f)$ through $p$: $0<w_f<1$ lends, $w_f=0$ is $p$,
  and $w_f<0$ borrows at $g_f$. A higher borrowing rate kinks the line at $p$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Which Sharpe Ratio?}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.6em}
    \item With $\sigma_{g,p}$ in the denominator, $\SR_p$ is dimensionless
      (inverse years over inverse years) but is the Sharpe ratio of a
      \hilite{one-observation-interval} holding period, because $\sigma_{g,p}$
      is the standard deviation of a one-day growth rate. Values are small.
    \item Practice divides by the volatility
      $\sqrt{\bw^{\top}\bC\bw}=\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\sigma_{g,p}$ instead: the
      \hilite{annualized} Sharpe ratio $\SR_p/\sqrt{\Delta t}$, larger by
      $\sqrt{252}\approx15.9$ for daily data.
    \item The two conventions rank portfolios identically, so every conclusion
      today holds under either. Quote which one. L6b onward quotes the annualized
      value; Example 1 prints both.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Tangent Portfolio}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \vspace{0.3em}
      Every risky portfolio has a CAL; steeper is better. The
      \hilite{tangent portfolio} $\Tan$ maximizes the Sharpe ratio; its CAL
      touches the efficient branch at $\Tan$. Shorts allowed, $\Sig\succ0$,
      and $\bone^{\top}\Sig^{-1}(\bar{\bg}-g_f\bone)>0$ (GMV beats $g_f$;
      otherwise the formula gives the minimum-Sharpe portfolio):
      \[
        \boxed{\bw_{\Tan}=\frac{\Sig^{-1}(\bar{\bg}-g_f\bone)}
          {\bone^{\top}\Sig^{-1}(\bar{\bg}-g_f\bone)}}
      \]
      Long-only: no closed form; Example 1 takes the largest Sharpe ratio on
      the computed efficient branch.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figs/Fig-Frontier-CAL-Schematic.png}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two-Fund Separation}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.6em}
    \item Because the tangent portfolio's CAL dominates every other, all
      mean--variance investors with the same inputs and the same $g_f$ hold the
      \hilite{same risky fund} $\Tan$ and differ only in $w_f$: the investment
      decision is separated from the financing decision (Tobin, 1958).
    \item Exact for the unconstrained problem with a single lending and
      borrowing rate; a long-only constraint on the risky weights alone keeps
      it, with the constrained maximum-Sharpe portfolio as the fund.
    \item No-borrowing and position-limit constraints, or a borrowing rate
      above the lending rate, kink the attainable boundary; then the best risky
      portfolio depends on where the investor sits on the line, and separation
      holds \hilite{piecewise, not globally}.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ==== market portfolio =======================================
\begin{frame}{When Is the Tangent Portfolio the Market Portfolio?}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.55em}
    \item CAPM assumptions: same beliefs about $\bar{\bg}$ and $\Sig$, same
      horizon, lend and borrow at one risk-free rate, every asset tradable
      without taxes or frictions.
    \item Then every investor holds the same tangent portfolio, and market
      clearing makes it the \hilite{market portfolio}: all risky assets weighted
      by market value. Its CAL is the \hilite{capital market line}; a CAL belongs
      to any risky portfolio, the CML to that one under those assumptions.
    \item An optimizer on a ticker list and eleven years of estimates returns a
      \hilite{sample-dependent} tangent portfolio for that universe and model;
      a broad index is one asset class and omits many risky assets.
    \item An equilibrium conclusion, not a label for every maximum-Sharpe
      solution. Example 1 compares two portfolios; it does not test the CAPM.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ==== estimation risk ========================================
\begin{frame}{Estimation Risk Can Dominate Optimization}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.25em}
    \item Both closed forms carry $\Sig^{-1}$, which amplifies the least-certain
      directions; the tangent portfolio also carries $\bar{\bg}-g_f\bone$, the
      noisiest input (two estimators of $\gbar_i$ from the same data differ by
      over ten percentage points per year for some firms), and answers with
      positions beyond the budget.
    \item Minimum-variance weights do not depend on $\bar{\bg}$: more stable, a
      standard baseline next to equal weights.
    \item Fit on pre-decision data, test on data the fit never saw; one split is
      a check, not a validation. Report shrinkage, constraints, and robust
      objectives: they change the problem.
    \item MAGBM is a \hilite{scenario engine} for a fixed allocation, not
      independent validation when the same data fed optimizer and simulator.
  \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}
  Today we defined a portfolio's reward and risk from its weights, means, and
  covariance, derived the minimum-variance portfolios and the efficient
  frontier, and added a risk-free asset to obtain the capital allocation line
  and the tangent portfolio.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{Key takeaways}}}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item \hilite{Covariance determines diversification}: reward is linear in
      the weights, variance collects every pairwise covariance; the objective
      is a one-period statistic.
    \item \hilite{The frontier is a family of constrained minimizations}: closed
      forms when shorts are allowed; long-only, a convex QP whose floor target
      returns the GMV portfolio and the efficient branch.
    \item \hilite{A risk-free asset selects one risky fund}: the CAL slope is
      the Sharpe ratio, the tangent portfolio maximizes it, and the market
      portfolio needs the CAPM assumptions.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.2em}

\end{frame}
