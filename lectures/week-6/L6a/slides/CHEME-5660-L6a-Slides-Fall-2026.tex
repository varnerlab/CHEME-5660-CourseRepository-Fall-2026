% ==== concept review =========================================
\begin{frame}{Concept Review: Data Driven Minimum-Variance Allocation}
  L5b: for a portfolio $\mathcal P$ of risky assets with weights $\bw$, the
  linearized one-period growth rate $g_p=\bw^{\top}\bg$ has reward
  $\E[g_p]=\bw^{\top}\bar{\bg}$ and risk $\Var(g_p)=\bw^{\top}\Sig\bw$; the
  long-only weights for a target $g_\star$ solve:
  \[
    \min_{\bw}\ \bw^{\top}\Sig\bw
    \quad\text{s.t.}\quad
    \bw^{\top}\bar{\bg}\ge g_\star,\qquad \mathbf 1^{\top}\bw=1,\qquad w_i\ge0.
  \]
  Sweeping the floor traces the GMV portfolio and the efficient branch.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  Every entry is an estimate: $\bar{\bg}$ and $\Sig$, one covariance per pair of
  assets, and L5b's advanced material showed how noisy those pairwise entries
  are and how the optimizer amplifies the noise. \hilite{Today's question}: can
  a model with far fewer parameters supply the same inputs?
  \slidenote{Catch-up:}{\dimmed{../../week-5/L5b/CHEME-5660-L5b-Example-Data-MinVar-Portfolio-Fall-2026.ipynb}}
\end{frame}

% ==== factor models ==========================================
\begin{frame}{Factor Models}
  A portfolio of $\np$ assets needs $\np$ means and $\np(\np+1)/2$ covariance
  entries; the second grows with the square of the first. Yet securities move
  together when market-wide news arrives. A \hilite{factor model} makes the
  shared movement explicit. Let $g_{i,t}$ be asset $i$'s growth rate on day $t$
  (inverse years), $g_{M,t}$ the growth rate of a market index $M$ (SPY here;
  the letter names the index), $f_{k,t}$ further factors, $\alpha_i$ an intercept
  (inverse years), $\beta_i$ the sensitivity to the market (dimensionless),
  $b_{ik}$ the sensitivity to factor $k$, and $\varepsilon_{i,t}$ the
  residual (inverse years):
  \[
    g_{i,t}=\underbrace{\alpha_i}_{\text{intercept}}
      +\underbrace{\beta_i\,g_{M,t}}_{\text{market}}
      +\overbrace{\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}b_{ik}f_{k,t}}^{\text{other factors}}
      +\varepsilon_{i,t}.
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  Co-movement between assets is carried by the factors; the residuals are what is
  left over.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two Named Factor Models, and the One We Keep}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.55em}
    \item \hilite{CAPM (bridge from L5b)}: an equilibrium statement about
      expected excess \emph{simple} returns, no intercept. In annualized growth
      rates with a risk-free growth rate $g_f$ the CAPM-style relation is
      $g_{i,t}-g_f\approx\beta_i(g_{M,t}-g_f)+\varepsilon_{i,t}$: an
      approximation, and a time-series regression keeps an intercept whose CAPM
      value would be zero. We borrow the one-factor structure, not the test.
    \item \hilite{Fama--French}: market plus a size factor (SMB) and a value
      factor (HML), written in returns; explains more of diversified portfolios
      at the cost of more parameters.
    \item \hilite{Single index model}: the market factor alone. Today's model.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ==== SIM ====================================================
\begin{frame}{Single Index Models (SIMs)}
  Sharpe (1963). For asset $i\in\mathcal P$ on days $t=1,\ldots,N$, with
  intercept $\alpha_i$ (inverse years), market exposure $\beta_i$
  (dimensionless), and residual $\varepsilon_{i,t}$ (inverse years):
  \[
    \boxed{g_{i,t}=\alpha_i+\beta_i\,g_{M,t}+\varepsilon_{i,t}}
  \]
  $\alpha_i$: expected growth when the market's is zero; $\varepsilon_{i,t}$:
  the \hilite{idiosyncratic} part of the day. Population assumptions:
  \[
    \E[\varepsilon_{i,t}]=0,\qquad
    \Cov(\varepsilon_{i,t},g_{M,t})=0,\qquad
    \Cov(\varepsilon_{i,t},\varepsilon_{j,t})=0\ \ (i\ne j).
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
    \item The first two define $\alpha_i,\beta_i$ as the least-squares
      projection of $g_i$ on $g_M$: free.
    \item The third is an \hilite{extra restriction}: the market is the only
      source of co-movement. Separate fits do not make it true; L6b's covariance
      rests on it; Example 1 checks it.
    \item Return form $r=\Delta t\,g$: same $\beta_i$,
      $\alpha_{r,i}=\Delta t\,\alpha_i$, $\varepsilon_{r,i,t}=\Delta t\,\varepsilon_{i,t}$.
      We stay in growth-rate units.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Beta Means}
  Covariance of both sides with $g_M$ (the intercept is a constant,
  $\Cov(\varepsilon_i,g_M)=0$). With $\sigma_{g,M}$, $\sigma_{g,i}$ the
  market's and asset's growth-rate standard deviations (L3a's $\sigma_g$, not
  volatilities; both positive) and $\rho_{iM}$ their correlation:
  \[
    \boxed{\beta_i=\frac{\Cov(g_i,g_M)}{\Var(g_M)}
      =\rho_{iM}\,\frac{\sigma_{g,i}}{\sigma_{g,M}}}
    \qquad
    \alpha_i=\E[g_i]-\beta_i\,\E[g_M].
  \]

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
    \item Beta is the slope of the projection: a market move of one unit comes
      with an \emph{expected} asset move of $\beta_i$ units; the intercept and
      the residual are there on every day.
    \item \hilite{Dial}: $\beta_i=1$ one for one; $>1$ amplifies; $0<\beta_i<1$
      dampens; $<0$ moves against the market. In our dataset betas run from near
      zero to a little above two, median close to one.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Risk Splits in Two}
  Variance of both sides, using only zero covariance between $\varepsilon_i$ and
  $g_M$ (independence not needed), with $\sigma^2_{g,\varepsilon,i}=\Var(\varepsilon_i)$:
  \[
    \boxed{\underbrace{\Var(g_i)}_{\text{total}}
      =\underbrace{\beta_i^2\,\sigma^2_{g,M}}_{\text{systematic}}
      +\underbrace{\sigma^2_{g,\varepsilon,i}}_{\text{idiosyncratic}}}
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
    \item Systematic risk is inherited from the market and cannot be diversified
      away by adding assets that share it; idiosyncratic risk is the asset's own.
    \item The two need not be comparable: a stock can have $\beta>1$ and be
      dominated by its residual; an index fund $\beta\approx1$ and almost none.
      So \hilite{``beta above one'' means ``amplifies market moves''}, not
      ``more volatile than the market''.
    \item The fraction the market explains is
      $\beta_i^2\sigma^2_{g,M}/\Var(g_i)=\rho_{iM}^2$: what the fitted $R^2$
      estimates (for a non-constant asset growth rate).
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ==== covariance =============================================
\begin{frame}{The Covariance Implied by a SIM}
  Stack the assets: growth rates $\bg$, intercepts $\bal$, betas $\bbe$,
  residuals $\beps$; let $\bar g_M=\E[g_M]$, $\sigma^2_{g,M}=\Var(g_M)$, and
  $\Dg=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2_{g,\varepsilon,1},\sigma^2_{g,\varepsilon,2},\ldots)$.
  The vector model $\bg=\bal+\bbe\,g_M+\beps$ gives, under the three
  assumptions:
  \[
    \boxed{\bar{\bg}=\bal+\bbe\,\bar g_M,\qquad
      \Sig=\sigma^2_{g,M}\,\bbe\bbe^{\top}+\Dg}
  \]
  so $\Cov(g_i,g_j)=\beta_i\beta_j\sigma^2_{g,M}$ off the diagonal and
  $\Var(g_i)=\beta_i^2\sigma^2_{g,M}+\sigma^2_{g,\varepsilon,i}$ on it.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
    \item \hilite{Rank at most one plus diagonal}: every off-diagonal covariance
      is two betas times one number.
    \item The compression: $2\np+1$ numbers (one beta and one residual variance
      per asset, plus the market variance) instead of $\np(\np+1)/2$ covariances,
      plus $\np+1$ for $\bar{\bg}$. For the S\&P 500, on the order of a thousand
      parameters against more than a hundred thousand.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Is Assumed Away}
  The zeros off the diagonal of $\Dg$ are the third assumption. If two assets'
  residuals are correlated, their true covariance is
  $\beta_i\beta_j\sigma^2_{g,M}$ \hilite{plus} a residual covariance the model
  sets to zero.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  Fitting each regression separately makes every residual orthogonal to the
  market column, and nothing else. Example 1 measures the residual correlations
  across our dataset:
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item most pairs close to zero (median absolute correlation below $0.1$),
    \item related firms a few tenths (banks with banks up to about one half),
    \item near-duplicate securities almost perfectly correlated.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  Those are the covariance errors L6b's model inherits: the number to quote when
  asked how good the SIM covariance is.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Portfolio Risk Under a SIM}
  For fixed weights $\bw$, the linearized growth rate $g_p=\bw^{\top}\bg$ has
  portfolio beta $\beta_p=\bw^{\top}\bbe$ and residual
  $\varepsilon_p=\bw^{\top}\beps$:
  \[
    \boxed{g_p=\bw^{\top}\bal+\beta_p\,g_M+\varepsilon_p,\qquad
      \Var(g_p)=\underbrace{\beta_p^2\,\sigma^2_{g,M}}_{\text{market risk}}
      +\underbrace{\bw^{\top}\Dg\bw}_{\text{residual risk}}}
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
    \item Market risk depends on the weights only through $\beta_p$; adding
      assets with the same exposure does not reduce it.
    \item Residual risk diversifies: equal weights $1/\np$ over $\np$ assets with
      residual variances below $K$ give
      $\bw^{\top}\Dg\bw=\frac{1}{\np^2}\sum_i\sigma^2_{g,\varepsilon,i}\le K/\np$.
    \item Two conditions carry it: spread-out fixed weights, and
      \hilite{uncorrelated residuals} (with a general residual covariance
      $\boldsymbol\Omega_\varepsilon$ the term is
      $\bw^{\top}\boldsymbol\Omega_\varepsilon\bw$ and does not vanish).
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ==== estimation =============================================
\begin{frame}{Estimation of SIM Parameters}
  One asset at a time, $N$ aligned daily observations (the L5a data-matrix
  columns). Response $\by=(g_{i,1},\ldots,g_{i,N})^{\top}$; design matrix
  $\bX\in\mathbb R^{N\times2}$ with a column of ones and the market column
  $g_{M,1},\ldots,g_{M,N}$; parameters $\bth_i=(\alpha_i,\beta_i)^{\top}$;
  residuals $\beps$: $\by=\bX\bth_i+\beps$.
  \[
    \boxed{\hat{\bth}_i=\arg\min_{\bth}\lVert\by-\bX\bth\rVert_2^2
      \;\Longrightarrow\;
      (\bX^{\top}\bX)\hat{\bth}_i=\bX^{\top}\by
      \;\Longrightarrow\;
      \hat{\bth}_i=(\bX^{\top}\bX)^{-1}\bX^{\top}\by}
  \]
  unique when $\bX$ has full column rank. Fitted values $\hat{\by}=\bX\hat{\bth}_i$,
  residuals $\mathbf r=\by-\hat{\by}$; $\hat\beta_i$ is the sample covariance
  over the sample market variance, $\hat\alpha_i=g_i'-\hat\beta_ig_M'$.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  In code: never form the inverse; a QR solve (\texttt{X\textbackslash y}) or the
  SVD gives the same estimate stably (Example 1 does it three ways). Ridge
  versions: optional advanced material.
  \slidenote{Example 1:}{\dimmed{CHEME-5660-L6a-Example-SVD-SIM-Estimation-Fall-2026.ipynb}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Uncertainty}
  \hilite{Classical assumptions}: design held fixed, $\E[\beps\mid\bX]=\mathbf 0$,
  errors independent across days, common variance, Gaussian,
  $\beps\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2_{g,\varepsilon}\mathbf I)$. Then
  $\hat{\bth}_i=\bth_i+(\bX^{\top}\bX)^{-1}\bX^{\top}\beps$ is unbiased and
  Gaussian with covariance $\sigma^2_{g,\varepsilon}(\bX^{\top}\bX)^{-1}$.
  Observations are already growth rates: \hilite{no $\Delta t$}.
  \[
    \boxed{s^2_{g,\varepsilon}=\frac{\lVert\mathbf r\rVert_2^2}{N-2},\qquad
      \widehat{\Cov}(\hat{\bth}_i)=s^2_{g,\varepsilon}(\bX^{\top}\bX)^{-1},\qquad
      \mathrm{SE}(\hat\theta_j)=\sqrt{[\widehat{\Cov}(\hat{\bth}_i)]_{jj}}}
  \]
  $95\%$ interval: $\hat\theta_j\pm t_{0.975,N-2}\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat\theta_j)$
  ($1.96$ for eleven years of daily data). Beta's interval is usually narrow;
  alpha's usually wide and often covering zero. Residual diffusion volatility:
  $\sqrt{\Delta t}\,s_{g,\varepsilon}$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fit, and What Daily Data Do to the Assumptions}
  \[
    R^2=1-\frac{\lVert\mathbf r\rVert_2^2}{\sum_t(g_{i,t}-g_i')^2}=\hat\rho_{iM}^2
    \quad\text{(least squares, intercept, non-constant response)}
  \]
  \vspace{-0.6em}
  An index fund can sit near $0.85$ against SPY, a stock with a larger beta
  near $0.25$: both consistent with the model.
  \vspace{-0.15em}

  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item Daily growth rates: heavy tails, clustered absolute values, and (from
      volume-weighted average prices) positive lag-one autocorrelation: the
      classical intervals are optimistic; a Newey--West standard error for beta
      is $40$ to $100\%$ larger.
    \item \hilite{Bootstrap}: $\by^{(b)}=\bX\hat{\bth}_i+\beps^{(b)}$, refit,
      read the spread; parametric (Gaussian) or empirical-residual (keeps the
      tails); both draw days independently. When dependence matters: HAC errors
      or a block bootstrap. Example 2 does all of it.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{-0.2em}
  \slidenote{Example 2:}{\dimmed{CHEME-5660-L6a-Example-SIM-Parameter-Uncertainty-Fall-2026.ipynb}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What R-squared, Alpha, and Beta Do Not Prove}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.55em}
    \item $R^2$ is the sample fraction of variation the fitted market term
      explains: not the probability the model is true, not forecast accuracy,
      not investment quality. Low $R^2$ with a precise beta, high $R^2$ with
      unstable parameters: both happen.
    \item A positive $\hat\alpha_i$ is an intercept conditional on the market
      proxy, the window, the price series (volume-weighted average prices, not
      total returns), and the specification; its interval usually covers zero.
    \item Beta is exposure to the chosen index at the chosen sampling interval,
      not total risk.
    \item Before the SIM covariance goes into L6b's optimizer: residual
      cross-correlations, stability across windows, sensitivity to the proxy,
      and one unit convention throughout (growth rate, log return, covariance
      rate differ by powers of $\Delta t$).
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

  {\large\textbf{\ink{Key takeaways}}}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}

      market explains, and they can disagree.
    \item \hilite{The SIM covariance is rank one plus diagonal, on an

      diversifies only if residuals are uncorrelated, which fits do not
      guarantee.

      units, no $\Delta t$; heavy tails, lag-one dependence, and clustering make
      bootstrap or robust intervals the complement.
  \end{itemize}
