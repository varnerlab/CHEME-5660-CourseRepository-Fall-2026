% ==== concept review =========================================
\begin{frame}{Concept Review: The Capital Allocation Line}
  A \hilite{complete portfolio} $c$ holds $w_f$ in the risk-free asset and $1-w_f$
  in the tangent portfolio $\Tan$:
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \E[g_c]&=g_f+(1-w_f)\bigl(\E[g_{\Tan}]-g_f\bigr) &&\quad\text{expectation is linear}\\
      \sigma^2_{g,c}&=(1-w_f)^2\,\sigma^2_{g,\Tan} &&\quad\text{the risk-free asset adds no variance}
    \end{aligned}
  \]
  Lending ($0<w_f<1$) and borrowing ($w_f<0$) move $c$ along the capital
  allocation line, whose slope is the Sharpe ratio of $\Tan$.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  By \hilite{two-fund separation}, investors with the same inputs who can lend and
  borrow freely at $g_f$ hold the same risky fund $\Tan$. Their risk preferences
  set only $w_f$, and the L6b client interview asked for $w_f$ directly.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  Which $w_f$ should a given investor choose?
\end{frame}

% ==== utility functions ======================================
\begin{frame}{Utility Functions and Rational Choice}
  Utility functions describe a decision-maker's preferences for different
  choices, outcomes, or states of the world. Utility is a proxy for
  satisfaction, so a larger utility is always better.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \hilite{Utility function}: an agent chooses from alternatives
  $\mathcal{X}=\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$, e.g., bundles of goods, portfolios, or states
  of the world. $U:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ assigns each alternative a
  number, in \emph{utils}, that ranks the agent's preferences:
  \[
    \boxed{U(x_i)>U(x_j)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;x_i\succ x_j
      \qquad\text{and}\qquad
      U(x_i)=U(x_j)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;x_i\sim x_j}
  \]
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
    \item \hilite{Ordinal}: utility ranks alternatives but does not measure how
      much better one is. Any order-preserving transformation of $U$, e.g.,
      $\ln U$ for positive $U$, describes the same preferences.
    \item \hilite{Key idea}: when faced with competing alternatives, a rational
      decision-maker always chooses the alternative with the largest utility.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Common Utility Functions}
  Utility functions, and their parameters, are subjective. Five common forms for
  a bundle of goods $\bx=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ with $x_i\ge0$ and $\alpha_i>0$:

  \vspace{0.4em}
  {\footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Utility function} & \textbf{Form} & \textbf{Models}\\
    \midrule
    Linear & $U(\bx)=\sum_i\alpha_ix_i$ & goods that are perfect substitutes\\[0.2em]
    Logarithmic ($\beta>0$) & $U(\bx)=\ln\bigl(\sum_i\alpha_ix_i+\beta\bigr)$ & diminishing satisfaction from more goods\\[0.2em]
    Cobb--Douglas & $U(\bx)=\prod_ix_i^{\alpha_i}$ & goods held together\\[0.2em]
    Leontief & $U(\bx)=\min\left(x_1/\alpha_1,\dots,x_n/\alpha_n\right)$ & perfect complements, fixed proportions\\[0.2em]
    CES ($\eta>0$, $\eta\ne1$) & $U(\bx)=\Bigl(\sum_i\alpha_ix_i^{(\eta-1)/\eta}\Bigr)^{\eta/(\eta-1)}$ & substitution set by the elasticity $\eta$\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  We use the Cobb--Douglas and CES utilities later today. As $\eta$ changes,
  CES moves between the linear ($\eta\to\infty$), Cobb--Douglas ($\eta\to1$, with
  $\sum_i\alpha_i=1$), and Leontief ($\eta\to0$) forms.
\end{frame}

% ==== risk and risk aversion =================================
\begin{frame}{Risk and Risk Aversion}
  When outcomes are uncertain, a rational decision-maker ranks choices by the
  \hilite{expected utility} $\E[U(W)]$
  (\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Neumann\%E2\%80\%93Morgenstern_utility_theorem}{von Neumann--Morgenstern}).
  The shape of the utility of wealth $U(W)$ sets the attitude toward risk:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}
  {\small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Curvature of $U$} & \textbf{Risky versus certain} & \textbf{Risk attitude}\\
    \midrule
    Concave ($U''<0$) & $\E[U(W)]\le U(\E(W))$ & risk averse\\
    Linear ($U''=0$) & $\E[U(W)]=U(\E(W))$ & risk neutral\\
    Convex ($U''>0$) & $\E[U(W)]\ge U(\E(W))$ & risk seeking\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  The \hilite{certainty equivalent} is the guaranteed wealth with the same
  utility, $U(\CE)=\E[U(W)]$. The \hilite{risk premium} is the expected wealth a
  decision-maker gives up to remove the risk:
  \[
    \boxed{\RP=\E(W)-\CE}
  \]
  For a risk-averse decision-maker, $\CE\le\E(W)$ and $\RP\ge0$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Arrow–Pratt Measures}
  How risk averse is a decision-maker? Arrow and
  \href{https://doi.org/10.2307/1913738}{Pratt (1964)} measure the curvature of
  a utility of wealth with $U'(w)>0$:
  \[
    \boxed{\underbrace{r(w)=-\frac{U''(w)}{U'(w)}}_{\text{absolute}}\qquad
    \underbrace{\bar r(w)=w\,r(w)}_{\text{relative}}}
  \]
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      {\small
      \begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
        \toprule
        & $U(w)$ & $r(w)$ & $\bar r(w)$\\
        \midrule
        Log & $\ln w$ & $1/w$ & $1$\\
        Linear & $a\,w+b$ & $0$ & $0$\\
        Power & $w^{\tau}$ & $(1-\tau)/w$ & $1-\tau$\\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      $r>0$: averse. $r=0$: neutral. $r<0$: seeking.

      \vspace{0.5em}
      For a small risk, larger $r$ means a lower CE:
      \[
        \RP\approx\tfrac12\,r(\E(W))\,\Var(W)
      \]
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \hilite{For a portfolio}: with small returns,
  CE return $\approx$ expected return $-\,\tfrac{\bar r}{2}\,{\times}$ return variance.
  Mean-variance utility has this form, with $A$ in the role of $\bar r$.
\end{frame}

% ==== mean-variance utility ==================================
\begin{frame}{Mean-Variance Utility}
  We choose $w_f$ by maximizing the investor's utility. Mean-variance utility
  rises with expected growth and falls with variance:
  \[
    \boxed{U(w_f)=\E[g_c]-\frac{A}{2}\,\Delta t\,\sigma^2_{g,c}}
  \]
  The risk-aversion coefficient $A>0$ (dimensionless) sets how much the investor
  dislikes variance. As in L5a, $\Delta t\,\sigma^2_{g,c}$ is the squared
  volatility, in year$^{-1}$. On the line, both terms depend only on $w_f$:
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      U(w_f)&=g_f+(1-w_f)\bigl(\E[g_{\Tan}]-g_f\bigr)-\tfrac{A}{2}(1-w_f)^2\,\Delta t\,\sigma^2_{g,\Tan}
        &&\quad\text{substitute the line}\\
      \frac{dU}{dw_f}&=-\bigl(\E[g_{\Tan}]-g_f\bigr)+A\,(1-w_f)\,\Delta t\,\sigma^2_{g,\Tan}
        &&\quad\text{differentiate}\\
      0&=-\bigl(\E[g_{\Tan}]-g_f\bigr)+A\,(1-w_f^\star)\,\Delta t\,\sigma^2_{g,\Tan}
        &&\quad\text{first-order condition}
    \end{aligned}
  \]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Optimal Complete Portfolio}
  Solving the first-order condition for the fraction in $\Tan$ gives:
  \[
    \boxed{1-w_f^\star=\frac{\E[g_{\Tan}]-g_f}{A\,\Delta t\,\sigma^2_{g,\Tan}}}
  \]
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
    \item \hilite{A maximum}: the second derivative, $-A\,\Delta t\,\sigma^2_{g,\Tan}$,
      is negative.
    \item \hilite{What moves it}: more excess growth $\E[g_{\Tan}]-g_f$ raises the
      fraction in $\Tan$. A larger $A$ or $\sigma^2_{g,\Tan}$ lowers it, and
      doubling $A$ halves it.
    \item \hilite{Who lends and who borrows?} The investor with
      $A_{\Tan}=(\E[g_{\Tan}]-g_f)/(\Delta t\,\sigma^2_{g,\Tan})$ holds only $\Tan$.
      $A>A_{\Tan}$ lends at $g_f$. $A<A_{\Tan}$ borrows at $g_f$ to buy more of $\Tan$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Indifference Curves and Risk Aversion}
  \hilite{Where does the optimum sit on the line?} An \hilite{indifference curve}
  joins the portfolios with the same utility. Fixing $U$ gives:
  \[
    \E[g_c]=U+\frac{A}{2}\,\Delta t\,\sigma^2_{g,c}
  \]
  Plotted against $\sigma_{g,c}$, each curve is a parabola that opens upward, and
  higher curves have higher utility. The optimal portfolio is where the highest
  curve the investor can reach touches the line.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  The result also runs backward. If a client picks $w_f<1$, the $A$ that makes
  that choice optimal is:
  \[
    \boxed{A=\frac{\E[g_{\Tan}]-g_f}{(1-w_f)\,\Delta t\,\sigma^2_{g,\Tan}}}
  \]

\end{frame}

% ==== utility over holdings ==================================
\begin{frame}{Utility over Holdings}
  Every investor so far holds the same risky fund. Instead, ask a
  consumer-choice question: given a budget and today's prices, which holdings
  does the investor prefer most? At time $t$, with prices $S_i(t)$ (USD per
  share) and wealth $\WP(t)$ (USD), choose the share counts $n_i\ge0$:
  \[
    \boxed{\operatorname*{maximize}_{n_1,\ldots,n_{\np}}\;U(n_1,\ldots,n_{\np})
      \qquad\text{subject to}\qquad\sum_{i\in\mathcal{P}}n_i\,S_i(t)=\WP(t)}
  \]
  This is a \hilite{holding utility}, a preference over the shares held today at
  today's prices. It has no variance term. Risk enters through the preference
  weights, which depend on the SIM betas and the state of the market.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Cobb–Douglas Utility}
  The exponents are the \hilite{preference weights} $\gamma_i\in(-1,1)$. The
  investor maximizes the product over every asset, subject to the budget and a
  small floor of $n_{\min}>0$ shares on each holding:
  \[
    \boxed{\begin{aligned}
      \operatorname*{maximize}_{n_1,\ldots,n_{\np}}\quad&U(n_1,\ldots,n_{\np})=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{P}}n_i^{\gamma_i}\\
      \text{subject to}\quad&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{P}}n_i\,S_i(t)=\WP(t)&&\quad\text{(budget constraint)}\\
      &n_i\ge n_{\min}\quad\forall\,i\in\mathcal{P}&&\quad\text{(share floor)}
    \end{aligned}}
  \]
  The floor keeps the utility finite, because for $\gamma_i<0$ the term
  $n_i^{\gamma_i}$ grows without bound as $n_i\to0$. The sign of $\gamma_i$ splits
  the assets in two: the \hilite{preferred set} $\Ap=\{i:\gamma_i>0\}$ is the
  basket, and the rest form the \hilite{non-preferred set} $\Am=\{i:\gamma_i\le0\}$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Cobb–Douglas Utility: Floors and Net Budget}
  Only the ranking matters, so $\ln U$ has the same maximizer as $U$. We set the
  floors first:
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \frac{\partial\ln U}{\partial n_k}&=\frac{\gamma_k}{n_k}\le0
        &&\quad k\in\Am\text{, so more shares never raise }\ln U\\
      n_k^\star&=n_{\min}
        &&\quad\text{spend every dollar above the floor on }\Ap
    \end{aligned}
  \]
  Substituting the floors into the budget constraint leaves the
  \hilite{net budget} for the preferred set:
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \sum_{i\in\Ap}n_iS_i(t)+n_{\min}\sum_{k\in\Am}S_k(t)&=\WP(t)\\
      \sum_{i\in\Ap}n_iS_i(t)&=\WP(t)-n_{\min}\sum_{k\in\Am}S_k(t)\equiv\Wadj>0
    \end{aligned}
  \]
  A Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ then splits $\Wadj$ over the preferred set,
  assuming each preferred share count stays above its floor.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Cobb–Douglas Utility: Allocation}
  \setlength{\jot}{0pt}
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \mathcal{L}&=\textstyle\sum_{i\in\Ap}\gamma_i\ln n_i-\lambda\bigl(\sum_{i\in\Ap}n_iS_i(t)-\Wadj\bigr)
        &&\text{floor terms are constant}\\
      0&=\gamma_i/n_i-\lambda\,S_i(t)
        &&\text{differentiate with respect to }n_i\\
      n_iS_i(t)&=\gamma_i/\lambda
        &&\text{dollars in proportion to }\gamma_i\\
      \Wadj&=\textstyle\sum_{j\in\Ap}\gamma_j/\lambda
        &&\text{sum over the preferred set}\\
      1/\lambda&=\Wadj\big/\textstyle\sum_{j\in\Ap}\gamma_j
        &&\text{solve for the multiplier}
    \end{aligned}
  \]
  Substituting $1/\lambda$ into $n_iS_i(t)=\gamma_i/\lambda$ gives:
  \[
    \boxed{n_i^\star=\underbrace{\frac{\gamma_i}{\sum_{j\in\Ap}\gamma_j}}_{\text{preference share}}
      \cdot\underbrace{\frac{\Wadj}{S_i(t)}}_{\text{budget in shares}}\quad(i\in\Ap),
      \quad n_i^\star=n_{\min}\quad(i\in\Am)}
  \]
  If a preferred count falls below $n_{\min}$, pin it at the floor and solve again
  with the rest. The dollar weight within $\Wadj$ is $\gamma_i/\sum_j\gamma_j$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{CES Utility: A Concentration Dial}
  The elasticity $\eta>0$ controls how willingly the allocator trades one
  preferred asset for another. For $\eta\ne1$:
  \[
    U_{\mathrm{CES}}=\Bigl(\sum_{i\in\Ap}\gamma_i\,n_i^{(\eta-1)/\eta}\Bigr)^{\eta/(\eta-1)}
    \qquad
    \boxed{n_i^\star=\Wadj\;\frac{\bigl(\gamma_i/S_i(t)\bigr)^{\eta}}
      {\sum_{j\in\Ap}S_j(t)\bigl(\gamma_j/S_j(t)\bigr)^{\eta}}\quad(i\in\Ap)}
  \]
  Share counts are proportional to the \emph{bang for the buck}
  $\gamma_i/S_i(t)$ raised to $\eta$, with the same floor check. Three limits:
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item \hilite{Unit elasticity} ($\eta\to1$): the Cobb--Douglas allocation.
    \item \hilite{Large elasticity} ($\eta\to\infty$): every other asset falls to
      its floor, and the rest goes to the largest $\gamma_i/S_i(t)$, when unique.
    \item \hilite{Small elasticity} ($\eta\to0^+$): equal share counts of every
      preferred asset.
  \end{itemize}
  \slidenote{Proofs:}{\href{\repo/advanced/adaptive_utility/CHEME-5660-L7b-Advanced-CES-Limits-Fall-2026.ipynb}{CES companion},
    which also shows that CES dollar weights depend on share price levels when $\eta\ne1$.}
\end{frame}

% ==== preference weights =====================================
\begin{frame}{Preference Weights from the Single Index Model}
  Each trading day, from the L6b SIM parameters $(\alpha_i,\beta_i)$, the recent
  market growth rate $\tilde g_{M,t}$, and the market-state signal $\xi_t$:
  \[
    \boxed{\gamma_i(t)=\tanh\!\left(\frac{\alpha_i}{\beta_i^{\xi_t}}+\beta_i^{1-\xi_t}\,\tilde g_{M,t}\right)}
  \]
  The $\tanh$ keeps the weight in $(-1,1)$. The argument equals
  $(\alpha_i+\beta_i\tilde g_{M,t})/\beta_i^{\xi_t}$, the SIM's expected growth of
  asset $i$ in today's market divided by $\beta_i^{\xi_t}$. The model assumes
  $\beta_i>0$.
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item \hilite{Which assets belong in the basket?} $\gamma_i(t)>0$ exactly when
      $\tilde g_{M,t}>-\alpha_i/\beta_i$. A weak market keeps only the assets with
      the lowest thresholds. If every $\gamma_i(t)\le0$, hold the floors plus cash.
    \item \hilite{How much of each?} The magnitude sets the budget share. A bearish
      signal ($\xi_t>0$) tilts toward low-beta names, and a bullish one toward
      high-beta names.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Exponential Moving Averages}
  Both market inputs come from the SPY price through \hilite{exponential moving
  averages} (EMAs). An EMA is a running average that gives recent prices the most
  weight. For a price series $S_t$ and a window of $L$ trading days:
  \[
    \boxed{\bar S_t=\omega\,S_t+(1-\omega)\,\bar S_{t-1}\qquad\text{where}\qquad\omega=\frac{2}{L+1}}
  \]
  This is the mean update of L5a with $\omega=1-\lambda$, but here a window $L$
  sets $\omega$ instead of a half-life. A 21-day window has a half-life of about
  7 days, shorter than the 21-day half-life used in L5a.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Market Inputs}
  With $L_{\mathrm{short}}=21$ and $L_{\mathrm{long}}=63$ days and a gain $G>0$,
  the market-state signal is the scaled crossover of the two price EMAs:
  \[
    \boxed{\xi_t=-G\left(\frac{\bar S^{\mathrm{short}}_t}{\bar S^{\mathrm{long}}_t}-1\right)}
  \]
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.2em}
    \item The short EMA responds faster. Falling market: $\xi_t>0$ (bearish).
      Rising market: $\xi_t<0$ (bullish). $G$ makes $|\xi_t|\le1$ on 99\% of
      training days.
    \item $\tilde g_{M,t}$: the EMA ($L_{\mathrm{growth}}=10$ days) of
      $g_{M,t}=\ln(S_t/S_{t-1})/\Delta t$. It swings by about one per year, several
      times the intercepts, so assets cross their thresholds.
    \item Both inputs on day $t$ use prices through day $t-1$.
  \end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Summary}

maximized mean-variance utility along the line and found the optimal risky

CES elasticity moves the allocation between concentration and equal share counts.

recent market growth decides which assets belong in the basket, and the
crossover signal tilts the budget between high-beta and low-beta assets.
\end{itemize}
