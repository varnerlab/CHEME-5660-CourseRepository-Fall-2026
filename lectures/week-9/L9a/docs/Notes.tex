\section{An Optimal Portfolio Is a Time-Stamped Decision}

A minimum-variance optimizer produces target weights for one information set, one covariance model, and one set of constraints. After prices move, a portfolio with fixed share counts no longer has those weights. Re-solving and trading continuously would chase every movement, consume liquidity, realize taxes, and expose the strategy to estimation noise. Dynamic rebalancing therefore asks a policy question: \emph{when is the benefit of returning toward a target worth the cost and risk of trading?}

The distinction between a target and a policy is fundamental. A target $\mathbf w_t^\star$ says what the portfolio would hold at decision time $t$ if it could trade immediately under the current model. A rebalancing policy maps observable state---pre-trade weights, estimates, costs, tax lots, and constraints---to an action. The policy must be specified before evaluating later returns.

\section{How Buy-and-Hold Weights Drift}

Let $\mathbf w_t^+\in\R^m$ be post-trade risky-asset weights at time $t$, with $\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}\mathbf w_t^+=1$, and let $R_{i,t+1}>-1$ be asset $i$'s simple return from $t$ to $t+1$. Before any new trade, the portfolio simple return is $R_{p,t+1}:=(\mathbf w_t^+)^{\mathsf T}\mathbf R_{t+1}$ and the next pre-trade weights are
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:weight-drift}
  w_{i,t+1}^-
  =\frac{w_{i,t}^+(1+R_{i,t+1})}{1+R_{p,t+1}},
  \qquad i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}.
\end{equation}
The superscripts $+$ and $-$ mean immediately after and immediately before a trade, respectively. Equation~\eqref{eq:weight-drift} is a self-financing accounting identity before costs: asset values grow at different rates, while the denominator rescales them to fractions of total wealth.

\begin{proposition}{When weights do not drift}{no-drift}
Assume $1+R_{p,t+1}>0$. For every asset with $w_{i,t}^+>0$, \eqnref{weight-drift} satisfies $w_{i,t+1}^-=w_{i,t}^+$ if and only if
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:no-drift}
  R_{i,t+1}=R_{p,t+1}.
\end{equation}
Thus all positively weighted assets must earn the same return over the interval for every weight to remain unchanged. Assets held at zero weight impose no condition.
\end{proposition}

If a single-index model supplies beta vector $\boldsymbol\beta_t$, the pre-trade portfolio beta becomes $\beta_{p,t+1}^-:=(\mathbf w_{t+1}^-)^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\beta_t$. Even if the beta estimates are held fixed, weight drift changes systematic exposure. With covariance estimate $\Sigma_t$, modeled pre-trade variance is $(\mathbf w_{t+1}^-)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_t\mathbf w_{t+1}^-$. A later model refit creates a second source of change; do not confuse parameter drift with mechanical weight drift.

\section{Three Families of Rebalancing Rules}

Let $\mathbf w_t^\star$ be the current target and let $d(\mathbf w_t^-,\mathbf w_t^\star)$ be a stated distance or risk-deviation measure. Common policies include:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em]
  \item \textbf{Calendar rebalancing.} Trade on predetermined dates---for example, monthly or quarterly---regardless of drift. It is simple to audit but can trade when nothing material changed and wait too long after a large move.
  \item \textbf{Threshold rebalancing.} Trade when $d$ exceeds a band, such as $\|\mathbf w_t^- - \mathbf w_t^\star\|_\infty>\delta$ for tolerance $\delta>0$. This creates a no-trade region but requires a convention for returning to the target, the nearest band edge, or somewhere between.
  \item \textbf{Cost-aware optimization.} Choose the new post-trade weights directly by balancing modeled risk, target return, and trading penalties. The action can be partial rather than all-or-nothing.
\end{enumerate}

A hybrid policy checks thresholds only on scheduled dates. Every rule needs an explicit clock, input timestamp, and action. ``Rebalance when needed'' is not reproducible.

\section{Turnover and the Cost of Returning to Target}

Let $\Delta\mathbf w_t:=\mathbf w_t^+-\mathbf w_t^-$ be the planned weight change. For fully invested portfolios with no external cash flow, $\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}\Delta\mathbf w_t=0$. The one-way turnover fraction is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:turnover}
  \tau_t:=\frac12\|\Delta\mathbf w_t\|_1.
\end{equation}
The factor $1/2$ avoids counting both sales and purchases as separate portfolio replacements. If a cost model charges a proportional rate $c_i\geq0$ to every dollar bought or sold in asset $i$, the cost relative to pre-trade wealth is instead approximately $\sum_i c_i|\Delta w_{i,t}|$; state which convention is being reported.

One convex cost-aware update uses risk-aversion coefficient $\gamma>0$ and turnover penalty $\lambda\geq0$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:cost-aware-rebalance}
  \begin{aligned}
  \min_{\mathbf w\in\R^m}\quad
    &\frac{\gamma}{2}\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_t\mathbf w
      -\boldsymbol\mu_t^{\mathsf T}\mathbf w
      +\lambda\|\mathbf w-\mathbf w_t^-\|_1\\
  \text{subject to}\quad
    &\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}\mathbf w=1,
    \qquad \boldsymbol\ell\leq\mathbf w\leq\mathbf u.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $\boldsymbol\mu_t\in\R^m$ and $\Sigma_t\in\R^{m\times m}$ are information-time expected-return and covariance estimates, while $\boldsymbol\ell,\mathbf u\in\R^m$ are position bounds. The penalty creates endogenous partial rebalancing: a trade occurs only when its modeled benefit overcomes its marginal penalty. Fixed commissions, bid--ask spreads, market impact, minimum lots, and taxes require richer and sometimes nonconvex models.

\section{Backtesting a Dynamic Policy Without Looking Ahead}

At each decision time, first mark the existing shares to current observable prices and compute $\mathbf w_t^-$. Then estimate model inputs using data whose timestamps precede the decision, construct $\mathbf w_t^\star$ or solve \eqnref{cost-aware-rebalance}, convert desired dollar changes into executable share quantities, apply fills and costs, and hold until the next permitted decision. Returns used to judge the action must occur strictly afterward.

Performance accounting should preserve the wealth recursion. If $C_t\geq0$ is the dollar implementation cost paid at time $t$ and $W_t^-$ is pre-trade wealth, then post-trade investable wealth is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:wealth-after-cost}
  W_t^+=W_t^- - C_t.
\end{equation}
Ignoring \eqnref{wealth-after-cost} while changing weights grants the backtest free trading. Also record turnover, realized taxes or a declared pretax convention, constraint violations, cash residuals from integer shares, and failed or stale orders.

Use walk-forward evaluation: select policy parameters such as calendar frequency, threshold $\delta$, and penalty $\lambda$ on training or validation data, then freeze them for a later test period. Compare buy-and-hold, full-target rebalancing, and the proposed partial policy on identical price data and cost assumptions. The relevant question is not whether rebalancing preserves the model target perfectly; it is whether the net risk--return outcome improves after all implementation effects.
