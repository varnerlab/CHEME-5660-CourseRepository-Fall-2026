\begin{frame}{Concept Review: A Bet on the Future}
  Suppose we pay $p$ today for a contract that pays 1 USD in $T$ years if
  $\ST\ge K$, and nothing otherwise.
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.40\textheight,keepaspectratio]{../figs/Fig-L9a-Bet-CashFlows.pdf}
  \end{center}
  Discounting the payoff at the benchmark growth rate $g_y$ gives a random NPV:
  \[
    \operatorname{NPV}_0=-p+\Dinv\,V(\ST)=\begin{cases}-p+e^{-g_yT}&\text{if }\ST\ge K\text{ (true)}\\-p&\text{if }\ST<K\text{ (false)}\end{cases}
  \]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Expected NPV of the Bet}
  Under our beliefs about $\ST$, the expected NPV is:
  {\small
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \E[\operatorname{NPV}_0]&=\left(-p+e^{-g_yT}\right)\underbrace{\mathbb{P}(\ST\ge K)}_{\text{true}}+(-p)\,\underbrace{\mathbb{P}(\ST<K)}_{\text{false}} &&\text{\footnotesize weight by probability}\\
      &=-p\,\underbrace{\left[\mathbb{P}(\ST\ge K)+\mathbb{P}(\ST<K)\right]}_{=1}+e^{-g_yT}\,\mathbb{P}(\ST\ge K) &&\text{\footnotesize collect the }p\text{\footnotesize\ terms}\\
      &=-p+e^{-g_yT}\,\underbrace{\mathbb{P}(\ST\ge K)}_{\text{true}} &&\text{\footnotesize probabilities sum to one}
    \end{aligned}
  \]
  }%
  The bet has a non-negative expected NPV when $p\le e^{-g_yT}\,\mathbb{P}(\ST\ge K)$.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  The bet is a \hilite{derivative contract}: its payoff depends on the future price
  of another asset (or event). Every buyer pays one market price, yet buyers with different
  beliefs value the same contract differently.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \textbf{\ink{How is the market price set?}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Is a Derivative?}
  A \hilite{derivative} is a contract between a buyer and a seller to transact an
  asset at a set price on a future date. It is an abstract asset whose cash
  flows depend on an underlying asset or event.
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.25em}
    \item \hilite{Futures, forwards, and swaps}: obligate both parties to
      transact. Futures trade on exchanges with standard terms. Forwards trade
      over the counter, where each party bears the other's default risk. Swaps
      exchange one asset's cash flows for another's, also over the counter.
    \item \hilite{Options}: the buyer holds the right, but not the obligation, to
      buy (call) or sell (put) at a set price. Options are exchange-traded.
    \item \hilite{Event contracts}: pay a fixed amount, usually one dollar, if an
      event occurs and nothing otherwise. Kalshi lists them.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Business Case for Long and Short Calls}
  A \hilite{call} option gives the owner the right, but not the obligation, to
  buy 100 shares of an underlying asset (per contract) at the strike price $K$
  by the expiration date.
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.6em}
    \item \hilite{Buyer (long)}: benefits from upside price movement without
      purchasing the asset directly. Long calls have defined risk: the maximum
      loss is the premium paid. If the owner exercises, they buy shares at $K$
      instead of the market price.
    \item \hilite{Seller (short)}: receives the premium. Short calls have
      unlimited upside risk, so calls are often sold only by investors who
      already own the shares, as a covered call (100 shares per contract).
      Short calls obligate the seller to sell shares at $K$ if the option is
      exercised.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Business Case for Long and Short Puts}
  A \hilite{put} option gives the owner the right, but not the obligation, to
  sell 100 shares at the strike price $K$ by the expiration date.
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.6em}
    \item \hilite{Buyer (long)}: benefits from downside price movement without
      selling the asset directly. Long puts have defined risk: the maximum loss
      is the premium paid. The owner can sell shares at $K$ instead of the
      market price.
    \item \hilite{Seller (short)}: receives the premium. Short puts have large
      but bounded downside risk: if the share price falls to zero, the seller
      loses the strike price minus the premium's future value per share. Puts
      are often sold only by investors with the cash to buy the shares (100 per
      contract), a cash-secured put. Short puts obligate the seller to buy shares at $K$ if
      the option is exercised.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Call and Put Payoffs}
  The payoff per share of a single call or put contract at expiration (or when
  the contract is exercised) is:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      V_{c}(K,\ST) & = \max\bigl(\ST-K,~0\bigr) = \bigl(\ST-K\bigr)^{+}\\
      V_{p}(K,\ST) & = \max\bigl(K-\ST,~0\bigr) = \bigl(K-\ST\bigr)^{+}
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
  where $K$ is the strike price and $\ST$ is the share price at expiration
  (time $t=T$). The seller charges the buyer a premium per share, $\Pc$ for a
  call and $\Pp$ for a put.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Profit at Expiration}
  The premium is paid at $t=0$ and the payoff arrives at $t=T$, so we compare
  them at expiration using the premium's future value, $\Dfv\cdot\mathcal{P}$,
  where $\Dfv=e^{g_{y}T}$. The buyer's profit per share for a single contract
  is:
  \begin{align*}
    P_{c}^{\text{buyer}}(K,\ST) & = V_{c}(K,\ST)-\Dfv\cdot\Pc\\
    P_{p}^{\text{buyer}}(K,\ST) & = V_{p}(K,\ST)-\Dfv\cdot\Pp
  \end{align*}
  A position's \hilite{orientation} $\theta$ is $+1$ for the buyer (long) and
  $-1$ for the seller (short). Its profit per share is
  $\theta\cdot P^{\text{buyer}}$, so the seller's profit is
  $P^{\text{seller}}=(-1)\cdot P^{\text{buyer}}$, and
  $P^{\text{buyer}}+P^{\text{seller}}=0$ at every share price.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \textbf{\ink{Options are a zero-sum game:}} one party's gain is the other's
  loss.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Breakeven}
  Setting the buyer's profit to zero gives the breakeven share price
  $S^{\ddagger}$ at expiration:
  \begin{align*}
    S^{\ddagger} & = K+\Dfv\cdot\Pc && \text{call}\\
    S^{\ddagger} & = K-\Dfv\cdot\Pp && \text{put}
  \end{align*}
  Above the call breakeven, and below the put breakeven, the buyer profits and
  the seller loses. Listed contracts often expire within weeks, so $\Dfv$ is
  close to one (about 1.003 for 30 days at $g_{y}=4\%$).
  \slidenote{Example 1:}{\href{\repo/CHEME-5660-L9a-Example-SingleContractPayoffProfit-Fall-2026.ipynb}{\dimmed{CHEME-5660-L9a-Example-SingleContractPayoffProfit-Fall-2026.ipynb}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pricing Put and Call Options Contracts}
  An option's price depends on the underlying asset's value, strike price, time
  to expiration, and exercise style.
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item \hilite{European-style}: exercise only at expiration.
    \item \hilite{American-style}: exercise any time through expiration.
    \item \hilite{Bermudan-style}: less common, exercise on specified dates.
  \end{itemize}
  The American option's added flexibility makes it worth at least as much as an
  otherwise identical European option.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  Options on single stocks and on exchange-traded funds such as SPY are
  American-style. The European-style contracts we can trade are cash-settled
  index options: SPX (SPXW for weekly expirations), XSP, NDX, and RUT.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  Let's start with European-style options and compute their net present value.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Options as Abstract Assets}
  \vspace{-0.6em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{../figs/Fig-L9a-Option-CashFlows.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.5em}
  The buyer pays the premium $\mathcal{P}$ today and receives the payoff
  $V(K,\ST)\geq 0$ at expiration, so the net present value at $t=0$ is:
  \[
    \operatorname{NPV}_{0}=-\mathcal{P}+\Dinv\cdot V(K,\ST)
  \]
  Factoring out $\Dinv$ gives $\operatorname{NPV}_{0}=\Dinv\cdot P^{\text{buyer}}$:
  the NPV is the buyer's profit at expiration discounted to today, so it is
  zero at the breakeven share price.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The European Premium}
  \textbf{\ink{How is the market price set?}} A call is a bet whose payoff grows
  with the share price. It pays $\ST-K$ instead of 1 USD when $\ST>K$, and
  nothing otherwise. Following the same steps as for the bet:
  {\small
  \[
    \begin{alignedat}{2}
      \E[\operatorname{NPV}_0]&=\left(-\mathcal{P}+\Dinv\,\bar{V}\right)\mathbb{P}(\ST>K)+(-\mathcal{P})\,\mathbb{P}(\ST\le K) &\enspace&\text{\footnotesize weighted sum}\\
      &=-\mathcal{P}\,\underbrace{\left[\mathbb{P}(\ST>K)+\mathbb{P}(\ST\le K)\right]}_{=1}+\Dinv\,\bar{V}\,\mathbb{P}(\ST>K) &&\text{\footnotesize collect }\mathcal{P}\text{\footnotesize\ terms}\\
      &=-\mathcal{P}+\Dinv\,\mathbb{P}(\ST>K)\,\bar{V} &&\text{\footnotesize sum to one}
    \end{alignedat}
  \]
  }%
  where $\bar{V}$ is the average payoff $\ST-K$ over the outcomes with $\ST>K$.
  The expected NPV is zero when the premium is the discounted chance of exercise
  times the average payoff when exercised.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Whose Probabilities?}
  $\mathbb{P}$ holds our own beliefs about the future share price $\ST$, so this
  premium is the most we would pay. The market prices every option with one
  shared \hilite{risk-neutral pricing rule} $\Q$, so there is one price. Under
  $\Q$, the expected share price grows at the risk-free rate $g_{y}$, and GBM
  with no dividends has drift $g_{y}$. Repeating the calculation under $\Q$:
  {\small
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \E_{\Q}[\operatorname{NPV}_0]&=\left(-\mathcal{P}+\Dinv\,\bar{V}\right)\Q(\ST>K)+(-\mathcal{P})\,\Q(\ST\le K)\\
      &=-\mathcal{P}\,\underbrace{\left[\Q(\ST>K)+\Q(\ST\le K)\right]}_{=1}\\
      &\qquad+\Dinv\,\underbrace{\left[\bar{V}\,\Q(\ST>K)+0\cdot\Q(\ST\le K)\right]}_{=\E_{\Q}[V_{c}(K,\ST)]}\\
      &=-\mathcal{P}+\Dinv\,\E_{\Q}\bigl[V_{c}(K,\ST)\bigr]
    \end{aligned}
  \]
  }%
  where $\bar{V}$ is now the average payoff over the outcomes with $\ST>K$
  under $\Q$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Market Premium}
  The second bracket is the expected payoff of the call under $\Q$: the payoff
  in each outcome, weighted by its probability. Setting
  $\E_{\Q}[\operatorname{NPV}_0]=0$ gives the market premium of the call,
  $\Pc=\Dinv\,\E_{\Q}\bigl[V_{c}(K,\ST)\bigr]$.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  A put, which pays $K-\ST$ when $\ST<K$, gives the same result with $V_{p}$ in
  place of $V_{c}$. For either contract, the premium is the discounted expected
  payoff under $\Q$:
  \[
    \boxed{\mathcal{P}^{\text{EU}}=\Dinv\cdot\E_{\Q}\bigl[V(K,\ST)\bigr]}
  \]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Event Contracts}
  The Concept Review bet is an \hilite{event contract} on $\ST\geq K$, which
  pays 1 USD if the event occurs and nothing otherwise. Its market price is:
  \[
    p=\Dinv\cdot\E_{\Q}\bigl[\mathbb{1}\{\text{event}\}\bigr]=\Dinv\cdot\Q(\text{event})
  \]
  The pricing probability $\Q(\text{event})$ can differ from the real-world
  probability $\mathbb{P}(\text{event})$. A general Kalshi event, such as an
  election, is not tied to a share price, so choosing its pricing probability
  requires additional assumptions.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Monte Carlo Premium}
  Simulation gives a direct way to estimate the market premium. The payoff
  depends only on $\ST$, so we draw it from the solution of GBM with drift $g_{y}$.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \hilite{Initialize}: given $S(0)$, $K$, the payoff function $V(K,S)$ of the
  call or put, $T$ (years), $\sigma$, $g_{y}$, and the number of simulated
  paths $M$.
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item For each path $i=1,2,\dots,M$ \textbf{do}:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item[a.] Draw a standard normal random number $Z_{i}$.
        \item[b.] Compute the share price at expiration:
          $S_{i}(T)\gets S(0)\cdot\exp\bigl[(g_{y}-\sigma^{2}/2)\,T+\sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z_{i}\bigr]$.
        \item[c.] Compute the payoff: $V_{i}\gets V(K,S_{i}(T))$.
      \end{itemize}
    \item The premium is the discounted average payoff:
      $\mathcal{P}\gets\Dinv\cdot\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}V_{i}$.
  \end{enumerate}
  \slidenote{Optional:}{an alternative way to think about the premium, in the \href{\repo/advanced/contingent_claims/CHEME-5660-L9a-Advanced-ContingentClaims-BSM-Fall-2026.ipynb}{contingent claims notebook}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Black–Scholes–Merton Equation}
  Merton and Scholes received the 1997 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences for this
  model. Fischer Black had passed away before the prize was awarded.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Assume the price of a non-dividend-paying share follows GBM with constant
  volatility $\sigma$, and the risk-free rate $g_{y}$ is constant. Write the
  premium at time $t$, when the share price is $S$, as $\mathcal{P}(S,t)$, with
  $\mathcal{P}(S,T)=V(K,S)$. It satisfies:
  \[
    \boxed{\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial t}+g_{y}\,S\,\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial S}+\frac{1}{2}\,\sigma^{2}S^{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{P}}{\partial S^{2}}=g_{y}\,\mathcal{P}}
  \]
  Only $g_{y}$ and $\sigma$ appear, not the real-world growth rate of the share
  price. Solving backward from the payoff gives $\mathcal{P}(S(0),0)=\mathcal{P}^{\text{EU}}$,
  the premium the Monte Carlo simulation estimates.
  \slidenote{Derivation:}{the \href{\repo/advanced/contingent_claims/CHEME-5660-L9a-Advanced-ContingentClaims-BSM-Fall-2026.ipynb}{contingent claims notebook}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Black–Scholes–Merton Premiums}
  Instead of simulating, we can compute this premium with the closed-form
  European call and put formulas:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      \Pc & = N(d_{+})\cdot S(0)-N(d_{-})\cdot K\cdot\Dinv\\
      \Pp & = N(-d_{-})\cdot K\cdot\Dinv-N(-d_{+})\cdot S(0)
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
  where $N$ is the standard normal CDF, $T$ is in years, and:
  \[
    d_{+}=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\left[\ln\frac{S(0)}{K}+\Bigl(g_{y}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Bigr)T\right],
    \qquad d_{-}=d_{+}-\sigma\sqrt{T}
  \]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Historical or Implied Volatility?}
  There are two ways to put a number on $\sigma$, and the second also gives a
  quick range for the share price:
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
    \item \hilite{Historical volatility}: previously, we estimated $\sigma$ from
      past share prices as
      $\sigma_{\mathrm{ann}}=\sqrt{\Delta t}\cdot\sigma_{g}$, where
      $\sigma_{g}$ is the standard deviation of the growth rates and $\Delta t$
      is the time between price observations. This estimate looks backward.
    \item \hilite{Implied volatility}: the
      \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implied_volatility}{implied volatility}
      (IV) is the market's forecast of a likely movement in the share price,
      the volatility ``implied'' by an option's market price through a pricing
      model such as Black--Scholes--Merton. It is often equal to or
      larger than the historical volatility.
    \item \hilite{How can we use it?} IV represents a one-standard-deviation
      movement in the share price over the next year. Over a horizon of $T$
      years, the size of that move scales with $\sqrt{T}$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Back-of-the-Envelope Range from IV}
  Moving one standard deviation up or down in the solution of geometric
  Brownian motion gives a back-of-the-envelope range for the share price:
  {\small
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
      S(T) & = S(0)\exp\left[\mu_{g}T + \sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z\right] && \text{solution of geometric Brownian motion}\\
      S_{\pm} & = S(0)\exp\left[\mu_{g}T \pm \text{IV}\sqrt{T}\right] && \text{set } \sigma=\text{IV} \text{ and } Z=\pm 1\\
      & \approx S(0)\exp\left[\pm\,\text{IV}\sqrt{T}\right] && \mu_{g}T \text{ is small next to } \text{IV}\sqrt{T} \text{ for short } T\\
      & \approx S(0)\left(1 \pm \text{IV}\sqrt{T}\right) && e^{x}\approx 1+x \text{ for small } x
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}}%
  Here $\mu_{g}$ is the mean growth rate, $Z$ is a standard normal random
  variable, and $S_{\pm}$ is the share price one standard deviation above or
  below $S(0)$. For example, a share at $S(0)$ = 100 USD/share with IV = 40\%
  and 30 days to expiration ($T$ = 30/365 years) has $\text{IV}\sqrt{T}$ =
  0.115, so the range is about 88.5 to 111.5 USD/share. Under the model, the
  share price ends inside this range about 68\% of the time.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Which \texorpdfstring{$\sigma$}{Sigma}?}
  If we knew the right volatility, the formula would return the market premium.
  In practice, we run it backward. Like a bond's yield to maturity, the
  \hilite{implied volatility} $\hat{\sigma}$ is the $\sigma$ at which the BSM
  premium matches the market premium $\mathcal{P}^{\text{market}}$.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \hilite{Initialize}: given $\mathcal{P}^{\text{market}}$, $S(0)$, $K$, $T$
  (years), $g_{y}$, a bracket $\sigma_{\text{lo}}<\sigma_{\text{hi}}$ with
  $\mathcal{P}(\sigma_{\text{lo}})\leq\mathcal{P}^{\text{market}}\leq\mathcal{P}(\sigma_{\text{hi}})$,
  a tolerance $\epsilon>0$, $\texttt{maxiter}$, and $i\gets 1$.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  While $\sigma_{\text{hi}}-\sigma_{\text{lo}}>\epsilon$ and $i\leq\texttt{maxiter}$ \textbf{do}:
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.2em}
    \item Compute $\sigma_{\text{mid}}\gets(\sigma_{\text{lo}}+\sigma_{\text{hi}})/2$
      and its BSM premium $\mathcal{P}(\sigma_{\text{mid}})$.
    \item If $\mathcal{P}(\sigma_{\text{mid}})<\mathcal{P}^{\text{market}}$,
      \textbf{then} $\sigma_{\text{lo}}\gets\sigma_{\text{mid}}$. Otherwise,
      $\sigma_{\text{hi}}\gets\sigma_{\text{mid}}$.
    \item Update the iteration counter $i\gets i+1$.
  \end{enumerate}
  The implied volatility is the midpoint of the final bracket.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Implied Volatility in Practice}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.6em}
    \item \hilite{One answer}: the BSM premium rises with $\sigma$, so exactly
      one $\sigma$ matches the market premium, and each step halves the bracket
      around it.
    \item \hilite{Why the formula}: the Monte Carlo premium changes with the
      random draws and costs $M$ simulated paths at every step.
    \item \hilite{One per contract}: each strike and expiration has its own
      implied volatility, although the BSM model assumes one constant $\sigma$. To price a contract without a quote, or on a future
      date, we can predict it from a surface fitted to today's quotes
      (\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.13998}{Sun et al., 2026}).
  \end{itemize}
  \slidenote{Optional:}{the \href{\repo/advanced/spxw_volatility_skew/CHEME-5660-L9a-Advanced-SPXW-Volatility-Skew-Fall-2026.ipynb}{SPXW volatility skew notebook} applies this bisection to a chain of S\&P 500 index options.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Put–Call Parity for European Contracts}
  For a European call and put with the same strike and expiration, the payoffs
  differ by $\ST-K$ at every share price. Subtracting the put premium from the
  call premium gives:
  {\small
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \Pc-\Pp&=\Dinv\cdot\E_{\Q}\bigl[V_{c}(K,\ST)-V_{p}(K,\ST)\bigr] &&\text{\footnotesize premium formula}\\
      &=\Dinv\cdot\E_{\Q}\bigl[\ST-K\bigr] &&\text{\footnotesize payoff identity}\\
      &=\Dinv\cdot\bigl(S(0)\,e^{g_{y}T}-K\bigr) &&\text{\footnotesize expected share price grows at }g_{y}\\
      &=S(0)-K\cdot\Dinv &&\Dinv=e^{-g_{y}T}
    \end{aligned}
  \]
  }%
  This is \hilite{put--call parity}: $\boxed{\Pc-\Pp=S(0)-K\cdot\Dinv}$

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \textbf{\ink{Parity holds only for European contracts.}} An American holder can
  exercise before expiration, so the difference between the call and put
  premiums is bounded, not fixed.
  \slidenote{Example 2:}{\href{\repo/CHEME-5660-L9a-Example-BSM-Premium-Fall-2026.ipynb}{\dimmed{CHEME-5660-L9a-Example-BSM-Premium-Fall-2026.ipynb}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What About American-Style Options?}
  The holder can exercise at any time $\tau\leq T$, chosen using only the
  information available at that time. The premium is the largest expected
  payoff, discounted from $\tau$ back to today, over all such exercise times:
  \[
    \mathcal{P}^{\text{AM}}=\max_{\tau\leq T}\E_{\Q}\Bigl[\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{\tau,0}(g_{y})\cdot V(K,S(\tau))\Bigr]\geq\mathcal{P}^{\text{EU}}
  \]
  The American put has no closed-form formula like BSM, so its premium is
  usually computed on a binomial lattice.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}
  In this lecture, we explored derivatives, a fundamental asset class used for
  risk management, hedging, and speculation.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{Key takeaways}}}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item \hilite{Derivatives derive their value from underlying assets}:
      common types are futures, forwards, swaps, and options, each with
      different characteristics, maturities, and uses.
    \item \hilite{Option payoffs are determined by strike prices and contract
      orientation}: payoff diagrams at expiration show profitability and risk
      exposure.
    \item \hilite{The premium makes the expected NPV zero under the
      risk-neutral pricing rule}: Monte Carlo simulation or the BSM formulas
      compute it for European contracts, and put--call parity links calls and
      puts.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
