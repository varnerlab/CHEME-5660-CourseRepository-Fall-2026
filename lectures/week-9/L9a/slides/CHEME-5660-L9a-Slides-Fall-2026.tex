\begin{frame}{Concept Review: A Bet on the Future}
  Suppose we pay $p$ today for a contract that pays 1 USD in $T$ years if the
  share price $\ST$ finishes at or above a target share price $K$, and nothing
  otherwise.
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.34\textheight,keepaspectratio]{../figs/Fig-L9a-Bet-CashFlows.pdf}
  \end{center}
  Discounting the payoff $V(\ST)$ at the benchmark growth rate $g_y$, with
  $\Dinv=e^{-g_yT}$, gives a random NPV:
  \[
    \operatorname{NPV}_0=-p+\Dinv\,V(\ST)=\begin{cases}-p+e^{-g_yT}&\text{if }\ST\ge K\text{ (true)}\\-p&\text{if }\ST<K\text{ (false)}\end{cases}
  \]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Expected NPV of the Bet}
  Under our beliefs $\mathbb{P}$ about $\ST$, the expected NPV is:
  {\small
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \E[\operatorname{NPV}_0]&=\left(-p+e^{-g_yT}\right)\underbrace{\mathbb{P}(\ST\ge K)}_{\text{true}}+(-p)\,\underbrace{\mathbb{P}(\ST<K)}_{\text{false}} &&\text{\footnotesize weight by probability}\\
      &=-p\,\underbrace{\left[\mathbb{P}(\ST\ge K)+\mathbb{P}(\ST<K)\right]}_{=1}+e^{-g_yT}\,\mathbb{P}(\ST\ge K) &&\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}l@{}}\footnotesize collect the $p$ terms,\\ \footnotesize probabilities sum to one\end{tabular}\\
      &=-p+e^{-g_yT}\,\underbrace{\mathbb{P}(\ST\ge K)}_{\text{true}}
    \end{aligned}
  \]
  }%
  The expected NPV is the discounted expected payoff minus the price paid
  today.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Bet Is a Derivative Contract}
  The bet has a non-negative expected NPV when its price is at or below its
  discounted expected payoff:
  \[
    p\le e^{-g_yT}\,\mathbb{P}(\ST\ge K)
  \]
  The bet is a \hilite{derivative contract}: its price and payoff depend on the
  future price of another underlying asset (or event).

  \vspace{0.6em}
  Each contract trades at a market price that every buyer pays, yet buyers with
  different beliefs $\mathbb{P}$ value the same contract differently.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \textbf{\ink{How is the market price set?}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Is a Derivative?}
  A \hilite{derivative} is a contract between a buyer and a seller to transact an
  asset at a set price on a future date. It is an abstract asset whose cash
  flows depend on an underlying asset or event.
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
    \item \hilite{Futures, forwards, and swaps}: obligate both parties to
      transact. Futures trade on exchanges with standard terms. Forwards trade
      over the counter, where the two parties negotiate the terms, and if one
      party cannot pay, the other takes the loss. Swaps exchange one asset's cash
      flows for another's, also over the counter.
    \item \hilite{Options}: the buyer holds the right, but not the obligation, to
      buy (call) or sell (put) at a set price. Stock, index, and ETF options are
      exchange-traded.
    \item \hilite{Event contracts}: pay a fixed amount, usually one dollar, if an
      event occurs and nothing otherwise. Kalshi lists them.
  \end{itemize}
  We focus on options in this lecture.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Call and Put Options Contracts}
  The two types of options contracts are \hilite{calls} and \hilite{puts}. In
  each, the buyer holds the right and the seller holds the obligation:
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth,height=0.54\textheight,keepaspectratio]{../figs/Fig-L9a-Contract-Right-Obligation.pdf}
  \end{center}
  The buyer always pays the premium. The shares move at the strike price only if
  the buyer exercises.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Business Case for Long and Short Calls}
  A \hilite{call} gives the owner the right, but not the obligation, to buy 100
  shares (per contract) from the seller at the strike price $K$ by the
  expiration date. What is the business case for each side?
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.6em}
    \item \hilite{Buyer (long)}: can benefit from upside price movement without
      buying the shares. If the buyer exercises, they buy shares at $K$ instead
      of the market price. The most the buyer can lose is the premium.
    \item \hilite{Seller (short)}: receives the premium and must sell shares at
      $K$ if the call is exercised. Calls are often sold by investors who
      already own the shares, 100 per contract (a covered call).
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  The buyer's risk is limited to the premium. The seller's risk has no upper
  limit, because the share price can rise without bound.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Business Case for Long and Short Puts}
  A \hilite{put} gives the owner the right, but not the obligation, to sell 100
  shares (per contract) to the seller at the strike price $K$ by the expiration
  date. What is the business case for each side?
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.6em}
    \item \hilite{Buyer (long)}: can benefit from downside price movement
      without selling the shares. If the buyer exercises, they sell shares at
      $K$ instead of the market price. The most the buyer can lose is the
      premium.
    \item \hilite{Seller (short)}: receives the premium and must buy shares at
      $K$ if the put is exercised. Puts are often sold by investors with the
      cash to buy the shares, 100 per contract (a cash-secured put).
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  The buyer's risk is limited to the premium. The seller's risk is large but
  bounded: at a share price of zero, the seller loses $K$ minus the premium's
  future value per share.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Call and Put Payoffs}
  The payoff per share of a single call or put contract at expiration (or when
  the contract is exercised) is:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      V_{c}(K,\ST) & = \max\bigl(\ST-K,~0\bigr) = \bigl(\ST-K\bigr)^{+}\\
      V_{p}(K,\ST) & = \max\bigl(K-\ST,~0\bigr) = \bigl(K-\ST\bigr)^{+}
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
  where $K$ is the strike price and $\ST$ is the share price at expiration
  (time $t=T$). The seller charges the buyer a premium per share, $\Pc$ for a
  call and $\Pp$ for a put.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  The premium is paid at $t=0$ and the payoff arrives at $t=T$, so we compare
  them at expiration using the premium's future value $\Dfv\cdot\mathcal{P}$,
  where $\Dfv=e^{g_{y}T}$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Profit at Expiration}
  The buyer's profit per share for a single contract at expiration is the
  payoff minus the premium's future value:
  \begin{align*}
    P_{c}^{\text{buyer}}(K,\ST) & = V_{c}(K,\ST)-\Dfv\cdot\Pc\\
    P_{p}^{\text{buyer}}(K,\ST) & = V_{p}(K,\ST)-\Dfv\cdot\Pp
  \end{align*}
  A position's \hilite{orientation} $\theta$ is $+1$ for the buyer (long) and
  $-1$ for the seller (short). Its profit per share is $\theta\cdot
  P^{\text{buyer}}$, so $P^{\text{seller}}=(-1)\cdot P^{\text{buyer}}$ and
  $P^{\text{buyer}}+P^{\text{seller}}=0$ at every share price.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \textbf{\ink{Options are a zero-sum game:}} one party's gain is the other's
  loss.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Breakeven}
  Setting the buyer's profit to zero gives the breakeven share price
  $S^{\ddagger}$ at expiration:
  \begin{align*}
    S^{\ddagger} & = K+\Dfv\cdot\Pc && \text{call}\\
    S^{\ddagger} & = K-\Dfv\cdot\Pp && \text{put}
  \end{align*}
  Above the call breakeven, and below the put breakeven, the buyer profits and
  the seller loses.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  Listed contracts often expire within weeks, so $\Dfv$ is close to one (about
  1.003 for 30 days at $g_{y}=4\%$). For longer-dated contracts it matters: a
  1-year contract at $g_{y}=4\%$ has $\Dfv=e^{0.04}\approx 1.041$, which moves
  the breakeven farther from the strike.
  \slidenote{Example 1:}{\href{\repo/CHEME-5660-L9a-Example-SingleContractPayoffProfit-Fall-2026.ipynb}{\dimmed{CHEME-5660-L9a-Example-SingleContractPayoffProfit-Fall-2026.ipynb}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pricing Put and Call Options Contracts}
  An option's price depends on the underlying asset's value, strike price, time
  to expiration, and exercise style.
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item \hilite{European-style}: exercise only at expiration.
    \item \hilite{American-style}: exercise any time through expiration.
  \end{itemize}
  The American option's added flexibility makes it worth at least as much as an
  otherwise identical European option.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  Options on single stocks and on exchange-traded funds such as SPY are
  American-style. The European-style contracts we can trade are cash-settled
  index options: SPX (SPXW for weekly expirations), XSP, NDX, and RUT.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  Let's start with European-style options and compute their net present value.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Options as Abstract Assets}
  \vspace{-0.6em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{../figs/Fig-L9a-Option-CashFlows.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.5em}
  The buyer pays the premium $\mathcal{P}$ today and receives the payoff
  $V(K,\ST)\geq 0$ at expiration, so the net present value at $t=0$ is:
  \[
    \operatorname{NPV}_{0}=-\mathcal{P}+\Dinv\cdot V(K,\ST)
  \]
  Factoring out $\Dinv$ gives $\operatorname{NPV}_{0}=\Dinv\cdot P^{\text{buyer}}$:
  the NPV is the buyer's profit at expiration discounted to today, so it is
  zero at the breakeven share price.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The European Call Contract Premium}
  \textbf{\ink{How is the market price set?}} A call pays $\ST-K$ when $\ST>K$,
  and nothing otherwise. Following the same steps as for the bet:
  {\small
  \[
    \begin{alignedat}{2}
      \E[\operatorname{NPV}_0]&=\left(-\mathcal{P}+\Dinv\,\bar{V}\right)\mathbb{P}(\ST>K)+(-\mathcal{P})\,\mathbb{P}(\ST\le K) &\enspace&\text{\footnotesize weighted sum}\\
      &=-\mathcal{P}\,\underbrace{\left[\mathbb{P}(\ST>K)+\mathbb{P}(\ST\le K)\right]}_{=1}+\Dinv\,\bar{V}\,\mathbb{P}(\ST>K) &&\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}l@{}}\footnotesize collect $\mathcal{P}$ terms,\\ \footnotesize sum to one\end{tabular}\\
      &=-\mathcal{P}+\Dinv\,\mathbb{P}(\ST>K)\,\bar{V}
    \end{alignedat}
  \]
  }%
  where $\bar{V}$ is the average payoff $\ST-K$ over the outcomes with $\ST>K$.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  The expected NPV is non-negative when
  $\mathcal{P}\leq\Dinv\,\mathbb{P}(\ST>K)\,\bar{V}$: the discounted chance of
  exercise times the average payoff when exercised.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Whose Probabilities?}
  The bound on the premium from the last slide depends on whose probabilities
  we use:
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
    \item \hilite{Our beliefs $\mathbb{P}$}: the probabilities are our own
      beliefs about $\ST$, so this premium is the most we would pay. A buyer
      with different beliefs computes a different premium.
    \item \hilite{The market's rule $\Q$}: the market prices every option with
      one shared set of probabilities, the \hilite{risk-neutral pricing rule}
      $\Q$, so there is one price.
    \item \hilite{Growth under $\Q$}: the expected share price grows at the
      risk-free rate $g_{y}$. For geometric Brownian motion with no dividends,
      $\Q$ replaces the drift with $g_{y}$.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  Pricing with $\Q$ gives one market premium that does not depend on who buys
  the contract.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Expected NPV Under \texorpdfstring{$\Q$}{Q}}
  Repeating the expected NPV calculation with $\Q$ in place of $\mathbb{P}$
  gives:
  {\small
  \[
    \begin{alignedat}{2}
      \E_{\Q}[\operatorname{NPV}_0]&=\left(-\mathcal{P}+\Dinv\,\bar{V}\right)\Q(\ST>K)+(-\mathcal{P})\,\Q(\ST\le K) &\enspace&\text{\footnotesize weighted sum}\\
      &=-\mathcal{P}\,\underbrace{\left[\Q(\ST>K)+\Q(\ST\le K)\right]}_{=1} &&\text{\footnotesize collect terms}\\
      &\qquad+\Dinv\,\underbrace{\left[\bar{V}\,\Q(\ST>K)+0\cdot\Q(\ST\le K)\right]}_{=\E_{\Q}[V_{c}(K,\ST)]} &&\text{\footnotesize call pays 0 if }\ST\le K\\
      &=-\mathcal{P}+\Dinv\,\E_{\Q}\bigl[V_{c}(K,\ST)\bigr]
    \end{alignedat}
  \]
  }%
  where $\bar{V}$ is now the average payoff $\ST-K$ over the outcomes with
  $\ST>K$ under $\Q$.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  The second bracket is the expected payoff of the call under $\Q$: the payoff
  in each outcome, weighted by its probability.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Market Premium}
  The seller's NPV is the negative of the buyer's. The only premium that gives
  neither side a positive expected NPV under $\Q$ sets
  $\E_{\Q}[\operatorname{NPV}_0]=0$:
  \[
    \boxed{\mathcal{P}^{\text{EU}}_{c}=\Dinv\,\E_{\Q}\bigl[V_{c}(K,\ST)\bigr]}
  \]
  A put, which pays $K-\ST$ when $\ST<K$, gives the same result with $V_{p}$ in
  place of $V_{c}$. For either contract, with payoff $V_{\star}(K,\ST)$:
  \[
    \boxed{\mathcal{P}_{\star}^{\text{EU}}=\Dinv\cdot\E_{\Q}\bigl[V_{\star}(K,\ST)\bigr]\quad\text{where }\star\in\{c,p\}}
  \]
  The European premium is the discounted expected payoff under $\Q$, one price
  for all buyers. Monte Carlo simulation and the Black--Scholes--Merton formulas
  are two ways to compute it.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Monte Carlo Premium}
  Simulation estimates the market premium directly. The payoff depends only on
  $\ST$, so we draw it from the solution of geometric Brownian motion (GBM) with
  drift $g_{y}$ and volatility $\sigma$.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \hilite{Initialize}: given $S(0)$, $K$, the payoff $V(K,S)$, $T$ (years),
  $\sigma$, $g_{y}$, and $M$ paths.
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item For each path $i=1,2,\dots,M$ \textbf{do}:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item[a.] Draw a standard normal random number $Z_{i}$.
        \item[b.] $S_{i}(T)\gets S(0)\cdot\exp\bigl[(g_{y}-\sigma^{2}/2)\,T+\sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z_{i}\bigr]$.
        \item[c.] $V_{i}\gets V(K,S_{i}(T))$.
      \end{itemize}
    \item Discount the average payoff:
      $\mathcal{P}\gets\Dinv\cdot\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}V_{i}$.
  \end{enumerate}
  The result is an estimate: it changes with the random draws, and it becomes
  more precise as $M$ grows.
  \slidenote{Optional:}{an alternative way to think about the premium, in the \href{\repo/advanced/contingent_claims/CHEME-5660-L9a-Advanced-ContingentClaims-BSM-Fall-2026.ipynb}{contingent claims notebook}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Black–Scholes–Merton Equation}
  Merton and Scholes received the 1997 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences for this
  model. Fischer Black had passed away before the award.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Assume the price of a non-dividend-paying share follows GBM with constant
  $\sigma$ and a constant, continuously compounded risk-free rate $g_{y}$. The premium $\mathcal{P}(S,t)$, with
  $\mathcal{P}(S,T)=V(K,S)$, satisfies:
  \[
    \boxed{\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial t}+g_{y}\,S\,\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial S}+\frac{1}{2}\,\sigma^{2}S^{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{P}}{\partial S^{2}}=g_{y}\,\mathcal{P}}
  \]
  Solving it backward in time from the payoff gives today's premium,
  $\mathcal{P}(S(0),0)$, and this solution equals the risk-neutral premium
  $\mathcal{P}^{\text{EU}}$ that the Monte Carlo simulation estimates.
  \slidenote{Derivation:}{the \href{\repo/advanced/contingent_claims/CHEME-5660-L9a-Advanced-ContingentClaims-BSM-Fall-2026.ipynb}{contingent claims notebook}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Black–Scholes–Merton Premiums}
  Instead of simulating, we can compute this premium with the closed-form
  European call and put formulas:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      \Pc & = N(d_{+})\cdot S(0)-N(d_{-})\cdot K\cdot\Dinv\\
      \Pp & = N(-d_{-})\cdot K\cdot\Dinv-N(-d_{+})\cdot S(0)
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
  where $N$ is the standard normal CDF, $T$ is in years, and:
  \[
    d_{+}=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{S(0)}{K}\right)+\Bigl(g_{y}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Bigr)T\right],
    \qquad d_{-}=d_{+}-\sigma\sqrt{T}
  \]
  We know $S(0)$, $K$, $T$, and $g_{y}$ when we price the contract. The
  volatility $\sigma$ is the one input we must choose.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Which Volatility Should We Use?}
  If we knew the right volatility, the BSM model (or the Monte Carlo model)
  would compute the observed market premium. In practice, we observe the market
  premium and back out the volatility that makes the model match it.
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.6em}
    \item \hilite{Historical volatility}:
      $\sigma_{\mathrm{ann}}=\sqrt{\Delta t}\cdot\sigma_{g}$, where $\sigma_{g}$
      is the standard deviation of past growth rates (units: $\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$)
      and $\Delta t$ is the time between observations (units: years). It looks
      backward.
    \item \hilite{Implied volatility}
      (\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implied_volatility}{IV}): the
      market's forecast of a likely future movement in the share price. It looks
      forward, and it is often at or above the historical volatility.
    \item \hilite{How can we use it?} IV represents a one-standard-deviation
      movement in the share price over the next year. Over $T$ years, the move
      scales with $\sqrt{T}$.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  This $\sqrt{T}$ scaling gives a quick range for the share price on the next
  slide.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Back-of-the-Envelope Range from IV}
  Moving one standard deviation up or down in the solution of geometric
  Brownian motion, $Z=\pm1$, gives a back-of-the-envelope range for the share
  price:
  {\small
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
      S(T) & = S(0)\exp\left[\mu_{g}T + \sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z\right] && \text{solution of geometric Brownian motion}\\
      S_{\pm} & = S(0)\exp\left[\mu_{g}T \pm \text{IV}\sqrt{T}\right] && \text{set } \sigma=\text{IV} \text{ and } Z=\pm 1\\
      & \approx S(0)\exp\left[\pm\,\text{IV}\sqrt{T}\right] && \mu_{g}T \text{ is small next to } \text{IV}\sqrt{T} \text{ for short } T\\
      & \approx S(0)\left(1 \pm \text{IV}\sqrt{T}\right) && e^{x}\approx 1+x \text{ for small } x
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}}%
  where $\mu_{g}$ is the mean growth rate, $Z$ is standard normal, and $S_{\pm}$
  is the share price one standard deviation above or below $S(0)$. Under the
  model, the share price ends inside this range about 68\% of the time.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Implied Volatility by Bisection}
  We back out the implied volatility $\hat{\sigma}$ by bisection.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \hilite{Initialize}: given $\mathcal{P}^{\text{market}}$, $S(0)$, $K$, $T$
  (years), $g_{y}$, a bracket $\sigma_{\text{lo}}<\sigma_{\text{hi}}$ with
  $\mathcal{P}(\sigma_{\text{lo}})\leq\mathcal{P}^{\text{market}}\leq\mathcal{P}(\sigma_{\text{hi}})$,
  a tolerance $\epsilon>0$, $\texttt{maxiter}$, and $i\gets 1$.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  While $\sigma_{\text{hi}}-\sigma_{\text{lo}}>\epsilon$ and $i\leq\texttt{maxiter}$ \textbf{do}:
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.2em}
    \item $\sigma_{\text{mid}}\gets(\sigma_{\text{lo}}+\sigma_{\text{hi}})/2$
      and compute its BSM premium $\mathcal{P}(\sigma_{\text{mid}})$.
    \item If $\mathcal{P}(\sigma_{\text{mid}})<\mathcal{P}^{\text{market}}$,
      \textbf{then} $\sigma_{\text{lo}}\gets\sigma_{\text{mid}}$. Otherwise,
      $\sigma_{\text{hi}}\gets\sigma_{\text{mid}}$.
    \item $i\gets i+1$.
  \end{enumerate}
  The implied volatility is the midpoint of the final bracket,
  $\hat{\sigma}\gets(\sigma_{\text{lo}}+\sigma_{\text{hi}})/2$. The BSM premium
  rises with $\sigma$, so exactly one $\sigma$ matches the market premium, and
  each step halves the bracket around it.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Implied Volatility in Practice}
  Three points about using implied volatility with real quotes:
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.6em}
    \item \hilite{Market quotes}: the SPXW volatility skew notebook applies this
      bisection to a chain of S\&P 500 index options.
    \item \hilite{One per contract}: each strike and expiration has its own
      implied volatility, although the BSM model assumes one constant $\sigma$.
      This pattern is known as the volatility smile or volatility skew.
    \item \hilite{Pricing without a quote}: to price a contract without a quote,
      or on a future date, we can predict its implied volatility from a surface
      fitted to today's quotes, which gives implied volatility as a function of
      strike and expiration
      (\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.13998}{Sun et al., 2026}).
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  Next, we link the call and put premiums with the same strike and
  expiration.

\begin{frame}{Put–Call Parity: The Payoff Identity}
  The call and put premiums are linked. For a European call and put with the
  same strike and expiration, the payoffs differ by $\ST-K$ at every share
  price:
  {\small
  \[
    \begin{alignedat}{2}
      V_{c}(K,\ST)-V_{p}(K,\ST)&=\max\bigl(\ST-K,~0\bigr)-\max\bigl(K-\ST,~0\bigr) &\enspace&\text{\footnotesize payoff definitions}\\
      &=\begin{cases}\bigl(\ST-K\bigr)-0&\text{if }\ST>K\text{: put pays 0}\\0-\bigl(K-\ST\bigr)&\text{if }\ST\le K\text{: call pays 0}\end{cases} &&\text{\footnotesize split at }K\\
      &=\ST-K &&\text{\footnotesize simplify both cases}
    \end{alignedat}
  \]
  }%
  Because the payoffs differ by $\ST-K$ in every outcome, the premiums differ
  by the discounted expected value of $\ST-K$ under $\Q$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Put–Call Parity for European Contracts}
  Subtracting the put premium from the call premium gives:
  {\small
  \[
    \begin{alignedat}{2}
      &\Pc-\Pp\\
      &\quad=\Dinv\cdot\E_{\Q}\bigl[V_{c}(K,\ST)\bigr]-\Dinv\cdot\E_{\Q}\bigl[V_{p}(K,\ST)\bigr] &\enspace&\text{\footnotesize premium formula}\\
      &\quad=\Dinv\cdot\E_{\Q}\bigl[V_{c}(K,\ST)-V_{p}(K,\ST)\bigr] &\enspace&\text{\footnotesize expectation is linear}\\
      &\quad=\Dinv\cdot\E_{\Q}\bigl[\ST-K\bigr] &\enspace&\text{\footnotesize payoff identity}\\
      &\quad=\Dinv\cdot\bigl(S(0)\,e^{g_{y}T}-K\bigr) &\enspace&\text{\footnotesize expected share price grows at }g_{y}\\
      &\quad=S(0)-K\cdot\Dinv &\enspace&\Dinv=e^{-g_{y}T}
    \end{alignedat}
  \]
  }%
  This is \hilite{put--call parity}: $\boxed{\Pc-\Pp=S(0)-K\cdot\Dinv}$

  \vspace{0.1em}
  The call--put difference does not depend on $\sigma$. It depends only on
  $S(0)$, $K$, and the discount factor.
  \slidenote{Example 2:}{\href{\repo/CHEME-5660-L9a-Example-BSM-Premium-Fall-2026.ipynb}{\dimmed{CHEME-5660-L9a-Example-BSM-Premium-Fall-2026.ipynb}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What About American-Style Options?}
  \textbf{\ink{Put--call parity holds only for European contracts.}} The
  derivation compares the two payoffs on the expiration date, but an American
  holder can exercise before then. For American contracts, the difference
  between the call and put premiums is bounded, not fixed.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  The American holder can exercise at any time $\tau\leq T$, chosen using only
  the information available at that time. The premium is the largest expected
  payoff, discounted from $\tau$ back to today, over all such exercise times:
  \[
    \mathcal{P}_{\star}^{\text{AM}}=\max_{\tau\leq T}\E_{\Q}\Bigl[\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{\tau,0}(g_{y})\cdot V_{\star}(K,S(\tau))\Bigr]\geq\mathcal{P}_{\star}^{\text{EU}}\quad\text{where }\star\in\{c,p\}
  \]
  The American put has no closed-form formula like BSM, so its premium is
  usually computed on a binomial lattice.
\end{frame}

  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}

      different characteristics, maturities, and uses.

      orientation}: payoff diagrams at expiration show profitability and risk
      exposure.

      puts.

  important for financial decision-making.
