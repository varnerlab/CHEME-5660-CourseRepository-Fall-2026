    \item \hilite{Apply the binomial model to price American options}: build

    \item \hilite{Decompose option premiums into components}: intrinsic value
      (immediate exercise value) and extrinsic value (time value).

\begin{frame}{Concept Review: CRR Binomial Pricing Model}
  American style options contracts allow the holder to exercise the option
  \hilite{at any time} before or on the expiration date. In L9a, we wrote the
  premium of an American-style contract as:
  \[
    \PAM=\sup_{\tau\leq T}\E_{\Q}\Bigl(\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{\tau,0}(g_{y})\cdot V(K,S(\tau))\Bigr)\geq\PEU
  \]
  The exercise time $\tau$ is chosen using only the information available at
  that time (a \hilite{stopping time}). The American premium is at least the European
  one because the holder can choose when to exercise.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  The standard method to price American options is the Cox, Ross, Rubinstein
  (CRR) binomial model.
  \slidenote{Reference:}{Cox, J. C., Ross, S. A., and Rubinstein, M. (1979). Option pricing: A simplified approach. \href{https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0304405X79900151}{Journal of Financial Economics 7(3): 229}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Concept Review: The CRR Lattice}
  At each time step, the share price moves up by a factor $u$ with the
  \hilite{risk-neutral probability} $q$, or down by a factor $d$ with
  probability $(1-q)$:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      q = \frac{\mathcal{D}_{1,0}\left(g_{y}\right) - d}{u - d}\quad\blacksquare
    \end{aligned}}
    \qquad\qquad
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      u &= e^{\sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}} \\
      d &= e^{-\sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}}
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item \hilite{Discount factor}: $\mathcal{D}_{1,0}\left(g_{y}\right)=e^{g_{y}\Delta t}$
      for one time step at the risk-free rate $g_{y}$.
    \item \hilite{L3b's risk-neutral probability}: $q=(R_{f}-d)/(u-d)$ with
      $R_{f}=\mathcal{D}_{1,0}(g_{y})$.
    \item \hilite{Up and down factors}: set by the implied volatility
      $\sigma$ of the underlying asset and the time step size $\Delta t$.
  \end{itemize}
  \slidenote{Where do $u$ and $d$ come from?}{\href{\repo/advanced/crr_factors/CHEME-5660-L9b-Advanced-CRR-Factors-Fall-2026.ipynb}{\dimmed{advanced/crr\_factors/CHEME-5660-L9b-Advanced-CRR-Factors-Fall-2026.ipynb}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Early-Exercise Premium}
  At expiration, each node's value is the contract's payoff. Working backward,
  the American holder takes the larger of exercising now and holding for the
  future, while the European holder can only hold:
  {\small
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      \hat{V}^{\text{AM}}_{t,k} & = \max\Bigl\{\underbrace{V(K,S_{t,k})}_{\text{exercise now}},~\underbrace{\Dstep\cdot\bigl(q\,\hat{V}^{\text{AM}}_{t+1,k+1} + (1-q)\,\hat{V}^{\text{AM}}_{t+1,k}\bigr)}_{\text{hold for the future}}\Bigr\}\\
      \hat{V}^{\text{EU}}_{t,k} & = \Dstep\cdot\bigl(q\,\hat{V}^{\text{EU}}_{t+1,k+1} + (1-q)\,\hat{V}^{\text{EU}}_{t+1,k}\bigr)
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}}%
  Here $\hat{V}_{t,k}$ is the contract's value at the node with $k$ up moves in
  $t$ steps, and $S_{t,k}=S_{0}u^{k}d^{t-k}$ is the share price there (L3b). The
  premium is the value at the root, $\mathcal{P}=\hat{V}_{0,0}$. The difference
  $\PAM-\PEU\geq 0$ is the value of the right to exercise early, the
  \hilite{early-exercise premium}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Compute the Price of an American Call and Put Option Contract}
  Now that we've explored the decision rule through the Hull example, let's
  apply these concepts to real market data.
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
    \item \hilite{Model versus market}: CRR prices for American calls and puts
      from the course's options archive, compared with market prices across
      strike.
    \item \hilite{Calls}: on a share that pays no dividend, exercising early
      never helps. Exercising early means paying the strike sooner and owning
      the shares if the price then falls below the strike. Holding is always
      worth at least as much, so $\PAM_{c}=\PEU_{c}$.
    \item \hilite{Puts}: exercising early can pay when the put is deep in the
      money. The holder receives the strike now and earns the risk-free rate on
      it,\newline so $\PAM_{p}>\PEU_{p}$ is possible.
  \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Extrinsic versus Intrinsic Value of American Options}
  The premium (price) of an American option can be broken down into two
  components: intrinsic value and extrinsic value.
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item \hilite{Intrinsic value}: the immediate exercise value, $\max(0, S - K)$
      for a call and $\max(0, K - S)$ for a put, where $S$ is the current stock
      price.
    \item \hilite{Extrinsic value}: also known as time value, the additional
      value from the potential for future price movements before expiration.
    \item \hilite{Time decay}: as time moves towards expiration, the extrinsic
      value tends to decrease, because there is less time for the share price
      to move favorably.
  \end{itemize}
  The extrinsic value $E_{V}(t)$ can be expressed mathematically as:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      E_{V}(t) = \text{Option Price} - \text{Intrinsic Value}\quad\blacksquare
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Extrinsic Value}
  In expanded form, the extrinsic value $E_{V}(t)$ is:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      E_{V}(t) &= \begin{cases}
      \mathcal{P}_{c}(K,S(t)) - \max(0, S(t) - K) & \text{(for Call Options)} \\
      \mathcal{P}_{p}(K,S(t)) - \max(0, K - S(t)) & \text{(for Put Options)}
      \end{cases}
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
  When the option is \hilite{out of the money}, it has no intrinsic value, and
  the entire option price is extrinsic value.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \textbf{\ink{Why do people pay money for something that is ``worthless''?}}
  There is still a chance that the share price moves favorably before
  expiration.

\end{frame}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Why does the CRR model use $u=e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ and
  $d=e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$? We match the variance of the lattice's
  one-step log return to the variance of geometric Brownian motion, which sets
  the size of the up and down moves. We then check the match on the lattice
  priced with $q$, and find how small the time step must be to keep $q$ between
  zero and one.

\begin{frame}{Summary}
  Today we priced American options with the CRR binomial model and split option
  prices into intrinsic and extrinsic components.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{Key takeaways}}}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item \hilite{Binomial trees enable American option pricing}: at each node,
      the holder exercises or holds, so backward induction computes the
      premium. The American premium exceeds the European premium only when early
      exercise pays, as for deep in-the-money puts.
    \item \hilite{Intrinsic and extrinsic value tell different stories}:
      intrinsic value is the immediate exercise value, and extrinsic value is
      the potential for favorable price movement before expiration.
    \item \hilite{Extrinsic value depends on multiple factors}: time to
      expiration, volatility, and moneyness. Longer-dated options typically carry
      more extrinsic value.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
