\begin{frame}{Objectives for Today}
  Previously, we priced European options, which can be exercised only at
  expiration. Standard options on stocks and ETFs are American-style and can be
  exercised at any time. What is the right to exercise early worth?

  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{Today's concepts}}}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
    \item \hilite{Distinguish between European and American options}: exercise
      only at expiration, or at any time before expiration.
    \item \hilite{Apply the binomial model to price American options}: build
      CRR trees and compute premiums with the decision rule for early exercise.
      Estimate the same premium by Monte Carlo simulation.
    \item \hilite{Decompose option premiums into components}: intrinsic value
      (immediate exercise value) and extrinsic value (time value).
  \end{itemize}

  \href{https://www.theocc.com/}{\hilite{OCC}} stands between the buyer and the

  Corporation and became OCC in 1975.
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item \hilite{Exercise and assignment}: OCC assigns each early exercise at
      random to a clearing member with a short position in the same contract.
    \item \hilite{Automatic exercise at expiration}: since 1977, an expiring
      equity option in the money by 0.01 USD/share or more is exercised unless
      the holder instructs otherwise.
    \item \hilite{Monte Carlo margin and binomial trees}: since 2006, STANS
      simulates 10,000 two-day scenarios and reprices American options on a
      binomial tree.
  \end{itemize}
  \textbf{\ink{Connection to today's lecture:}} a seller of an American put can be
  assigned once exercising now beats holding, so a European-premium margin
  would leave out the early-exercise premium.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{American Style Options Contracts}
  American-style options contracts allow the holder to exercise the option
  \hilite{at any time} before or on the expiration date. Previously, we wrote
  the premium of an American-style contract as:
  \[
    \PAM=\sup_{\tau\leq T}\E_{\Q}\Bigl(\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{\tau,0}(g_{y})\cdot V(K,S(\tau))\Bigr)\geq\PEU
  \]
  The exercise time $\tau$ is chosen using only the information available at
  that time (a \hilite{stopping time}). The American premium is at least the
  European one because the American holder can always wait until expiration.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  The standard method to price American options is the Cox, Ross, Rubinstein
  (CRR) binomial model.
  \slidenote{Reference:}{Cox, J. C., Ross, S. A., and Rubinstein, M. (1979). Option pricing: A simplified approach. \href{https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0304405X79900151}{Journal of Financial Economics 7(3): 229}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{CRR Binomial Lattice Model}
  At each time step, the share price moves up by a factor $u$ with the
  \hilite{risk-neutral probability} $q$, or down by a factor $d$ with
  probability $(1-q)$. The CRR parameters are no longer data driven: $q$ makes
  the expected return of the underlying asset match the risk-free rate $g_{y}$.
  {\small
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
      \mathcal{D}_{1,0}(g_{y})\cdot S_{0} & = \E_{\Q}\left(S_{1}\right) && \text{grow at the risk-free rate under }\Q\\
      & = q\cdot(u\cdot S_{0}) + (1-q)\cdot(d\cdot S_{0}) && \text{up with probability } q\text{, down with } (1-q)\\
      & = S_{0}\bigl(qu + d - qd\bigr) && \text{factor out } S_{0}\text{ and expand } (1-q)d\\
      & = S_{0}\bigl(d + q(u - d)\bigr) && \text{collect the } q \text{ terms}\\[0.3em]
      \Longrightarrow\quad & \boxed{q = \frac{\mathcal{D}_{1,0}\left(g_{y}\right) - d}{u - d}} && \text{divide by } S_{0}\text{ and solve for } q
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}}%
  Here $\mathcal{D}_{1,0}(g_{y})=e^{g_{y}\Delta t}$ is the risk-free accumulation
  factor for one time step. This is the risk-neutral probability we found by
  ruling out arbitrage when we introduced lattice models, with
  $R_{f}=\mathcal{D}_{1,0}(g_{y})$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{CRR Up and Down Factors}
  Unlike the data-driven binomial model we discussed earlier, the CRR model
  derives $u$ and $d$ based on the volatility $\sigma$ of the underlying asset,
  the time step $\Delta t$, and the symmetric condition $ud = 1$:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      u &= e^{\sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}} \\
      d &= e^{-\sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}}
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
  Here $\sigma$ is the same volatility as in geometric Brownian motion and the
  Black--Scholes--Merton formula (units: $\mathrm{yr}^{-1/2}$).
  \slidenote{Where do $u$ and $d$ come from?}{\href{\repo/advanced/crr_factors/CHEME-5660-L9b-Advanced-CRR-Factors-Fall-2026.ipynb}{\dimmed{advanced/crr\_factors/CHEME-5660-L9b-Advanced-CRR-Factors-Fall-2026.ipynb}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Historical or Implied Volatility?}
  There are two ways to put a number on $\sigma$, and the second also gives a
  quick range for the share price:
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
    \item \hilite{Historical volatility}: previously, we estimated $\sigma$ from
      past share prices as
      $\hat{\sigma}=\sigma_{\mathrm{ann}}=\sqrt{\Delta t}\cdot\sigma_{g}$, where
      $\sigma_{g}$ is the standard deviation of the growth rates and $\Delta t$
      is the time between price observations. This estimate looks backward.
    \item \hilite{Implied volatility}: the
      \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implied_volatility}{implied volatility}
      (IV) is the market's forecast of a likely movement in the share price,
      the volatility ``implied'' by an option's market price through a pricing
      model such as Black--Scholes--Merton or CRR. It is often equal to or
      larger than the historical volatility.
    \item \hilite{How can we use it?} IV represents a one-standard-deviation
      movement in the share price over the next year. Over a horizon of $T$
      years, the size of that move scales with $\sqrt{T}$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Back-of-the-Envelope Range from IV}
  Moving one standard deviation up or down in the solution of geometric
  Brownian motion gives a back-of-the-envelope range for the share price:
  {\small
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
      S(T) & = S_{0}\exp\left[\mu_{g}T + \sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z\right] && \text{solution of geometric Brownian motion}\\
      S_{\pm} & = S_{0}\exp\left[\mu_{g}T \pm \text{IV}\sqrt{T}\right] && \text{set } \sigma=\text{IV} \text{ and } Z=\pm 1\\
      & \approx S_{0}\exp\left[\pm\,\text{IV}\sqrt{T}\right] && \mu_{g}T \text{ is small next to } \text{IV}\sqrt{T} \text{ for short } T\\
      & \approx S_{0}\left(1 \pm \text{IV}\sqrt{T}\right) && e^{x}\approx 1+x \text{ for small } x
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}}%
  Here $\mu_{g}$ is the mean growth rate, $Z$ is a standard normal random
  variable, and $S_{\pm}$ is the share price one standard deviation above or
  below $S_{0}$. For example, a share at $S_{0}$ = 100 USD/share with IV = 40\%
  and 30 days to expiration ($T$ = 30/365 years) has $\text{IV}\sqrt{T}$ =
  0.115, so the range is about 88.5 to 111.5 USD/share. Under the model, the
  share price ends inside this range about 68\% of the time.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Early-Exercise Premium}
  At expiration, each node's value is the contract's payoff. Working backward,
  the American holder takes the larger of exercising now and holding for the
  future, while the European holder can only hold:
  {\small
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      \hat{V}^{\text{AM}}_{t,k} & = \max\Bigl\{\underbrace{V(K,S_{t,k})}_{\text{exercise now}},~\underbrace{\Dstep\cdot\bigl(q\,\hat{V}^{\text{AM}}_{t+1,k+1} + (1-q)\,\hat{V}^{\text{AM}}_{t+1,k}\bigr)}_{\text{hold for the future}}\Bigr\}\\
      \hat{V}^{\text{EU}}_{t,k} & = \Dstep\cdot\bigl(q\,\hat{V}^{\text{EU}}_{t+1,k+1} + (1-q)\,\hat{V}^{\text{EU}}_{t+1,k}\bigr)
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}}%
  Here $\hat{V}_{t,k}$ is the contract's value at the node with $k$ up moves in
  $t$ steps, $S_{t,k}=S_{0}u^{k}d^{t-k}$ is the share price there, and
  $\Dstep=e^{-g_{y}\Delta t}$ discounts one time step. The hold term is the
  European premium formula applied over one step. The premium is
  $\mathcal{P}=\hat{V}_{0,0}$, and $\PAM-\PEU\geq 0$ is the
  \hilite{early-exercise premium}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pseudocode: Pricing on the CRR Lattice}
  \hilite{Initialize}: given $S_{0}$, $K$, the payoff function $V(K,S)$ of the
  call or put, $T$, $\sigma$, $g_{y}$, and the number of time steps $h$. Compute
  $\Delta t=T/h$, $u$ and $d$, $q$, and $\Dstep=e^{-g_{y}\Delta t}$.
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item Build the share price lattice: $S_{t,k}\gets S_{0}u^{k}d^{t-k}$ for
      each level $t=0,1,\dots,h$ and node $k=0,1,\dots,t$.
    \item At expiration, set each node value to the payoff:
      $\hat{V}_{h,k}\gets V(K,S_{h,k})$ for $k=0,1,\dots,h$.
    \item For each level $t=h-1,h-2,\dots,0$ \textbf{do}:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item For each node $k=0,1,\dots,t$ \textbf{do}:
          \begin{itemize}
            \item[a.] Compute the value of holding for the future:
              $H_{t,k}\gets\Dstep\cdot\bigl(q\,\hat{V}_{t+1,k+1}+(1-q)\,\hat{V}_{t+1,k}\bigr)$.
            \item[b.] If the contract is American, \textbf{then}:
              $\hat{V}_{t,k}\gets\max\bigl\{V(K,S_{t,k}),~H_{t,k}\bigr\}$.
              Otherwise, $\hat{V}_{t,k}\gets H_{t,k}$.
          \end{itemize}
      \end{itemize}
    \item The premium is the value at the root: $\mathcal{P}\gets\hat{V}_{0,0}$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

  apply these concepts to real market data: CRR prices for American calls and
  puts from the course's options archive, compared with market prices across

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \textbf{\ink{When does early exercise pay?}} For a call on a share that pays no
  dividend, exercising early never helps. Exercising early means paying the
  strike sooner and owning the shares if the price then falls below the
  strike, so $\PAM_{c}=\PEU_{c}$.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  For a put, exercising early can pay when the put is deep in the money. The
  holder receives the strike now and earns the risk-free rate on it, so
  $\PAM_{p}>\PEU_{p}$ is possible.

\begin{frame}{Monte Carlo Pricing of American Options}
  Previously, we priced European contracts by simulating share prices under
  $\Q$ and averaging the discounted payoffs. Longstaff and Schwartz extended
  this approach to American contracts.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  On every path, the decision rule needs the expected value of holding.
  Longstaff and Schwartz estimate it by regression, working backward from
  expiration:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \hilite{Initialize}: given $S_{0}$, $K$, the payoff function $V(K,S)$ of the
  call or put, $T$, $\sigma$, $g_{y}$, the number of exercise dates $N$ after
  today, and the number of paths $M$. Compute $\Delta t=T/N$ and
  $\Dstep=e^{-g_{y}\Delta t}$.
  \slidenote{Reference:}{Longstaff, F. A., and Schwartz, E. S. (2001). Valuing American options by simulation: a simple least-squares approach. \href{https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/14.1.113}{Review of Financial Studies 14(1): 113–147}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pseudocode: Longstaff–Schwartz}
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item \hilite{Simulate}: generate $M$ share price paths under $\Q$ on the
      exercise dates $t=0,1,\dots,N$, where $S_{i}(t)$ is the share price on
      path $i$ at date $t$.
    \item At expiration, set each path's cash flow to its payoff:
      $Y_{i}\gets V(K,S_{i}(N))$ for $i=1,2,\dots,M$.
    \item For each date $t=N-1,N-2,\dots,1$ \textbf{do}:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item[a.] Discount each cash flow back one date: $Y_{i}\gets\Dstep\cdot Y_{i}$.
        \item[b.] \hilite{Regress}: on the in-the-money paths, fit
          $\hat{C}(S)=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}S+\beta_{2}S^{2}$ to the pairs
          $\bigl(S_{i}(t),Y_{i}\bigr)$ by least squares.
        \item[c.] \hilite{Decide}: for each in-the-money path, if
          $V(K,S_{i}(t))>\hat{C}(S_{i}(t))$, \textbf{then}: exercise, so
          $Y_{i}\gets V(K,S_{i}(t))$.
      \end{itemize}
    \item Discount the cash flows to today and average them:
      $\bar{Y}\gets\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{1,0}(g_{y})\cdot\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}Y_{i}$.
    \item The premium is $\PAM\gets\max\bigl\{V(K,S_{0}),~\bar{Y}\bigr\}$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Extrinsic versus Intrinsic Value of American Options}
  The premium (price) of an American option can be broken down into two
  components: intrinsic value and extrinsic value.
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item \hilite{Intrinsic value}: the immediate exercise value,
      $\max(0, S(t) - K)$ for a call and $\max(0, K - S(t))$ for a put, where
      $S(t)$ is the current share price.
    \item \hilite{Extrinsic value}: also known as time value, the additional
      value from the potential for future price movements before expiration.
  \end{itemize}
  The extrinsic value $E_{V}(t)$ can be expressed mathematically as:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      E_{V}(t) = \text{Option Price} - \text{Intrinsic Value}\quad\blacksquare
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Extrinsic Value}
  In expanded form, the extrinsic value $E_{V}(t)$ is:
  \begin{equation*}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
      E_{V}(t) &= \begin{cases}
      \mathcal{P}_{c}(K,S(t)) - \max(0, S(t) - K) & \text{(for Call Options)} \\
      \mathcal{P}_{p}(K,S(t)) - \max(0, K - S(t)) & \text{(for Put Options)}
      \end{cases}
    \end{aligned}}
  \end{equation*}
  When the option is \hilite{out of the money}, it has no intrinsic value, and
  the entire option price is extrinsic value.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \textbf{\ink{Why do people pay money for something that is ``worthless''?}}
  There is still a chance that the share price moves favorably before
  expiration.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  As time moves towards expiration, the extrinsic value tends to decrease
  (\hilite{time decay}), because there is less time for the share price to
  move favorably.

\begin{frame}{Summary}
  Today we priced American options with the CRR binomial model and Monte Carlo
  simulation, and split option prices into intrinsic and extrinsic components.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{Key takeaways}}}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item \hilite{Binomial trees and simulation price American options}: at
      each node, the holder exercises or holds, so backward induction computes
      the premium. The American premium exceeds the European premium only when
      exercising early can pay at some node, as it can for puts.
      Longstaff--Schwartz simulation estimates the same premium by regression.
    \item \hilite{Intrinsic and extrinsic value tell different stories}:
      intrinsic value is the immediate exercise value, and extrinsic value is
      the potential for favorable price movement before expiration.
    \item \hilite{Extrinsic value depends on multiple factors}: time to
      expiration, volatility, and moneyness. Longer-dated options typically carry
      more extrinsic value.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
