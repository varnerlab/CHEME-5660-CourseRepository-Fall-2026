How We Will Work

Throughout the course, we will repeat the same cycle: ask a question, use a model
to explore it, and test the answer against evidence.

     • Each idea answers a question. New concepts will be introduced through the
       ﬁnancial problems they help us solve.
     • Code is our laboratory. Most of the code will be provided; you will change
       assumptions, inspect the results, and explain what the model is doing.
     • Evidence decides. A model can be mathematically correct and still fail. You will
       compare its claims with data and revise it when the evidence disagrees.

You will learn to explain what a model predicts, test that prediction against data, and
recognize when the model fails.

Most of You Will Eventually Own a Portfolio

Many of you may not have a retirement account today. But roughly 61% of U.S.
adults have a tax-preferred account, such as a 401(k) or IRA. That makes portfolio
ownership widespread, not something limited to wealthy people. (2025)
     • A retirement account holds a portfolio: a mix of investments.
     • Many account holders keep the default mix selected for them.
Keeping the default mix is still an investment decision. When that decision becomes
yours, it will take place inside ﬁnancial markets far larger than the industries many of
you will enter.

The options row is not like the other two. The USD 37B is the price paid for the
options. That price, called the premium, is not the total value of the shares those
options control.
Option contracts generally use a multiplier of 100. Across 60.4 million contracts a

The Three Kinds of Assets
The investment choices in this course fall into three broad groups. We name them
today and examine each one later, when the course needs it.

    Kind                      What you own                               Where the course takes it apart
    Debt                      a promise of payments on stated dates      Unit 1, weeks 2 and 3
    Equity                    ownership of what remains after debts      Unit 2, weeks 3 to 7
    Derivative                a contract tied to another asset’s value   Units 3 and 4, weeks 8 onward

These categories differ in what they promise and where uncertainty enters. Debt
speciﬁes payments, but the borrower can fail to pay. Equity promises no ﬁxed
payments and is worth whatever remains after the company pays its debts. A
derivative’s value depends on another asset and on the terms of the contract.

Today: we use only the ﬁrst kind, and we do not use its name until later.

The Validation Ladder
Four ways to test an idea. Each stage uses evidence the one below did not see, and
each can reveal a failure that the earlier stages cannot.

                                                                             assumptions   past data   current data   money at risk

                                                            Real money
                                         tests: “I would actually do this”
                                                          Forward test
                              tests: “the market cooperates in real time”
                                                        Backtest
           tests: “the math works on data that already happened”
                                                                 Theory
                                       tests: “my reasoning is coherent”



A backtest runs on past data. A forward test runs on actual current data with no
money at stake, using current market data from a service such as Alpaca.
Skipping a stage means discovering a failure later, perhaps with real money at stake.

Three Questions We Cannot Answer Yet

Most hands went to C or D, which is reasonable given the information on the
previous slide. That slide left out three things.
     • Why not C? It pays the most of the three government rows, but you may need
       the money before year thirty. If you sell early, you must accept the market price
       at that time, which depends on interest rates. Week 2.
     • Why not D? It pays more than any other row. Its rate is 0.71 percentage point
       above the ten-year government rate (5.42% − 4.71%). That difference reﬂects
       default risk and lower liquidity. Units 2 and 3.
     • Why not E? It pays the least of any row, but that comparison is not valid: its rate
       is quoted in different units. Later in the course.
Only A, B, and C state ﬁxed nominal payments.

Why You Cannot Rank the Five Yet

Every promise on that list comes from the United States government or from large
companies with long records of paying what they owe. No vote in this room was
careless.

You cannot rank those ﬁve yet, and the reason is precise:

           You have no rule for comparing dollars that arrive on different dates.

$264 in thirty years and $10,028 in four weeks cannot be compared until we state
what a dollar on one date is worth on another. We build that rule on Thursday; it is
the ﬁrst valuation tool in the course.

Summary
This lecture placed the course in context, showed why portfolio decisions will matter
to many of you, and read ﬁve ﬁnancial promises as lists of payments and dates.
Key takeaways
     • Retirement accounts require portfolio decisions. Someone must decide
       how the money is invested, even if the default mix remains unchanged.
     • Each promise is a payment schedule. A bill, a note, a bond, a corporate
       bond, and an inﬂation-indexed security are labels for lists of payments and
       dates, though not every payment is known in advance.
     • Testing happens in stages. Theory, backtest, forward test, and real money
       use different evidence, and each can reveal a different kind of failure.
On Thursday we build the rule that lets us compare those ﬁve payment schedules.
